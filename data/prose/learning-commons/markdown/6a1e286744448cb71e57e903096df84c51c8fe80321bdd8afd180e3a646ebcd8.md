Every year around this time, I feel the shift\. Job postings start popping up more frequently, LinkedIn gets busier, and conversations slowly turn into *“Have you started applying yet?”* For many of us, summer internships feel like the next big milestone\. It’s a way to apply what we’ve learned in class, gain experience, and figure out what we might want to do after graduation\. 

I’ve been there too\. And while the process can feel overwhelming, I’ve learned that breaking it down into intentional, manageable steps makes it far more doable and *far* less stressful\.

## Start With a Thoughtful List \(Not a Panic Search\) 

One of the first things I’ve learned is that jumping straight into applications without clarity can make the process exhausting\. Instead of applying to everything at once, I try to start by asking myself a few simple questions: *What am I interested in? What skills do I want to build? What kind of environment do I want to work in?* 

From there, I make a list of roles and organizations that actually align with those goals\. It doesn’t have to be perfect or final, it’s more of a working list that gives me direction\. Having that list helps me stay focused and prevents the feeling of endlessly scrolling through job boards without a plan\. 

## Building Your Network \(Even When It Feels Intimidating\) 

Networking is one of those things that everyone says is important, but few people really explain how to get started, *especially* as a student\. I used to hate having coffee chats, they’re so awkward, as you don’t know this person and it almost always feels like an interview\. What’s helped me is reframing networking as thinking of networking as *learning*, not asking for favours\. 

Reaching out to people on LinkedIn, attending campus events, or asking a mutual connection for an introduction can feel uncomfortable at first, but those conversations often provide valuable insight into roles, industries, and workplace culture\. More importantly, they remind me that most professionals were once students too, figuring things out as they went\. 

I’ve learned that asking thoughtful questions and showing genuine interest goes a long way, even if it doesn’t lead directly to an interview\. 

## Methodically Working on Applications 

Instead of rushing through resumes and cover letters, I’ve found it far more effective to take a methodical approach\. Tailoring application materials to specific roles takes more time, but it also helps me articulate my experiences and skills more effectively\. 

Something I always try to do now is focus on *impact,* not just what I did, but what came out of it\. And before submitting anything, I ask someone I trust to proofread\. A second set of eyes catches things I’d otherwise miss\. 

There are also student services at UBC that offer resume and cover letter feedback, which can be incredibly helpful when you’re unsure if you’re on the right track\. 

## Applying Early and Trusting the Process 

One of the biggest lessons I’ve learned \(often the hard way\) is not to leave applications until the last minute\. Submitting earlier gives me time to review everything properly and reduces unnecessary stress\. It also matters because many organizations review applications on a rolling basis, meaning interviews can start well before the deadline\.

Once applications are submitted, the waiting period can be tough\. I try to remind myself that not hearing back right away is normal\. Preparing for interviews, practicing with friends, and focusing on other commitments help keep me grounded during that time\. 

And if an application doesn’t result in an offer, I try not to see it as a failure — but as practice\. Every application, interview, and conversation builds skills that carry forward into future opportunities\. 

The goal of landing a summer internship is important, but I’ve realized that how I approach the process matters just as much as the outcome\. Breaking things down, being intentional, and giving myself grace along the way has made job searching feel less like a race and more like a learning experience\. 

If you’re in the middle of this process right now, know that you’re not alone and that figuring it out as you go is part of it\. Progress doesn’t always look like an offer letter\. Sometimes, it looks like clarity, confidence, and growth\.
