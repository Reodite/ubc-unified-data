January always arrives with a lot of expectations\. New calendars, new deadlines, new energy we’re all supposed to magically have after the break; even though it’s cold, dark, and my brain is still very much in winter mode\. 

As a student, January doesn’t always feel like a fresh start\. It feels like pressure\. 

Midterms start creeping up almost immediately\. Papers pile on\. Group projects reappear\. And somehow, in between all of that, I’m expected to keep my life together? Show up to work, keep up with extracurriculars, eat real meals, and maintain a social life\. Most days, I manage\. From the outside, it probably looks like I’m doing fine\. 

And that’s kind of the problem\. 

## The Quiet Way Mental Health Shows Up

Physical health tends to be loud\. When I’m sick, my body makes it obvious — I feel run down, exhausted, and eventually forced to slow down\. 

Mental health is quieter\. It doesn’t always announce itself the way physical illness does\. 

What I’ve noticed, especially in university, is how easy it is to keep functioning even when something feels off\. Classes still get attended, assignments still get submitted, and messages still get answered with a casual “I’m busy\.” 

I think of this as ‘*mental stealth’–st*ruggling internally while still appearing put\-together and capable\. It’s not always dramatic, and it’s not always immediately noticeable — *even* to ourselves\. 

And the more I’ve learned about mental health, the more I’ve realized how common this experience is\. Nearly **half of people who struggle with depression or anxiety have never sought professional help**, meaning many people carry these experiences quietly\. In British Columbia, **1 in 5 people will experience a mental health issue at some point in their lives**, which puts this quiet kind of struggle into perspective\. 

## When Being Overwhelmed Feels Normal

One of the most challenging aspects of student life is how normalized stress has become\. Being exhausted is expected\. Being overwhelmed is relatable\. Running on caffeine and deadlines feels like part of the culture\. 

I’ve caught myself minimizing my own feelings because everyone else seems just as busy, just as tired\. I tell myself this is just what university is like\. I’ll deal with it later: after this midterm, after this paper, after reading break\. Later keeps moving\.

Mental stealth thrives in that mindset\. When struggle is common, it becomes harder to recognize when it’s actually taking a toll\. You don’t need to be at a breaking point to deserve support\. 

## Acknowledging Mental Health and Finding Support

One thing I’ve come to realize is that needing support doesn’t mean something has gone wrong, it just means something needs attention\. Mental health struggles don’t always show up as crises, and they don’t have to reach a breaking point to be valid\. Sometimes it’s simply noticing that things feel heavier than usual, or that getting through the day takes more effort than it used to\. 

Because these experiences are so common, support exists for such moments too\. Organizations like the **Canadian Mental Health Association** share accessible information, and at UBC there are resources such as **Counselling Services**, **Student Health Services**\. Knowing these options exist has been a reminder that struggling silently isn’t a personal failure, and that support can be part of everyday care, not just emergencies\. 

Mental stealth might make it easier to keep going, but it doesn’t have to mean doing everything alone\. Taking mental health seriously, even in subtle, quiet ways, is still an important step toward feeling better\.
