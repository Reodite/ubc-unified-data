Whenever I’m feeling stressed or overwhelmed, my instinct is to retreat home and recharge\. But staying indoors too long can backfire, especially when I end up scrolling on my phone instead of actually unwinding\.

[Image](https://lc2023.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/06/unnamed-1.jpg)

Dr\. Sally Aitken’s “Into the Woods” course \(designed for forest science newbies like me\) introduced me to the Japanese practice of *shinrin\-yoku*, or forest bathing, which entails visiting a forest and immersing yourself in the experience\. [Cleveland Clinic \(2023\)](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/why-forest-therapy-can-be-good-for-your-body-and-mind) notes that, although research on forest bathing is still relatively new, studies suggest that it can benefit our overall wellbeing, both physically and mentally\. What drew my attention most is its potential to reduce stress, anxiety, and depression

Given how stressed I’ve been while juggling school, work, and life, I decided to give forest bathing a try for the month of October\. *After all*, I thought, *my commute takes me by Pacific Spirit Regional Park—why not go someday after class?* I’ve also been wanting to connect with nature, and this seemed like a great alternative to hiking or camping, which I’ve never been athletic or outdoorsy enough to enjoy\.

On my first outing, my sister, dog, and I followed a quiet trail when a loud hammering noise caught our attention\. Trusting my amateur birding instincts, I peeked around a tree and came face\-to\-face with a pileated woodpecker\! I was thrilled, the woodpecker was unfazed, and my sister took a video \(screenshot included\)\.

Since then, I’ve been to Pacific Spirit Park once more\. Both experiences were a breath of fresh air, but I felt most at peace when I went by myself, likely because I was better able to focus on my surroundings\. While I’m unable to \(dis\)prove any claims about the effects of forest bathing based on this very brief, personal experiment, I *have* noticed that mindful walks in the woods help steady my breathing, relax my facial muscles, and clear my head\.

But I’ll be honest, it’s hard to muster the energy or find time to visit the forest frequently\. While “park bathing” may not be as effective as forest bathing, something is better than nothing, so I’ve taken advantage of more accessible ways to explore the outdoors\. 

The adjacent image shows the golden canopy of a horsechestnut tree in my neighbourhood\. Moments before a rogue chestnut nearly concussed me, I was appreciating how the leaves rustled gently in the breeze and how the sunlight filtered through them… It feels cheesy writing this, but I swear, the clichés are clichés for a reason\! Paying attention to things like this helps me ground myself in the present and take a break from deadlines and everyday worries\.

[Image](https://lc2023.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/06/unnamed-2-scaled-1.jpg)

If you want to explore nature while also practicing a little mindfulness, consider giving forest \(or perhaps park\) bathing a try\. There are many green spaces on or near campus that are worth exploring\. I particularly enjoy visiting Nitobe Memorial Garden, Pacific Spirit Park, and Jericho Beach Park\. Based on my experience, even a brief time outdoors can help soothe stress and provide perspective\. Who would’ve thought touching grass is actually good for us?

Let us know if you try forest bathing and how it makes you feel\! If you’d like a guide to some common plants and critters to look for on your walks, check out Jaya’s blogpost [here](https://learningcommons.ubc.ca/common-plants-and-animals-found-on-campus/)\.
