Scrolling through TikTok a few months ago, I kept seeing people “cash stuffing” portions of their paychecks and spare bills into cute envelopes\. At first, I thought, *this is way too extra for me*\. But then I remembered how easily I swipe my card, so I figured why not give it a shot? Worst case, I’d end up with a stack of envelopes and a funny story\. Best case, I’d finally get a handle on my random spending\. 

## What is the Envelope System?

The envelope system is pretty simple: you divide up your money \(usually from each payday or month\) into different categories: groceries, dining out, gas, entertainment, etc\. Each category gets its own envelope\. Once the cash in an envelope is gone, that’s it for spending in that category until the next refill\. The idea is: if the money’s not available physically, you’re less likely to overspend\. 

You can use physical envelopes \(actual cash\), or a digital version \(apps or spreadsheets that mimic the envelopes\)\. [Capital One](https://www.capitalone.com/learn-grow/money-management/envelope-budget-system/) describes both approaches and emphasizes that this system is especially useful for *variable expenses*, the ones that tend to fluctuate every month\.

## How I Set It Up?

### Here’s what I did:

1.  **Chose my categories**: I looked through my bank statements from the past few months to figure out where my money was going\. I picked 5\-6 variable spending categories: groceries, eating out, entertainment, personal care, and “miscellaneous”\. 
2.  **Assigned amounts:** Based on historical spending, I decided how much cash I’d allot to each envelope per month \(I left a little wiggle room, just in case\)\.
3.  **Got the cash:** On payday, I withdrew the total envelope money I needed\. Then I divided it up into the designated envelopes\. 
4.  **Tracked as I spent:** I marked down how much I took from each envelope, and kept a running total so I could see what I had left\. I kept the amounts written on the envelopes themselves so I could see my balance right away \(but a small tracker sheet or phone note works too\)\. 
5.  **No refill until reset:** Once an envelope was empty, I tried to avoid dipping into others \(though I did once or twice\)\.  
    

## What was good?

-   **More Awareness of Spending:** Seeing cash physically leave an envelope made me more conscious of spending\. I hesitated more often before buying non\-essentials\. There was something grounding about it; I’d pause and ask myself, “Do I really want this, or is it just a momentary craving?” Sometimes I’d even put the item back and feel oddly proud of that small act of restraint\. Writing down the remaining amount on the envelope after each purchase also made me realize how quickly small expenses add up, it turns numbers on a screen into something tangible\.
-   **Motivation to Save Leftovers:** If I had cash left over, I felt proud keeping it, or using it for something I’ve been wanting, like a nice dinner, or putting it toward savings\.

## Digital vs Physical : What I Learned

I tried both ways:

-   Physical cash envelopes feel more tangible\. The act of handing over cash hits differently than swiping a card\. 
-   But digital envelopes \(via a spreadsheet\) are more convenient for me, especially for online purchases, bills, or simply when I don’t have cash on me\. 

## Verdict : Will I Keep Doing It?

Yes, but with a few tweaks\. I think a hybrid method may be the sweet spot for me: physical for groceries, maybe digital for categories I usually buy things online for or that require card payments\. 

The envelope system taught me a lot about where my money was going and helped me pause before impulse buys\. Even though “ideal” didn’t always happen, I found it helpful enough that I’m sticking with a version that works for me\. 

## Tips If You Want to Try It

-   Start small: pick a couple of categories first\. Don’t try to envelope everything\.
-   Use your past spending data to set amounts \(look at the last 2–3 months\)\.
-   Track rigorously \(or try to\): writing down or logging every expense helps more than you think\.
-   Allow margin for surprises: occasional buffer money helps reduce stress\.
-   Adjust as you go: if something isn’t working, change it\. Budgets aren’t one\-size\-fits\-all\.
