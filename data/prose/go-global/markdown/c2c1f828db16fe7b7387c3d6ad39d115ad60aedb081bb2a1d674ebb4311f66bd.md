UBC undergrad Elsa Pyper shares how a conservation\-focused Global Seminar in South Africa shaped her understanding of community, wildlife, and responsibility in ecosystem protection\.

April 27, 2026

*Are you a UBC undergrad thinking about going on a student exchange? It can be a daunting decision\. You might wonder, what's it like spending a year or a semester in another country? How can you prepare? And how do you handle it when things go sideways? We talked with a group of* [*UBC Global Ambassadors*](https://global.ubc.ca/go-global/new-student-experiences/global-ambassadors-and-global-cultural-ambassadors) *who studied abroad with a* [*Go Global program*](https://global.ubc.ca/go-global)*\. They spoke with us candidly, sharing their personal impressions, fears and misgivings, best memories, top tips and advice\. \(This story is part of a series of Global Exchange Insider interviews\.\)* 

*Elsa Pyper is a 3rd year UBC undergraduate in the Forest Sciences program in the Faculty of Forestry\. She was born and raised in Langley, BC\.*

[Image](https://goglobal.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/solo%20photo%20-%20Elsa%20Pyper.jpg)

#### Before you go   

**Why did you pick the** [**South Africa Global Seminar: Communities, Wildlife, and Conservation in Post\-Apartheid South Africa**](https://goglobal.ubc.ca/go-global/programs-ubc-students/global-seminars/programs/south-africa-communities-wildlife-and "South Africa: Communities, Wildlife, and Conservation in Post-Apartheid South Africa - CONS 454 (6 credits)")**? How did you hear about it?**  
I actually made this decision based on pure coincidence\! I was enrolled in CONS 101, and my professor \- the wonderful Dr\. Bunn – mentioned it in class one day and encouraged students to sign up\. Being the head of this program’s teaching team \(and a born\-and\-raised South African himself\), he spoke with such excitement about the experience that it immediately piqued my interest\. The program’s uniqueness and cultural value felt incomparable to any other Go Global seminar I had heard about\. I, thinking such a program would be impossible to get into, signed up on a whim\. I am so very grateful I did, as it would turn out to be one of the most meaningful experiences of my life\.

**What was the application process like? Any tips?**   
Thankfully, the Go Global application portals are generally simple and straightforward\. For our seminar, we answered several online essay questions with an additional in\-person interview\. The biggest piece of advice I can give anyone in the application process is to NETWORK WITH YOUR PROGRAM LEAD\. Whether this be your professor \(as was in my case\) or a faculty member you’ve never met, it is crucial that you interact with them in some way\. Showing the initiative to ask questions and share information about yourself makes you pop out amongst other applicants\. It also shows that you are a proactive and passionate person, which are necessary qualities to have when being considered for a Go Global program\.

**How did you prepare? Anything to be aware of? \(travel, cultural differences, classes, etc\.\)**   
Our lovely program staff equipped us with many resources to help prepare for our trip\. We were given a packing list, had guides for acquiring things like vaccinations and travel insurance, and were able to attend pre\-departure meetings that helped with unanswered questions\. As far as personal preparation goes, I encourage students to make copies of all necessary documents to keep in a folder with you during your trip\. This includes photocopies of passports, insurance, travel itineraries and government IDs\. Best case scenario, you will not have to use them; however, if your main documents ever get misplaced or stolen, these copies will make sure you can still travel safely in a foreign country\.

Another prevalent concern among many students is differences of culture; the students in my program were especially focused on how to show respect for the culture which we were to enter\. Our program’s faculty shed light on what was appropriate to wear, how to use the local language and how to generally assimilate; however, one of the most important takeaways they emphasized was to simply try your best\. Although us students are quick to worry about how we will be perceived in a foreign place, it became very clear to me that South African culture is warm and welcoming, and any cultural blunders I made would be met with appreciation to the fact that I was trying my best\. In a new place, you’re bound to make mistakes – don’t be too hard on yourself\!

**What worries did you have beforehand? How did you overcome the anxiety?**  
Having never travelled without my family before, to a country I’ve never been to before, I was understandably nervous\. A main worry that I had was safety; South Africa has a generally low safety rating due to violent crime\. This factor almost made me reconsider participation in the program altogether\. However, when I voiced my concerns to the program staff, all my qualms were resolved\. It was explained to me that we would be nowhere close to the crime hotspots, and that the program is designed to keep the student’s absolute safety top of mind\. And just like they said, I never at any point in the entire trip felt even the least bit unsafe \- unless you are counting the freaky\-looking baboons\. Even then, the baby ones are still pretty cute \(well, kind of\)\. So please, remember to ask questions to the program staff\! They are there to help and guide you, so just be honest with what you are feeling\.

#### During the experience

**How was the overall experience? What did you learn?**   
This experience was definitely life changing\. We weren’t just learning things, we were living them\! For example, being taught how to remove snares from the women of the Black Mamba Anti\-Poaching Unit was simply an experience you can’t get anywhere else in the world\. While I learned a great deal about conservation strategies, I also learned how these strategies directly affect the surrounding communities, and the importance of taking actions to benefit both the local people and the wildlife\.

**If you could change something or do it differently, what would you change or do?**   
Something I will say to you now, and that I wish I had realized before my trip, is to plan your trip with both fiscal responsibility and comfort in mind\. As a student booking her own plane tickets for the first time, I prioritized savings over comfort and ended up with a seat right beside the bathroom for 11 hours\. So, unless you like your naps with a side of stank, please consider your own comfort for long journeys\. If I could do it all over again, I would also start preparing for the trip much more in advance; vaccinations, gathering insurance documents and buying equipment can take time\. As the saying goes, it’s much better to have it and not need it, than to need it and not have it\. Being a student means your schedule is pretty packed; give yourself ample time to prepare and save your future self from stress\!

**How were the academics?**   
Top tier\. Not only were we led by professional game trackers and guides throughout the trip, but we also received lectures from some of the most established ecologists and researchers in the world\. Members of NASA, South African National Parks and Transfrontier Africa taught us invaluable lessons about the scientific strategies and processes in which we protect the world’s ecosystems\.

In terms of schoolwork, we completed several individual assignments, one of which included writing journal entries throughout the trip\. Definitely do this even if it’s not required for your program, as it’s a great way to make a personal time capsule of your trip\. There were also multiple group projects that were especially interesting, as we were able to adopt many new hands\-on skills such as setting up camera traps, collecting data on vegetation and my personal favourite, wielding the machete\.

**How was the social aspect?**  
The friendships that I made in South Africa were some of my most valuable takeaways from this experience\. Looking back, it was the people that I learned with, worked alongside, ate with and played games with that truly made the trip as remarkable as it was\. There’s just something about eating a bowl of caterpillars together that bonds you \(don’t worry, they are yummy\)\. Given that we were in the wilderness most of the time, us students didn’t go on many non\-academic excursions; however, we still spent many nights around the fire talking, having dance parties and journaling together\. One of my favourite memories of the trip was simply everyone \(including the instructors\) playing a game of two truths and a lie while grilling South African sausages on the fire\.  
 

A piece of advice: bring games\! It’s a great way to break the ice at the beginning of your program\.

#### After the experience

**Would you recommend this experience to others? What can they look forward to?**   
I have already recommended this experience to DOZENS of students, and will continue to hype it up until I graduate \(and probably even after that\)\! I would say that the biggest thing to look forward to is access; the knowledge, scenery and experiences on this trip exist far beyond what is normally accessible to people\. We were doing things that very few people ever get a chance to do; walking in the savannah of Kruger National Park when tourists can’t even exit their cars and meeting the royal family of the HaMakuya area are just a few of the many examples from this trip\. Dear reader, if you retain anything from this page, let it be this: the experiences on this program are simply not available anywhere else\. Spending time in authentic local villages, receiving lectures from renowned NASA and Kruger National Park scientists, being in the bush amongst the most majestic animals you’ve ever seen – it’s truly a once in a lifetime experience to receive all these privileges wrapped up into one program\.

**What was the impact on your academic and personal development?**    
I came to be much more aware of my role as a student, in the future of ecosystem conservation\. Rather than viewing conservation as something expert or organization\-led, we must recognize our own responsibility, as we begin our professional lives, to actively contribute to change\. This program really let me think critically about how my own education, goals and decisions can influence the future of conservation\.
