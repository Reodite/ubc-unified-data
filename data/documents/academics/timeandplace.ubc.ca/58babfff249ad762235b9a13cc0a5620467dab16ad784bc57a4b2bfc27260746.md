Before you start exploring the timeline, it must be made clear that the timeline is not a static and exhaustive list of historical events in Canada or of Aboriginal peoples\. It is a living document work in progress\. While it was developed with an aim to serve for a broad audience, Hanae’s social position and her learning trajectory set the timeline’s limitations and potentials as a teaching/learning material\. Hanae is a Ph\.D\. student in the Department of Educational Studies at UBC\. She was born and raised in Japan and came to UBC in 2007 after spending seven years in the US\. As may be the case for many people who are new to Canada, she came to Canada with very little knowledge about Canadian history and politics\. Since her arrival in Canada, she picked up information about Canada’s history with Aboriginal peoples from time to time through news reports, class discussions, on\-campus events addressing diversity issues, and so on\. However, most of the information pieces remained undigested, disconnected, and abstract in her understanding\.

An inspiration for this timeline project occurred to her when she attended the symposium, [Addressing injustice: UBC’s Response to the internment of Japanese Canadians students](http://japanese-canadian-student-tribute.ubc.ca/news/#symposium), in March 2012\. At the symposium, for the first time, she learned about 76 Japanese Canadian students who were forced to leave UBC for internment camps during the Second World War\. What particularly interested her was UBC’s action in the given historical context and its significant impact on students’ lives\. The symposium included talks by Musqueam Elder Larry Grant on the historical parallels and differences between Aboriginal peoples and Japanese Canadians and by Dr\. Henry Yu on why history still matters to us today\. Listening to their talks, she wondered, “If Japanese Canadian students were expelled from UBC for internment camps, what was the history like for Aboriginal students at UBC? How does the history inform Aboriginal students’ experiences at UBC today?” This timeline project took off with these simple questions\.

Until starting to work on the timeline project, she had not thought of herself as part of Canadian history\. However, as working on the project, she came to realize how much her experience at UBC is entrenched in the colonial power dynamics developed through Canadian history\. Her social position as a globally mobile student from a so\-called developed country has granted her privileged access to various opportunities\. At the same time, being a non\-native English speaker in a colored female body with limited cultural and social capital in Canadian academia often places her in a marginal position\. Her learning process in the timeline project has led her to realize that she is not outside but part of Canadian history\. She is affected by, as well as benefitted from, the system developed through the history no matter how temporary her life in Canada may be, for she will carry on her sense of self shaped by the experiences of privilege, marginalization, and many others that she has had in Canada\.
