My path to doctoral research has not been a straight line\. I came to the PhD after many years of teaching, senior leadership, board service, and applied business work\. I have spent much of my career working on issues connected to people, learning, community well\-being, education, women, families, and responsible business\.

I am an award\-winning instructor at the UBC Sauder School of Business and also teach in UBC Forestry\. I like working across disciplines and with people who care about research that can be used outside the university\. My doctoral work continues that same practical interest\. I am especially interested in how tourism, business, and education can support better decisions, better relationships, and more respectful engagement between visitors, communities, and place\.

I live in the Southern Gulf Islands with my family and dog, Archie, where I spend time on regenerative gardening and learning about Indigenous plants\.

My doctoral research looks at Indigenous tourism in British Columbia, with research contexts on Haida Gwaii and Squamish lands\. I study what happens when tourism is more than sightseeing, and when visitors are asked to think about place, history, culture, responsibility, and their own role as guests on Indigenous lands\.

Much of my work focuses on authenticity, expectations, trust, and visitor responsibility in Indigenous tourism\. I am interested in how visitors decide whether an experience feels meaningful and credible, and why emotional responses may matter as much as factual or cultural content\. I also look at how visitors come to trust what they are learning, especially when cultural authority, guide legitimacy, and relationships affect how knowledge is received\.

My work also examines storytelling, guide trust, respectful learning conditions, and the uncertainty visitors may feel when learning about difficult histories\. I am interested in whether Indigenous\-led tourism can help visitors reflect on history, responsibility, and their own relationship to Indigenous lands and communities\. I do not assume that tourism produces reconciliation\. Instead, I ask what kinds of learning, reflection, and responsibility may be possible in these settings\.

My dissertation uses visitor surveys, interviews, and qualitative analysis\. It connects tourism studies, consumer behaviour, Indigenous entrepreneurship, Indigenous business, and responsible travel\. I want the research to contribute to academic debates while also being useful to Indigenous tourism operators, educators, destination organizations, and policymakers\.
