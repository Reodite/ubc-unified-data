***Finding words in speech: How bilingual learners use statistical and prosodic cues***  
**Helen Shiyang Lu, PhD**  
Killam Postdoctoral Research Fellow, School of Audiology and Speech Sciences, University of British Columbia

**ABSTRACT****:** Early language development requires infants to discover words in the continuous stream of speech they hear every day\. This process, often called word segmentation, is an important step for building vocabulary and later language skills\. However, natural speech does not contain clear acoustic markers separating words\. To solve it, learners can rely on multiple sources of information in the speech signal, including statistical regularities \(how often one syllable follows another\) and language\-specific prosodic patterns \(such as the strong\-weak stress pattern typical of English words like “BAby”\)\. Understanding how learners integrate these cues during typical development remains an important question, particularly for bilingual learners who must navigate two linguistic systems that may provide different or even conflicting signals\. In this talk, I will present ongoing work examining how listeners weigh statistical and prosodic cues when segmenting speech\. English monolingual and English\-Cantonese bilingual adults completed word\-segmentation tasks in which these cues pointed to different word boundaries\. By comparing performance across language contexts, we can examine whether listeners flexibly adjust how they use these cues\. I will also briefly discuss parallel work with 9\-month\-old infants that investigates when this flexibility begins to emerge in development\.

**BIO:**  
**Helen Shiyang Lu** is a postdoctoral research fellow in the School of Audiology and Speech Sciences, working in the Language and Development Lab and the Infant Studies Centre in the Department of Psychology\. She received her PhD in Developmental Psychology from the University of Southern California in 2024 and her BA in Psychology and Linguistics from UCLA in 2018\. Her research examines the perceptual and cognitive mechanisms that support early language learning, with a focus on statistical learning and speech segmentation in monolingual and bilingual learners\.
