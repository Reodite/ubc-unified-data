##  Undergraduate Students Sub\-Navigation

#### NSERC USRAs and WLI URAs

The Mathematics Department is pleased to offer summer research positions for academically high\-achieving undergraduate students\. The NSERC Undergraduate Student Research Awards \(USRAs\) and Work Learn International Undergraduate Research Awards \(WLI URAs\) give students the opportunity to work with a faculty supervisor, acquiring valuable research experience in an academic setting\. This opportunity is also useful for enhancing students’ resumes, for those interested in enrolling in a graduate program or for helping to decide a future career route\.

Jump to [Research Projects](https://www.math.ubc.ca/undergraduate/employment/undergraduate-research-positions#Research%20Projects)

**Position Details**  
The normal duration of these research positions is 16 weeks of full\-time work in the summer\.To be eligible, applicants need to reside in British Columbia and not be taking summer courses for the duration of the program\. At the end of the research position, students write a short report on their activities\.

The stipend for these awards is yet to be confirmed\. In 2025 summer the minimum paid for the 16 weeks of full\-time work was $10187\.27\.

**Eligibility**

-   Applicants must have a strong academic standing, an average of over 80% in Mathematics Courses and a minimum of 70% overall\.
-   NSERC USRAs \- If you are a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, check [UBC NSERC website](https://students.ubc.ca/career/campus-experiences/nserc-undergraduate-student-research-awards) for eligibility details\. We welcome applications from students at other eligible universities\. 
-   WLI URAs \- If you are an international student, check the Work Learn International website [Work Learn International website](https://students.ubc.ca/career/campus-experiences/undergraduate-research/work-learn-international-undergraduate-research-awards) for eligibility details\. You must be attending UBC Vancouver\.
-   Students who are offered a position but do not meet the criteria above will not be able to accept a position\.
-   Students intending to graduate in 2026 will be given a lower priority\.
-   You must be residing in British Columbia during the length of the project \(usually May \- August\)\.

Chadi Saad\-Roy \- Projects in the Mathematics of Infectious Disease

There are multiple possible projects available on mathematical modelling the dynamics of infectious diseases\.

1\) **Mathematical models to understand the effects of disease\-induced mortality after recovery**

Infectious disease can affect individuals long after recovery \(e\.g\. Long COVID\)\.These effects can take many forms, and include elevated mortality after recovery, i\.e\., post\-infection mortality \(PIM\)\. Recent work has studied the epidemiological impacts of PIM and revealed that PIM can cause long\-term epidemiological oscillations \(Saad\-Roy et al\., Proc R Soc B 2023\), and examined the interactions with characteristics of immunity \(Saad\-Roy et al\., PLoS Complex Syst 2025\)\. Subsequent work \(Saad\-Roy et al\., Proc R Soc B 2024\) examined the evolution of PIM\. However, many questions surrounding PIM, its evolution, and its effects on population dynamics remain\. Mathematical models will be needed to investigate these\. This project will build on these initial studies to develop models for the exploration of either the epidemiological effects and/or the evolutionary dynamics of PIM\. This project will require knowledge of differential equations, some programming language for numerics, and linear algebra\.

2\) **Mathematical modelling the interactions between infectious disease dynamics and human behaviour**

As seen throughout the COVID\-19 pandemic, infectious disease can affect human behaviour and shape individual decision\-making\. At the same time, this decision\-making can directly affect transmission\. To capture these interactions, evolutionary game theoretic and epidemiological models can be coupled\. This project will build on prior work \(Traulsen, Levin, and Saad\-Roy, PNAS 2023; Saad\-Roy and Traulsen 2023\) to further explore these interactions\. This project will require knowledge of differential equations, some programming language for numerics, and linear algebra\.

3\) **Mathematical models for SARS\-CoV\-2 immuno\-epidemiology**

Host immune responses can shape medium\- and long\-term population\-level trajectories of SARS\-CoV\-2\. A number of characteristics, such as the strength and duration of immunity following recovery \(Saad\-Roy\*, Wagner\* et al\., Science 2020\), vaccine dosing regimes \(Saad\-Roy et al\., Science 2021\) the accumulation of immunity \(Saad\-Roy et al\., J R Soc Interface 2023\), severity\-blocking immunity \(Saad\-Roy et al\. PLoS Comp Biol 2024\), or vaccine nationalism \(Wagner\*, Saad\-Roy\* et al\., Science 2021\) can affect these trajectories\. This project will build on these previous works to further investigate the roles of various immune uncertainties on the dynamics of SARS\-CoV\-2 immuno\-epidemiology\. This project will require knowledge of differential equations and knowledge of R\.

Ido Levin \- Mathematical Modelling and Computational Geometry of Shape\-Morphing Materials

My group combines experiments and mathematical modelling to investigate diverse questions at the intersection of geometry, soft materials, and pattern formation\. Most projects use a semi\-analytical approach, combining analytical analysis with numerical calculations\. These projects also involve analysing and presenting various types of data\. Many include collaboration with experimental work conducted in my lab\.

**Currently available projects:**

1.  **Inverse and Forward Design of Shape\-Morphing Programmable Materials**  
    This project focuses on developing numerical schemes to design and analyse shape\-morphing materials \(materials that can change their shape in response to an external trigger\)\. Forward design involves optimising mechanical energy expressed in geometric terms\. Inverse design involves searching for the optimal configuration to achieve a desired shape or set of mechanical properties\.  
     
2.  **Geometric Phase Space and Information Density of Multi\-Mode Materials**  
    Most existing shape\-morphing materials are programmed with only a single shape\. My group fabricates the next generation of these materials—ones that can be programmed with multiple shapes and actuated by different external signals\. The design space for these materials is extremely rich\. This project will explore different design strategies for our multi\-mode materials by studying the dynamics of various trajectories in geometric design space\.  
     
3.  **Chemo\-Mechanical Pattern Formation**  
    My group is building autonomous shape\-morphing materials—materials that change their shape without any external signal\. To achieve this, we utilize chemical reactions to fuel and orchestrate shape transformations, very similar to how electrical signals control the shape of our beating hearts\. Mathematical modelling of the chemo\-mechanical coupling between reactions and materials involves reaction\-diffusion PDEs \(a type of nonlinear diffusion equation\) that evolve on curved surfaces\. This project will study how the evolving shape affects the spatiotemporal dynamics of the chemical reaction\.

Other projects may be available as well\. For inquiries and additional details, please email [idolevin@math\.ubc\.ca](mailto:idolevin@math.ubc.ca)\.

Dominic Liao\-McPherson &amp; Miranda Holmes\-Cerfon \- Distributionally Robust Optimization for Materials Design

This project will be jointly supervised by Dominic Liao\-McPherson \(Mechanical Engineering\) and Miranda Holmes\-Cerfon \(Mathematics\)\.

Lattice\-based or porous materials are found widely in nature and provide an elegant solution where low weight and high performance are required\. Examples include strong but light\-weight materials for aerospace components or running shoes, materials for sound insulation or transmission, and materials with desirable responses to electromagnetic properties such as cloaking or lensing\. Designing such materials has traditionally focused on designing a unit cell, using deterministic rules alongside optimization methods, which is then repeated periodically to create a long range structure\. Unfortunately, periodic, lattice\-based materials result in a confined space of materials properties, often with unavoidable anisotropy, and are prone to catastrophic failure under modest defect formation\. On the other hand, adding some disorder to a lattice, has recently been shown to improve its resilience, increase the isotropy of its response, and to offer a larger design space of achievable properties\. Indeed, biological materials, such as bone, shells, beaks, leaves, wood, etc, are disordered, a property that is thought to give them the ability to perform well in a greater variety of conditions\.

Our goal is to develop methods for designing disordered lattices using tools from stochastic optimization, optimal transport, and distributionally robust optimization\. We will formulate material design and a problem of optimizing macroscopic performance and robustness properties of the material over a probability distribution of unit cell properties\. The result will be a mathematical framework for designing materials with disordered lattices\. We hypothesize that by embracing randomness and disorder we can systematically design more performant and resilient materials\.

**Prerequisites:**  
Excellent knowledge of probability or statistics, some knowledge of optimization, and good programming skills\.

Zinovy Reichstein &amp; Danny Ofek \- The 3\-Pfister Number of a Quadratic Form

Over the field of complex numbers, every n\-dimensional non\-degenerate quadratic form is isomorphic to the unit form \(x\_1, \.\.\., x\_n\) \-&gt; x\_1^2 \+ \.\.\. \+ x\_n^2\. Over the field of real numbers, every n\-dimensional non\-degenerate quadratic form is isomorphic to \(x\_1, \.\.\., x\_n\) \-&gt; x\_1^2 \+ \.\.\. \+ x\_a^2 \- x\_\{a\+1\}^2 \- \.\.\. \- x\_n^2, for a unique choice of a \= 0, 1, \.\.\., n\.

The classification of quadratic forms over an arbitrary field F is much more complicated\. Non\-degenerate quadratic forms over F \(of all dimensions\) can be organized into a so\-called Witt ring W\(F\)\. The Witt ring has a maximal ideal I\(F\) consisting of even\-dimensional forms\. The definitions of W\(F\) and I\(F\) are entirely elementary, but these objects give rise to very deep mathematics\. In particular, the quotients of I^k\(F\)/I^\{k\+1\}\(F\) of successive powers of the fundamental ideal are the subject of the celebrated Milnor Conjecture, proved by Voevodsky in 1996\.

This project is related to other \(still open\) problems about the structure of W\(F\) and I\(F\)\. A r\-fold Pfister form is a product of r two\-dimensional forms &lt;1, a\_1&gt;, \.\.\., &lt;1, a\_r&gt; in the Witt ring\. These are important building blocks of W\(F\)\. It is easy to show that every form q in I^r\(F\) can be written as a sum of r\-fold Pfister forms\. The minimal number of summands in any such decomposition of q is called that r\-Pfister number of q and denoted by Pf\_r\(q\)\. Now let q ranges over the non\-degenerate forms of fixed dimension n in I^k\(F\)\. For r &gt;\=3, it is not known how large the value of Pf\_r\(q\) can be\. If we fix n and allow arbitrary F and q, the supremum Pf\_r\(n\) of Pf\_r\(q\) is known to be finite for r &lt;\= 3\. For r \= 3, Pf\_r\(n\) is known to increase exponentially with n, but no specific upper bound is known\. For r &gt;\= 4, Pf\_r\(n\) is conjectured to be infinite\. If we allow the field F to be arbitrary, these questions are out of reach at the moment\. The project will focus on a particular \(still fairly rich\) class of fields, where W\(F\) admits a combinatorial model which makes these questions more accessible\.

Ian Frigaard,Yosef Rezaei &amp; Soheil Akbari \- Undergraduate Research Opportunity in Fluid Mechanics and Rheology

Leakage of reservoir fluids from oil and gas wells is a major public concern due to the risks of groundwater contamination and greenhouse gas \(GHG\) emissions\. Wells often pass through sensitive geological formations containing brines and hydrocarbons\. To maintain wellbore integrity, a steel casing is installed inside the wellbore, and cement is pumped to displace the resident fluids in the annular space between the casing and the surrounding rock, establishing a durable seal, ensuring long\-term wellbore integrity\. Despite its importance, inadequate cementing practices have resulted in thousands of leaking wells worldwide, contributing significantly to methane emissions\.

This research project aims to improve fundamental understanding of displacement and exchange flows that occur during cementing processes\. The work involves preparing and characterizing complex fluids, analyzing their rheological behavior and investigating the flow dynamics associated with displacing Newtonian and non\- Newtonian \(viscoplastic\) fluids\. Particular attention is given to the influence of wall irregularities, casing ovality and well inclination\. A defining feature of viscoplastic fluids is their yield stress, which governs whether the material flows under applied stress, hence playing a critical role in buoyancy\-driven displacement flows\. By combining experimental investigation with modeling, this project seeks to generate insights that can inform improved industrial practices and help mitigate environmental risks associated with well leakage\.

Ian Frigaard &amp; Hossein Rahmani \- Undergraduate Research Opportunity in Fluid Mechanics and Rheology

Leakage of CH₄ and other gases from oil and gas wells is a major source of industrial greenhouse\-gas \(GHG\) emissions\. This risk becomes even more consequential as depleted or repurposed reservoirs are used for carbon capture and storage \(CCS\), where CO₂ can migrate along compromised wellbores, micro\-annuli, and caprock discontinuities \(e\.g\., faults and fractures\)\. Data suggests that on the order of 10% of wells may exhibit leakage due to sustained integrity failures under current construction practices\. Therefore, leak detection and integrity verification during decommissioning, which are necessary steps before final abandonment, are critical for long\-term containment and highlight the urgent need for robust, scalable leakage\-mitigation technologies\. Squeeze cementing, in which cement slurry is injected through a perforated casing to fill leakage paths, is the standard repair process used to mitigate well leakage\. However, squeeze cementing often has a low success rate, with only around 30% succeeding on the first attempt\. This motivates the development of sophisticated, experiment\-supported models to predict and optimize squeeze\-cementing operations\.

In this research project, the aim is to develop models, supported with experiments, that predict cement\-slurry penetration into leakage pathways that form around the well casing\. Such models will enable prediction of the pumping pressure required to ensure these pathways are effectively sealed\. Because the geometry of these pathways is highly uncertain, the model must be fast enough to simulate thousands of leakage scenarios within a few minutes\. These predictions can guide squeeze\-cementing operations and improve their success rate\. In addition to modeling, experiments will be conducted to validate and support the model predictions\.

Ian Frigaard &amp; Fatemeh Bararpour \- Non\-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics and How It Addresses Real\-world Industrial Challenges

**Background:** Gas emissions are the key concern in the construction of oil and gas wells\. The gas emission mainly occurs due to methane migration from constructed wells in which the annular space between the well’s wall and the rock layers is not sealed properly with cement slurry\. The gas leakage reduces oil production rates and causes health issues\. One of the key factors to avoid gas leakage problems is a successful primary cementing operation\. As the annular space of constructed wells is initially filled with drilling mud, the main goal of this operation is to remove the drilling mud and replace with cement, to seal the annular space completely with cement layers\.

**Specific area for summer project:** In this operation, the displacing fluid \(cement slurry\) is pumped at a high flow rate, and mixing between the drilling mud and the cement slurry occurs, thereby reducing the effectiveness of the cement layers in sealing the annular space\. Cementing fluids behave as viscoplastic fluids: they switch from fluid to solid behaviour when they are not sufficiently stressed\. In this project, we aim to investigate the influence of fluid properties and geometrical parameters on this displacement process, and to find a way to minimize mixing between the cement slurry and drilling mud, thereby making the cement layers more effective at sealing the annular space\. To this end, we plan to simulate the process using a cementing model, namely the dispersive two\-dimensional gap\-averaged \(D2DGA\) model, and three\-dimensional \(3D\) simulations, and finally compare the outcomes with those observed in experiment\.

**What intern will do \(skills needed/developed\):** A large number of experiments have already been conducted\. The summer student will assist with organizing the experimental data and performing image processing for selected cases\. Moreover, the summer student will help with running both D2DGA and 3D simulations for those scenarios\. All these tasks require a strong passion and enthusiasm for learning viscoplastic fluid flows in an annular space and understanding how the proposed research study can contribute to designing a successful primary cementing operation\. By the end of the project, the summer student will achieve a high level of experimental and computational skills in image processing and post\-processing of computational results\. The summer student will also develop strong teamwork, collaboration, and research skills through well\-structured research training and mentorship\.

**Who should apply:** This position could be suitable for undergraduate students in Applied Mathematics, Engineering, Engineering Physics or and Physics\. Students who have successfully completed a fluid mechanics course are encouraged to apply\. The student will work in the Complex Fluid Research Group led by Dr Ian Frigaard and will receive financial support through NSERC USRA or WLIURA programs, which are competitive – hence a strong academic record is needed\.

Ailana Fraser \- Minimal Surfaces

Minimization problems arise naturally in many branches of mathematics and science\. For example, problems in navigation involve finding paths of least length \(geodesics\) on the earth's surface\. Minimal surfaces, which are two\-dimensional analogs of geodesics, are minimizers \(or simply critical points\) of the area function, and arise naturally in material science; for example in fluid interface problems and elasticity problems\. A simple physical example of a minimal surface is the soap film that forms after dipping a wire frame into a soap solution\. By the laws of surface tension this soap film has the property that it is stable, that is it becomes larger under slight deformations\. The theory of minimal surfaces has had striking applications, for example to general relativity and low dimensional topology\.

This project will involve theoretical, numerical, and graphical problems with minimal surfaces and eigenvalue problems, tailored to the interests and background of the research assistant\.

Ben Williams \- Distinguishing Orders in the 4\-generator Rational Exterior Algebra

Let R denote the Q\-algebra generated by 4 variables x\_1, x\_2, x\_3, x\_4 satisfying x\_i x\_j \= \-x\_j x\_i for all i,j\. Each variable is assigned a degree of 1, making R into a 16\-dimensional graded Q\-vector space equipped with a multiplication\. An \_order\_ in R is a subring A whose underlying additive abelian group is a graded free abelian group generated by some homogeneous basis for the Q\-vector space R\.

This project will consider the problem of finding invariants to distinguish non\-isomorphic orders\. Variations on the problem can also be considered\.

The intended application of the project is the following: we know how to construct topological spaces X whose cohomology rings H^\*\(X; Z\) are certain orders in R\. In the jargon we use, these orders are realizable\. We hope that by understanding all orders better, we can find new constructions of topological spaces that allow us to realize all orders in R\. The analogous problem for 3 variables is essentially solved\.

The project requires knowledge of ring theory and of elementary number theory, and fluency with finitely generated abelian groups\. Knowledge of topology is not required\.

Malabika Pramanik\- Structure and Randomness Across Discrete and Continuous Worlds

Many modern problems in mathematics, including those arising in harmonic analysis and geometric measure theory, bridge ideas and techniques across multiple areas of the subject\. This project explores the interplay between structure and randomness across discrete and continuous mathematical settings\.

Students will investigate how combinatorial and probabilistic ideas in finite or discrete systems connect to questions about size, density, and structure in infinite or continuous spaces\. Through concrete examples and guided problem\-solving, the project will examine when random behavior leads to rigid structure, and when highly structured objects can mimic randomness\. Emphasis will be placed on understanding how discrete models approximate continuous phenomena, and how mathematical properties can change when passing from finite to infinite settings\.

The project is designed to be accessible to undergraduates with a strong interest in pure mathematics and emphasizes creative reasoning, experimentation, and proof\. An interest in real analysis and their applications is a prerequisite\. A background in discrete mathematics and/or measure theory is helpful, but not required\.

In order to form a normal embryo, cells have to specialize and commit to specific fates\. Greg Martin \- A question of Erdös on Sidon sets

A Sidon set is a set whose pairwise sums a\+b are all distinct \(other than the trivial a\+b \= b\+a\)\. If we let R\(n\) denote the size of the largest Sidon subset of \{1, 2, \.\.\., n\}, then it is known that R\(n\) is roughly νn for large enough n\. Erdös asked whether it is possible that \| R\(n\) \- νn \| is bounded; such an unlikely result would indicate some extreme regularity to Sidon sets\. The student will work with [Greg Martin](https://www.math.ubc.ca/~gerg/), where we propose to settle this question in the negative\. Most constructions of large Sidon sets proceed by constucting a Sidon set modulo some integer q \(for example, the construction might take place in a finite field\) and then noting that any Sidon set \(mod q\) remains a Sidon set when considered as a set of integers\. Moreover, the \(mod q\) construction is usually preserved under affine transformations x \-\-&gt; ax\+b \(mod q\), a property that can be detected using exponential sums\. If one of these affine transformations ends up moving the whole Sidon set so that it does not intersect the interval \[m, q\], then the resulting Sidon set is still large but is now a subset of \{1, 2, \.\.\. , m\}, resulting in a large enough value for R\(m\) to answer Erdös's\.
