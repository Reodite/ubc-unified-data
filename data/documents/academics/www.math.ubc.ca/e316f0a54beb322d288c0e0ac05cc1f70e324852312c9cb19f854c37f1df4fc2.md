Abstract: 

Given $n$ lines in the plane, among which no two are parallel, how many points lie in at least two of the lines? If all the lines go through a single point, then the answer is "just $1$"\. But what if not all lines go through a point? In 1983, J\. Beck gave an essentially sharp answer\. For example, he showed that if no point lies in half of the lines, then the  number of line intersections is close to $n^2$, which is the maximum possible value\. This can be thought of as a dichotomy: either "many" of the lines meet at a point, or there is a "nearly maximal" number of line intersections\. After reviewing Beck's Theorem, I will discuss a "fuzzy" variant of this problem in which lines are replaced by thin tubes \($\\delta$\-neighbourhoods of a line\), and we identify points that are $\\delta$\-close to each other\. This innocent\-looking question turns out to be related to deep problems in harmonic analysis, combinatorial geometry, and geometric measure theory\. The result I will present can be seen as a fuzzy analog of Beck's theorem, and was recently obtained in collaboration with Tuomas Orponen \(Jyväskylä\) and Hong Wang \(NYU\)\. The talk will be accessible to a wide audience including undergraduates\. No background will be assumed\.
