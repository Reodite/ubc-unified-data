## What is this course all about?

What happens when the brain becomes the primary language through which we explain who we are?

This course examines the cultural, political, and poetic life of neuroscience\. Rather than treating neuroscience only as a body of laboratory knowledge, NSCI 486A considers how neuroscientific ideas move through psychiatry, therapy culture, education, social media, advertising, self\-help discourse, public policy, and everyday conversation\. Students will explore how concepts such as plasticity, prediction, circuitry, disorder, attention, and trauma influence contemporary understandings of the self\.

At the centre of the course is the figure of the cerebral subject: the modern self increasingly understood through the language of the brain\. Through this lens, students will ask how brain\-based explanations make certain forms of identity, suffering, treatment, and care more visible, while potentially obscuring others\. The course pays close attention to what neuroscience can explain, but also to what may remain outside its reach\.

The course moves between research and lived experience, between synapse and story, and between the measurable and the embodied\. Alongside foundational research, students will engage with memoir, poetry, film, ethnography, media, psychiatry, science and technology studies, and critical theory\. These materials are treated as forms of knowledge that can extend and reframe neuroscientific accounts of human life\. 

## What will you learn and gain?

By the end of NSCI 486A, students will be able to:  

• Explain and critically evaluate key concepts in contemporary neuroscience related to selfhood, perception, memory, emotion, and mental health\. 

• Analyze how neuroscientific ideas circulate culturally and politically through media, institutions, and everyday life\. 

• Examine the relationship between neuroscience, psychiatry, identity, and power\.

• Interpret memoir, poetry, film, and narrative as forms of knowledge about mind and experience\. 

• Critically evaluate neuroscientific claims in public discourse and popular media\. 

• Communicate complex ideas across scientific, humanistic, and creative modes of inquiry\. 

• Develop nuanced approaches to the relationship between biology, subjectivity, culture, and lived experience\.

## Who is this course for?

This course is designed for students who are interested in neuroscience, psychology, mental health, psychiatry, media studies, literature, science and technology studies, and the arts\.

Students should come prepared to think across disciplines and to engage with ambiguity\. The course asks students to take scientific ideas seriously while also examining their limits and social consequences\. It is well suited to students who are curious about how neuroscience shapes contemporary ways of understanding what it means to be human\.

A background in one of NSCI 140, NSCI 201, or PSYC 301 is required to register\. Students outside the Faculty of Science who meet that prerequisite requirement will need to email [advising@neuro\.ubc\.ca](mailto:advising@neuro.ubc.ca) in order to register\.
