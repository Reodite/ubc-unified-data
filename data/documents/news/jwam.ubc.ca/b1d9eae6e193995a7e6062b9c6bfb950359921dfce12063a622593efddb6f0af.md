A series of multimedia stories chronicling efforts by Metro Vancouver Aboriginal communities to address such major health and social issues as suicide, sexual abuse, diabetes and the survival of traditional languages launched today at [www\.indigenousreporting\.com](http://www.indigenousreporting.com/)\.[RIIC student Linsday sample behind the camera](https://jwam.ubc.ca/news/ubc-j-school-partners-with-first-nations-to-report-on-major-health-issues/)

The stories will run on CBC Radio and TV in B\.C\. throughout the week, and be featured on its [websites](http://www.cbc.ca/bc/community/blog/2012/04/special-series---shxweyelh---stories-of-health-and-healing-in-first-nations-communities.html)\. The national [CBC program The Story from Here](http://www.cbc.ca/thestoryfromhere/) is scheduled to broadcast the entire series as a one\-hour radio documentary on June 20\.

The project was produced by students in the UBC Graduate School of Journalism’s [inaugural Reporting in Indigenous Communities class](https://soj.sites.olt.ubc.ca/news/item/ubc_launches_groundbreaking_aboriginal_journalism_course) – Canada’s only university journalism course dedicated to improving the quality of Aboriginal representation by the news media – by way of a unique partnership with Metro Vancouver Aboriginal communities\.

“Far too often, news media portray Aboriginal people in ways that reinforce negative or inaccurate stereotypes,” says [Duncan McCue](https://soj.sites.olt.ubc.ca/faculty/duncan_mccue), the award\-winning CBC reporter and adjunct professor who led the course\. “By exposing the next generation of journalists to Aboriginal stories, cultures and protocols, we have produced a series that shows Aboriginal people not as victims, but as catalysts of positive change\.”

The students learned the history, politics and unique cultures of Aboriginal peoples in Canada and abroad, and analyzed media representation issues\. They also studied the [online guide and checklist](http://www.riic.ca/reporters-checklist/) that McCue – one of the only Aboriginal mainstream reporters in Vancouver – created during a [Knight Fellowship](http://knight.stanford.edu/fellows/2011/mccue/) at Stanford University to help journalists respect Aboriginal cultures and resist stereotypes\.

“We had to engage with the community to develop relationships and trust,” said student Kate Adach, who worked on a story about an Aboriginal suicide response team being developed by members of Sto:lo Nation near Chilliwack, B\.C\. “There is so much to learn about Indigenous history in Canada\. It might seem daunting, but it is absolutely vital that reporters continue to engage, educate ourselves, and report on these important stories responsibly\.”[Malin Dunfors shoots photos for the RIIC class](https://jwam.ubc.ca/news/ubc-j-school-partners-with-first-nations-to-report-on-major-health-issues/)

The five Aboriginal course partners are the Squamish First Nation, Tsleil\-Waututh First Nation, Tsawwassen First Nation, Sto:lo First Nation and the Metro Vancouver Aboriginal Executive Council\.

“A course like this is long, long overdue, and helped to create a positive experience for our community and the students,” says Ernie Crey, an advisor to the Sto:lo Tribal Council\. “Aboriginal people have an important story to tell and they are eager to tell it\. It is important that the outside world understand what is going on\.”

The eight stories from UBC’s inaugural Reporting in Indigenous Communities class are:

-   A Squamish Nation woman’s attempt to bring back the chocolate lily, a traditional staple food that could reduce diabetes
-   A new Sto:lo Nation Aboriginal Suicide and Critical Incident Response Team, which aims to provide culturally\-appropriate response to suicides
-   A Tsleil\-Waututh Nation daycare where teachers are reconnecting youth to traditional language and culture to improve health and happiness
-   Efforts by the Squamish Nation and a provincial judge to rehabilitate offenders with mental health and addiction issues through a First Nations Court
-   At Tsawwassen First Nation, how a young girl’s disclosure may signal a new attitude toward the disturbing problem of sexual abuse
-   A unique support group in Vancouver’s Downtown Eastside, where Aboriginal grandparents raising their grandchildren are learning to care for kids diagnosed with Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder
-   The Seabird Island Mobile Diabetes Clinic, which travels across southern British Columbia so patients can manage their disease from home
-   Students training as Health Care Assistants in First Nation communities, in a course designed for Aboriginal learners at the Native Education Centre in Vancouver
