By Anne Khong, UBC SOWK Alumni

After I completed my BSW program at UBC, I worked as a social worker in community health\. Through this work, revealed the gaps in our health care system, immigration laws and policies that continue to present barriers that directly impact our community members in their access to essential care\. In this social work blog, I will be examining the barriers for uninsured pregnant migrants’ access to perinatal care and the need for structural changes to increase access to health care regardless of one’s status in Canada\.

**Literature Review**

Uninsured migrants may identify as someone who has temporary/precarious status \(i\.e\., work permit\) or no status living in Canada, and they do not qualify for provincial health coverage \(Porter et al\., 2025\)\. According to the National Collaborating Centre for Determinants of Health \(2025\), “immigration status is a significant yet often overlooked determinant of health”\. For uninsured pregnant migrants, due to their status, they may face barriers to access essential care which may pose risks to maternal health and childbirth outcomes \(Goldenberg et al\., 2023\)\. Furthermore, Canada’s health care system is not universal as it claims to be \(Goldenberg et al\., 2023; Ridde et al\., 2020\)\.

The primary barrier preventing uninsured pregnant migrants from receiving adequate care is financial cost, followed by the confusion of navigating the Canadian health care system \(Jarvis et al\., 2019; Darling et al\., 2019; Ridde et al\., 2020\)\. In addition, uninsured pregnant migrants were categorized as most impacted in their experiences of accessing prenatal care which serves as a violation of maternal and child health rights \(Ridde et al\., 2020; Goldenberg et al\., 2023\)\. Being denied essential care due to structural racism and other forms of oppression that are embedded in our systems and policies may lead to discriminatory practices and poor health outcomes \(Goldenberg et al\., 2023\)\.

Canada’s universal health care system operates based on jurisdiction which determines who is eligible and what health care services are to be covered or not set by the Canada Health Act \(Government of Canada, 2025\)\. Those who do not qualify must pay on their own \(out of pocket\) or through private insurance plans \(Government of Canada, 2025\)\. Uninsured migrants are excluded from public health insurance and when they do access care such as going to the emergency department for specialized care, there is a possibility of getting denied care which exposes them to high levels of stress and mistrust in the system \(Goldenberg et al\., 2023\)\. The exclusion of uninsured migrants may result in an economic burden for both the person and health care system \(Ridde et al\., 2020\)\. From a health provider’s perspective, this raises ethical dilemmas on how to prioritize and provide care based on affordability and how the ethics of care may differ across professions \(Porter et al\., 2025; Jarvis et al\., 2019\), including social work\.

**Recommendations for Social Work Practice**

I remember being shocked and devastated when I came across the costs it would take for an uninsured person to give birth at a hospital in Canada and having to relay that information to them\. This puts families in a difficult position to choose between paying for their health, food or housing\. As a social worker in these situations and being human, strong emotions may emerge and our political stance and biases may be projected onto the client\. When this occurs, it is helpful to seek supervision or consultation to critically reflect on the forms of oppression in our health care system and policies that stem from systemic racism that discriminate against BIPOC community members from accessing care and how social workers may be perpetuating that narrative and potential harm\.

In alignment with the social work values of respecting human dignity and promotion of social equity and justice \(CASW, 2024\), it is important as social workers to acknowledge our positionality when supporting marginalized and diverse populations to advocate and address the barriers and inequities with cultural humility and utmost compassionate care\. Social workers can support uninsured pregnant migrants by redirecting them to appropriate cost\-saving resources in the community and within their team, educating their colleagues on how to effectively work with uninsured migrants \(Porter et al\., 2025\)\. To conclude, there are programs across Canada that exist to support uninsured pregnant migrants to access adequate care with dignity\. Moreover, there are grassroots organizations and collectives across Canada that social workers and the public can engage in to learn and get involved in policy advocacy to increase access to health care services for all \(NCCDH, 2025\)\.
