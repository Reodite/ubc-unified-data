By Shamil Alizada, BSW 2026

Modern psychiatry often speaks with the confidence of science\. It presents itself as a field that can identify, classify, and treat mental distress through diagnostic categories, biological explanations, and clinical interventions\. I do not deny that psychiatry has a role, especially in severe conditions where a person’s relationship to reality is visibly disrupted\. But I do question the authority that psychiatry claims over the full range of human suffering\.

My concern is not simply that psychiatry is imperfect\. Every field is imperfect\. My concern is deeper: psychiatry often presents philosophical and spiritual problems as if they were primarily medical ones\. It takes distress that may arise from the human condition itself and translates it into the language of symptoms, disorders, and treatment plans\. In doing so, it risks misunderstanding the very suffering it claims to explain\.

Anxiety and depression are often explained through childhood adversity, genetics, neurobiology, or chemical processes\. These explanations may have some value\. A person who grows up in a violent, unstable, or neglectful home may develop a nervous system that is more alert, fearful, or reactive\. Genetics may also influence temperament and vulnerability\. These points are reasonable\.

But they are not enough\.

Much of the evidence used in these discussions is correlational\. A correlation between adverse childhood experiences and later anxiety or depression does not, by itself, establish a clean causal relationship\. Human beings are not machines where one input produces one predictable output\. Childhood, biology, and social conditions matter, but none of these explanations fully captures what it means for a person to suffer\.

This is where psychiatry often becomes too narrow\. It tries to explain distress by looking downward, toward chemicals, brain processes, symptoms, and diagnostic criteria\. But much of human suffering points upward and outward, toward meaning, death, justice, goodness, evil, disappointment, loneliness, and the apparent indifference of the world\.

These are not side issues\. They are central\.

Many people who experience anxiety or depression are not merely responding to a faulty brain state\. They are asking questions that have always troubled human beings\. Why is there suffering? Why does evil exist? What is the point of life if death ends everything? Is justice real, or is it just a human invention? Why should we believe in goodness when the world often rewards cruelty, power, and indifference? How can someone continue to live with hope after seeing too much pain?

Psychiatry can easily classify these struggles as symptoms\. Persistent worry, loss of joy, preoccupation with death, lack of motivation, despair, hopelessness, and fear can all be placed under diagnostic language\. But this creates a serious problem\. Once these experiences are absorbed into the category of disorder, the deeper questions behind them can disappear\.

The person is no longer seen primarily as someone confronting the weight of existence\. They become a patient with symptoms\.

It assumes that the normal human state is emotional stability, productivity, and general contentment, and that prolonged distress is therefore a deviation from health\. But who decided that this is the normal state of the human mind? On what grounds? There is no neutral, purely scientific answer to that question\. Psychiatry depends on value judgments about what counts as normal, functional, healthy, or disordered\. It is not simply reading facts from nature\. It is interpreting human life through a particular framework\.

This is why I do not believe psychiatry can be treated as a pure science in the way chemistry or physics can\. Psychiatry deals with meaning, behaviour, emotion, identity, morality, and social expectations\. These are not only biological realities\. They are also philosophical and cultural realities\. To call something a disorder is not merely to describe it\. It is also to make a judgment about how a person ought to be\.

The history of psychiatry should make us cautious here\. This is the same field that once classified homosexuality as a mental disorder\. That was not a small technical error\. It was a profound failure of judgment\. It shows that psychiatric categories are not immune to cultural prejudice, moral assumptions, and institutional authority\. They can reflect the worldview of a time while presenting themselves as the objective truth\.

This does not mean all psychiatry is useless\. It means psychiatry should be much more humble than it often is\.

I am especially skeptical of attempts to reduce depression and anxiety to chemical explanations\. Human experience cannot be fully understood by isolating neurotransmitters or brain regions\. Of course, the brain is involved in suffering\. The brain is involved in everything we experience\. But involvement is not the same as explanation\.

A bicycle is not just a pile of metal parts\. If you dismantle it, you can describe each part in detail, but you no longer have the bicycle as a whole\. In the same way, if you isolate chemicals, neurons, and biological processes, you may describe some mechanisms involved in distress, but you have not explained the subjective experience of suffering itself\.

Despair is not just a chemical event\. It is also a confrontation with reality, or at least with how reality appears to the person suffering\.

This is where spirituality becomes necessary\.

By spirituality, I do not mean forcing religion onto people\. I do not mean pretending that baptism, prayer, or religious belief will make everyone happy\. That would be simplistic\. I mean that human beings have always carried spiritual questions, whether or not they belong to a religion\. We ask whether life has meaning, whether suffering can be redeemed, whether goodness is real, whether death is final, and whether justice exists beyond human opinion\.

These are not decorative questions\. They sit at the centre of human life\.

Religion, whatever one thinks of its truth claims, has historically addressed these questions directly\. It tells human beings that suffering is not meaningless, that goodness is not foolish, that justice is real, that death is not necessarily the end, and that human life is grounded in something higher than accident\. Again, whether these claims are true is a separate question\. But religion at least understands the depth of the problem\.

Modern psychiatry often does not\.

It tries to manage distress without asking whether the distress is telling us something important\. It asks how to reduce symptoms, but not always what the symptoms are about\. It asks whether a person is functioning, but not whether the world they are functioning in is spiritually empty\. It asks whether a person can return to work, regulate emotions, and resume ordinary life\. But it often avoids the harder question: what if ordinary life itself feels unbearable because the person sees too clearly its fragility, injustice, and lack of ultimate grounding?

The natural world, viewed purely as nature, is amoral\. It contains beauty, but also violence, disease, suffering, and death\. It does not seem to care about human pain\. This is one reason human beings turn toward spirituality\. Spirituality is not an escape from reality\. At its best, it is an attempt to face reality at its deepest level\.

Some people suffer not because they are biologically defective, but because they cannot reconcile themselves to a world that appears indifferent to their pain\. Some people are anxious not because their nervous system is simply malfunctioning, but because they are awake to uncertainty, death, and the instability of everything they love\. Some people are depressed not because they lack insight, but because they have looked too closely at suffering and cannot easily believe that goodness will prevail\.

To call all of this disorder is, in my view, intellectually lazy\.

Social work should be especially careful not to inherit psychiatry’s narrowness\. At its best, social work understands people within their full context: family, culture, poverty, oppression, trauma, community, belief, history, and meaning\. If that is true, then spirituality cannot be treated as an optional add\-on\. It is part of how many people understand suffering\. It is part of how people survive\. It is part of how people make sense of pain\.

This does not mean social workers should preach, moralize, or impose religion\. They should not\. But they should be able to recognize when distress is existential or spiritual rather than merely clinical\. They should be able to sit with questions of meaning, death, guilt, hope, justice, and despair without immediately translating them into symptoms\.

The goal should not be to replace psychiatry with religion\. The goal is to stop pretending that psychiatry has the authority to explain the whole human person\.

Human beings are not only biological organisms\. They are not only social products\. They are not only nervous systems shaped by trauma\. They are also spiritual beings, meaning\-seeking beings, moral beings, and suffering beings\. Any mental health framework that ignores this will remain incomplete, no matter how scientific it sounds\.

If we truly want to understand human distress, we need to take spirituality seriously\. Not as a sentimental comfort, and not as a private hobby, but as one of the primary dimensions of human life\.
