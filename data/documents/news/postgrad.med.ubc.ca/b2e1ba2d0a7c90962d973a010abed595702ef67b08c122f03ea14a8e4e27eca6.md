The UBC residents with their “patient,” a high\-fidelity mannequin\.

The man was brought to the Emergency Department from the nearby airport\. He was agitated, confused and feverish\. His heart rate was alarmingly high\.

The team of University of British Columbia residents responsible for treating him sprang into action\. First, they had to make a diagnosis without the patient’s history, and only symptoms\. Was it sepsis? Were these psychiatric symptoms? Was it a drug overdose?

The doctors assessed vital and clinical signs, then conducted a rectal exam, which yielded the problem: a packet of cocaine\. As a drug mule, he had ingested the packet, which later ruptured and caused massive drug poisoning\. They administered fluids and drugs to decrease the heart rate, fever and agitation\.

To the six residents on the case, the situation felt very real, complete with high adrenalin levels and quick decision making\. In fact, the “patient” was a high\-fidelity mannequin whose vitals and other responses could be controlled\. The doctors were treating him as part of an annual Simulation Olympiad held recently at the Family Medicine Forum in Toronto\. They faced the added pressure of treating him in front of medical judges and their peers from across Canada\.

Their handling of this mock scenario, then a second, where they treated an elderly man who had suffered a spinal cord injury after falling in a bathtub and did not speak English, earned them first place in the competition\. The judges praised their team communication, skills and knowledge\.

“Simulations are used through residents’ training as a novel educational tool,” says Dr\. Jeanne Macleod, Clinical Assistant Professor with the Faculty of Medicine’s Department of Emergency Medicine and physician lead at Providence Health Care’s new Simulation Resource Education Team, who trained the champions for the competition\. “They get everyone fired up and excited\.”

[Image](https://postgrad.med.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Sim-Olympiad-formal-shot-360x247-1.jpg)

The winning team \(L\-R: Residents Richie Lee, Indy Sahota, Mathieu Surprenant, Ana Gomez, Dan Abrams, Sara Pankratz and Jeanne MacLeod, Clinical Faculty\)

Dr\. Indy Sahota, who is part of an enhanced skills residency program in Emergency Medicine at St\. Paul’s Hospital in Vancouver and acted as team lead, says the competition was certainly nerve\-wracking\. “None of us had participated in simulation competitions before\. We didn’t know what to expect, so it was pretty anxiety\-provoking, which I think was the point\.” The team was given bare\-bones information only\. An added stressor was that he and some others on the team are looking for jobs next year and potential employers were watching\.

“Simulation provides some of the best learning we’ve ever had,” he says\. “It helps you tackle the anxiety when you’re dealing with lots of unknowns, which is part of emergency medicine\.”

Dr\. Macleod says the simulations provide value by exposing the residents to rare medical cases, particularly around resuscitations\. “You may have the training, but you don’t necessarily have the practice\. This gives you those skills\.”

And how did the patient fare after being provided with award\-winning care? “He survived,” she jokes, “to get to another Olympiad\.”
