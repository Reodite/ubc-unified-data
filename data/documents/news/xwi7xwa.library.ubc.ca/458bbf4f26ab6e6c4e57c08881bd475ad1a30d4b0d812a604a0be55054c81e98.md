# **Getting to know your campus libraries**

As you settle into classes take the time to explore campus and discover what UBC Libraries have to support you\! UBC has 14 library branches and divisions that focus on subjects including art, science, law, architecture and more\. Check out all the different [library locations](https://hours.library.ubc.ca/) here\! If you’re new to UBC Libraries or would like to learn more about how to use the different library services and technology \(like how to print and finding your nearest printer\!\), you can start [here](https://www.library.ubc.ca/services/)\.

## Need reference help?

Librarians and student staff are more than happy to help you find resources for your assignments and research\. If you need help formatting citations or finding books and other resources for your courses or would like to learn useful ways to search the libraries at UBC, there are lot of [supports available to you](https://www.library.ubc.ca/help/)\!

Did you know that UBC Library has a service called [AskAway](https://ask.library.ubc.ca/)? This is a place where you can ask your reference questions and chat online with librarian staff to get support\.

If you need more specialized help in your subject area, subject librarians are also available to support you with related research\. They work with their collections in their specific library branches, and you can find a list of [Subject Librarians](https://directory.library.ubc.ca/subjectlibrarians/) here to find yours\!

# Xwi7xwa Library

[Image](https://xwi7xwa-library-10nov2016.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/08/Sidewalk_2-300x225-1.jpeg)    [Image](https://xwi7xwa-library-10nov2016.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/08/Screenshot_11-8-2025_105611_www.bing_.com_-e1755101989891-300x175.jpeg)

At Xwi7xwa Library we centre Indigenous scholarship, and our collection privileges Indigenous perspectives and approaches\. This means that X̱wi7x̱wa collects materials written from First Nations perspectives, such as materials produced by First Nations, First Nations organizations, tribal councils, schools, publishers, researchers, writers and scholars\. Our collection focuses on Indigenous materials from British Columbia and also includes contextual materials on Canadian First Nations\. Additionally, the collection at X̱wi7x̱wa focuses on issues of national and international interest to Indigenous Peoples\.

## Research guides &amp; learning about Musqueam

Xwi7xwa Library has created [Research Guides](https://guides.library.ubc.ca/xwi7xwaresearchguide) to get you started on research topics from land acknowledgements, residential schools, treaties, Indigenous languages and education, decolonization and much more\! These guides include great search strategies for finding resources that are relevant to the multidisciplinary study of Indigenous topics and materials written from Indigenous perspectives\. 

UBC Vancouver campus is located on the traditional, ancestral, and unceded territory of the hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓ speaking xʷməθkʷəy̓əm \(Musqueam\) people\. To learn about Musqueam First Nation see our [xʷməθkʷəy̓əm: Land Beneath Our Feet](https://guides.library.ubc.ca/Musqueam) research guide\. You have likely heard land acknowledgements like the one above being said as you return to campus to acknowledge that UBC is situated on Musqueam territory\. If you would like to learn further about what [land acknowledgements](https://guides.library.ubc.ca/Musqueam/MusqueamandUBC) are and the meaning behind them, and about Musqueam\-UBC relationships and history, you can look at the resources on this page within the Musqueam research guide\.

    #### **These Mysterious People: Shaping History and Archaeology in a Northwest Coast Community, Second Edition \(2016\) by Susan Roy\.**
    
    ##### “Archaeologists studying human remains and burial sites of North America’s Indigenous peoples have discovered more than information about the beliefs and practices of cultures–they have also found controversy\. *These Mysterious People* shows how Western ideas and attitudes about Indigenous peoples have transformed one culture’s ancestors, burial grounds, and possessions into another culture’s “specimens,” “archaeological sites,” and “ethnographic artifacts,” in the process disassociating Natives from their own histories\. Focusing on the Musqueam people and a contentious archaeological site in Vancouver, *These Mysterious People* details the relationship between the Musqueam and researchers from the late\-nineteenth century to the present\. Susan Roy traces the historical development of competing understandings of the past and reveals how the Musqueam First Nation used information derived from archaeological finds to assist the larger recognition of territorial rights\. She also details the ways in which Musqueam legal and cultural expressions of their own history–such as land claim submissions, petitions, cultural displays, and testimonies–have challenged public accounts of Aboriginal occupation and helped to define Aboriginal rights in Canada\. An important and engaging examination of methods of historical representation, *These Mysterious People* analyses the ways historical evidence, material culture, and places themselves have acquired legal and community authority\.”– Provided by publisher\.

    #### **Musqueam Reference Grammar by Wayne Suttles\.**
    
    ##### “Based on over half a century’s dedication to work with Coast Salish peoples and cultures, this grammatical description of hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓ provides extensive documentation of the language traditionally spoken by the Musqueam people, whose aboriginal territory includes much of the Fraser Delta and the city of Vancouver\. This language is one of the twenty\-three languages that belong to the Salish Family\. It is spoken in three distinct forms: Upriver Halq’eméylem or Halkomelem, by the Stó:lō of the Fraser Valley; Downriver hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓, of which Musqueam is the only surviving representative; and Island Hul’q’umin’um’, spoken by the Nanaimo and Cowichan of Vancouver Island\. Wayne Suttles worked with knowledgeable older people, eliciting traditional stories, personal narratives, and ethnographic accounts\. The grammar provides detailed coverage of phonology, morphology, and syntax, illustrated by numerous sentences selected for their cultural relevance, providing insight into traditional practices, social relations, and sense of humour\. There are also chapters on kinship and on space and time as well as five texts and appendices giving an index of grammatical elements, names of places and peoples, and the history of work on  hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓ and its related dialects\. It is written using the terms of traditional grammar as much as possible, without following a particular theoretical perspective\. This monumental work is perhaps the fullest account of any Salish language\.”– Provided by publisher\.

    #### **People Among the People: The Public Art of Susan Point by Robert D\. Watt\.**
    
    ##### “Susan Point’s unique artworks have been credited with almost single\-handedly reviving the traditional Coast Salish art style\. Once nearly lost to the effects of colonization, the crescents, wedges, and human and animal forms characteristic of the art of First Nations peoples living around the Salish Sea can now be seen around the world, reinvigorated with modern materials and techniques, in her serigraphs and public art installations–and in the works of a new generation of artists that she’s inspired\. People Among the People beautifully displays the breadth of Susan Point’s public art, from cast\-iron manhole covers to massive carved cedar spindle whorls, installed in locations from Vancouver to Zurich\. Through extensive interviews and access to her archives, Robert D\. Watt tells the story of each piece, whether it’s the evolution from sketch to carving to casting, or the significance of the images and symbolism, which is informed by surviving traditional Salish works Point has studied and the Oral Traditions of her Musqueam family and elders\. In her long quest to re\-establish a Coast Salish footprint in Southwest British Columbia and the Pacific Northwest of the US, Point has received many honours, including the Order of Canada and the Audain Lifetime Achievement Award\. This gorgeous and illuminating book makes it clear they are all richly deserved\.”– Provided by publisher\.

    #### **Susan Point: Works on Paper by Dale Croes, Susan Point, and Gary Wyatt\.**
    
    ##### “”Through my artwork I have used a visual language to help carry the torch, a torch that was lit by my ancestors,” says Susan Point\. “Printmaking has been the flame of my torch\.” Susan Point is from Musqueam, a Coast Salish village near the mouth of the Fraser River that is now part of Vancouver, British Columbia\. She grew up surrounded by Coast Salish culture and its design forms fill her heart\. The river and the great Pacific Ocean offered a wealth of salmon, sea life, plants and animals, which fed the people and their stories\- and provided Susan with endless inspiration for her prints\. Point began making prints in 1981\. As her reputation as an artist grew, public art became a dominant part of her production, but she has never stopped making prints, releasing a few limited editions every year\. Most are serigraphs, but she has mastered most printmaking techniques\. The 169 works in *Susan Point: Works on Paper* offer a startling cross\-section of her techniques and subjects\. “I have used printmaking to create images of educational value, giving historical insight into the roots of my culture, the environment and current social issues,” Point explains\. “My prints are my fingerprints; they reveal my thoughts and feelings on paper\.”” — Provided by publisher\.

    #### **Contemporary Coast Salish Art edited by Rebecca Blanchard &amp; Nancy Davenport with Steven C\. Brown\.**

These First Nations artists traditionally made the words and ideas of their sacred teachings tangible by carving, weaving, and painting them on ceremonial and everyday objects\. As a testament to their cultural resilience, increasing numbers of contemporary Coast Salish artists have embraced the new materials that “progress” has bestowed\- glass, concrete, and steel – juxtaposing ancient images with modern materials\.     #### **Decolonizing Research: Indigenous Storywork as Methodology edited by Jo\-ann Archibald Q’um Q’um Xiiem, Jenny Bol Jun Lee\-Morgan, and Jason De Santolo\.**
    
    ##### “From Oceania to North America, indigenous peoples have created storytelling traditions of incredible depth and diversity\. The term ‘indigenous storywork’ has come to encompass the sheer breadth of ways in which indigenous storytelling serves as a historical record, as a form of teaching and learning, and as an expression of indigenous culture and identity\. But such traditions have too often been relegated to the realm of myth and legend, recorded as fragmented distortions, or erased altogether\.
