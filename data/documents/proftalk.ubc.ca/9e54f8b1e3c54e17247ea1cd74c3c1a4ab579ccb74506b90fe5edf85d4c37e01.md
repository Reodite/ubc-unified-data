## [Dr\. Leigh VanHandel on How Music Works — and How We Understand It](https://www.spreaker.com/episode/dr-leigh-vanhandel-on-how-music-works-and-how-we-understand-it--75160084 "Dr. Leigh VanHandel on How Music Works — and How We Understand It")

Posted on Sep 15, 2026

In this conversation, I speak with Dr\. Leigh VanHandel, Associate Professor of Music Theory at UBC’s School of Music, whose research brings together music theory, music cognition, rhythm and meter, the relationship between music and language, and the ways we teach and learn music\. Leigh began university as a science major, intending to pursue genetic engineering, before returning to the music that had been such an important part of her life growing up\. Her path eventually led her to music theory and the emerging field of music cognition, where her interests in music, psychology, language and science could come together\. Her research asks how music works, how we perceive and process it, and how our experience shapes what we hear\. Her current work explores how we perceive and process tempo, what makes rhythmic patterns seem complex, and whether our native language can influence the way we perceive that complexity\. She is also the editor of \*The Routledge Companion to Music Theory Pedagogy\*, which received the Society for Music Theory’s Outstanding Multi\-Author Publication Award in 2022\. At the centre of her work is a deceptively simple question: how does music work, and how do we understand it?

## [Hrishabh Srivastava: Probing Dark Matter with Nature’s Magnifying Glass](https://www.spreaker.com/episode/hrishabh-srivastava-probing-dark-matter-with-nature-s-magnifying-glass--75017165 "Hrishabh Srivastava: Probing Dark Matter with Nature’s Magnifying Glass")

Posted on Sep 08, 2026

In this conversation, I speak with Hrishabh Srivastava, a PhD student in Astronomy at the University of British Columbia and a member of the Euclid Strong Lensing Science Working Group\. Originally trained in materials engineering and data science at the Indian Institute of Technology Madras, Hrishabh transitioned into astrophysics, where his research focuses on strong gravitational lensing, dark matter and the enormous quantities of astronomical data being produced by the Euclid Space Telescope\. Earlier this year, he was a finalist in UBC’s Three Minute Thesis competition with \*Probing Dark Matter with Nature’s Magnifying Glass\*\. His research explores how the gravity of a foreground galaxy bends and distorts light from a more distant galaxy—a consequence of Einstein’s theory of general relativity—and how these rare gravitational lenses can be used to investigate the distribution of otherwise invisible dark matter\. He is developing a method using Zernike polynomials to identify the geometric features of strong lenses in Euclid images, providing a simpler and more interpretable alternative to computationally intensive deep\-learning methods\. We discuss what dark matter is—and what we still do not know about it—how gravitational lensing allows astronomers to investigate something they cannot see directly, and how the Euclid mission could dramatically expand the number of known strong gravitational lenses\.

In this conversation, I speak with Jennifer Kramer\. She is Associate Professor of Anthropology at the University of British Columbia and Curator of the Pacific Northwest at UBC’s Museum of Anthropology\. For more than two decades, she has worked alongside Indigenous artists, knowledge keepers and communities, exploring how museums can become places of collaboration, cultural renewal and shared authority\. ## [In Conversation with Professor Philip Stamp: Superfluid Helium and Quantum Vacuum Tunnelling](https://www.spreaker.com/episode/in-conversation-with-professor-philip-stamp-superfluid-helium-and-quantum-vacuum-tunnelling--72873614 "In Conversation with Professor Philip Stamp: Superfluid Helium and Quantum Vacuum Tunnelling")

Posted on Jul 07, 2026

Professor Philip Stamp is Professor of Theoretical Physics at the University of British Columbia and Director of the Pacific Institute of Theoretical Physics, known as PITP, which he returned to Canada to establish in 2002\. Mostly raised in New Zealand and educated in the United Kingdom, Professor Stamp began his academic life in philosophy and literature before turning to theoretical physics\. He has since worked as a physicist in France, Spain, New Zealand, the Netherlands, Japan, Canada, and the United States\. His research explores many aspects of quantum mechanics, including macroscopic quantum phenomena, quantum tunnelling, quantum gravity, and quantum cosmology\. In this episode, we speak about his recent work with collaborators on superfluid helium and quantum vacuum tunnelling\. The research uses an ultra\-thin film of superfluid helium to examine the Schwinger effect, a famous prediction in quantum electrodynamics in which, under extreme conditions, the quantum vacuum may produce particle–antiparticle pairs\. In Professor Stamp’s work, this idea is approached through vortex and anti\-vortex pairs emerging in a quantum liquid\. Our conversation turns to the meaning of the quantum vacuum, the difficulty of observing the Schwinger effect directly, the relationship between theory and experiment in physics, and finally, the strange beauty of a universe that is, in Professor Stamp’s words, both magical and real\.
