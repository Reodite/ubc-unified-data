### Showing community\-driven coordination, collaborative project creates tailored heat and smoke guides\.

[Collage of infographic posters overlaying a picture of the mural on the side of Nora Hendrix Place\.](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/09/Hogans-Alley-Infographics-Project-hires-940x421.jpg)

Photo illustration showing student infographics in foreground with Nora Hendrix Place in the background\.

People in the Downtown Eastside often express frustration when they hear the word “research\.” Perhaps more so when it comes to the deadly reality of climate change\. After the 2021 heat dome disproportionately impacted communities like the Downtown Eastside, it can feel to some like yet another report is only as good as a fan you can fold it into\.

So, when Matt Hume heard about the [Climate Resilience Roadmap for Non\-Profits](https://www.sfu.ca/content/dam/sfu/ceri/images/Archive/Publications/climate-road-map-final.pdf), an action\-oriented set of recommendations that Simon Fraser University’s community\-engaged research initiative, CERi, developed with local non\-profits, the Student Research and Learning Coordinator had an idea to bring one of the recommendations to life that built off two other innovative Learning Exchange initiatives and a strong connection with a local housing provider\.

“Every spring the [UBC Urban Ethnographic Field school](https://uefs.arts.ubc.ca/) hosts their class at the Learning Exchange,” said Matt\. “It’s an amazing place\-based, six\-week, six\-credit course that the departments of Sociology and Anthropology have been offering for 16 years now, 10 of which at the Learning Exchange\.”

The course pairs students with a variety of community organizations to connect with and learn from community perspectives while exploring the complexities and nuances of community\-driven research\. In addition to providing the space for the classes, the Learning Exchange team helps connect the course with community partners\.

“One of the recommendations in the climate resilience roadmap is to develop educational and outreach materials that are specific to the Downtown Eastside,” said Matt\. “And we’re also involved in the UBC SHARE student infographics project, which just finished an extensive project evaluation that also received feedback that both individuals and organizations wanted infographics more aligned to their specific contexts\.”

Led by Dr\. Evan Mauro and the late Dr\. Kirby Mania, SHARE \(previously the [MRAi Kx Project](https://learningexchange.ubc.ca/community-based-research/making-research-accessible-initiative/)\) teaches students in how to develop accessible ways—like infographics, videos, and interactive websites—to share research related to the Downtown Eastside\. SHARE expanded to five courses in UBC’s Coordinated Arts Program and UBC iSchool this year and completed the comprehensive project evaluation with support from the UBC Teaching and Learning Enhancement Fund\.

“There was a lightbulb moment when reading the climate roadmap,” said Matt\. “We could draw upon some of the SHARE infographic resources to support SFU’s roadmap recommendations\. Plus, a student librarian working with SHARE had developed an infographics how\-to guide that we could easily adapt\. With the UEFS course always running just ahead of the hot summer months, the timing was perfect—we just needed to identify the right community partner to work with the students\.” said Matt\.

#### **Working with Hogan’s Alley**

This is where the Hogan’s Alley Society comes into the picture\.

The UBC Learning Exchange has been leading a collaborative project called [CLEAR](https://learningexchange.ubc.ca/community-based-research/climate-equity-action-resilience-clear/) with the UBC Sustainability Hub and local non\-profits to help move climate research into action\. One of those non\-profits is Hogan’s Alley, a Black\-led organization that operates a temporary modular social housing development called Nora Hendrix Place\. Nora Hendrix was a notable community builder in the historic Hogan’s Alley community and the grandmother of famed guitarist Jimi Hendrix\.

This year six students in the UBC Urban Ethnographic Field school were paired with Nora Hendrix Place residents\. Their mission? To tailor existing wildfire and smoke information from existing health authorities to the context of the Downtown Eastside\.  

They hosted focus groups with residents to review existing heat resources and information sources and then distilled the feedback into six new infographic\-style posters \(see gallery below\)\. The posters are now posted in Nora Hendrix Place and have been distributed widely in the Downtown Eastside\.

#### Experiential student learning

“One of my biggest takeaways was realizing how knowledgeable the people in the Downtown Eastside are about their own community and the issues they experience,” said Dania Othman, a UEFS student majoring in Sociology and minoring in Family Studies\.

“What surprised me most was realizing how different reality can be from what I expected\. I had assumed that some of the resources and supports available during extreme heat would be more accessible to residents, but the conversations showed me that there can be a significant gap between what is planned or promised and what people actually experience\. I was also surprised by how knowledgeable residents were about these issues and how clearly they could identify what their community needs\.”

Another student, Elias Irving, echoed Dania’s sentiments\. Elias, who said he took UEFS to see how some of the theories he was learning in Geography worked in practice, gave the example that people were often directed to cooling centres to avoid the heat and smoke but that many in the Downtown Eastside have mobility challenges and wouldn’t be able to get to those locations easily\.

“Everyone I talked to was extremely different and it became clear to me that the way people outside of the neighborhood treat the people inside as a monolith is deeply problematic and will hinder any sort of policy\-making or citywide action that actually takes the diversity of needs and desires in the neighborhood into account,” he said\.

“I think the biggest learning for me was I realized there are stories you hear about the neighbourhood and they are told by people who are not living in the neighbourhood,” he said\.

Elias also reflected on the limitations of the project, saying that he felt a lot of pressure as the one responsible for selecting and developing the information, preferring a longer and more in\-depth engagement period to work with the residents of Nora Hendrix Place\.

“This is something we often hear students say near the end of their experiences,” said Matt, reflecting on the limitations of certain university/community collaborations\. “Navigating the tensions between academic and community timelines is an ever\-present challenge of community engagement work, and a key learning for students as they look back on their time in community\.”
