### Several colleagues published books recently about the Downtown Eastside, Chinatown, experiential education, and more\. Have a look\! 

By Nayeli Martin del Campo Esquivez

April 27, 2025

In the spirit of two\-way learning between UBC and the Downtown Eastside, here is a short list of books recently published by our friends, colleagues, and partners that relate to community\-university engagement work, UBC, Chinatown, and/or Vancouver’s Downtown Eastside\.

#### [***Reconciling: A Lifelong Struggle to Belong***](https://ecwpress.com/products/reconciling)

**By Larry Grant in conversation with Scott Steedman**

“How come we don’t go with our cousins?” Elder Larry Grant asked when he was a child\. “My mom said, ‘Because the government says you are different\. You are not an Indian; you are a Chinese kid\. And they don’t have Chinese kids in residential schools\.’ So how am I different?” 

This is how Larry Grant describes part of his childhood growing up the son of a Chinese father and Musqueam mother in his new book *Reconciling: Lifelong Struggle to Belong,* a book in which Larry is reconciling the part of himself raised in Vancouver’s Chinatown, a short block away from where the UBC Learning Exchange now stands, and the part raised on what Canadian law calls “Musqueam Indian Reserve \#2,” which sits adjacent to the main UBC campus\. It’s also a story about reconciling with Canada’s past\.      

Larry Grant is an adjunct professor and Elder\-in\-Residence at UBC\. He is an educator of the hən̓q̓əmin̓əm̓ language and is recognized for his work to share Musqueam tradition and history\. The book is in the form of a conversation with Scott Steedman, a senior lecturer in the Simon Fraser University Publishing program and a writer and editor in order “to create a meaningful dialogue and understanding between all people, including those who have been colonized\.”

#### [***Juggling Rhythms: Working\-Student Life in the 21st Century***](https://brill.com/display/title/72365)

**By Alison Taylor** 

[Image](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/03/Screenshot-2026-03-20-124250-1.png)

With rising costs of living and education, many undergraduate students in Canada must work while studying\. This reality led Dr\. Alison Taylor, a UBC professor in the Department of Educational Studies and a long\-time collaborator with the Learning Exchange, to study how students juggle work and school, learning from 57 students between 2020 and 2022 through focus group interviews, life maps, audio diaries and one\-on\-one interviews\. Her book, *Juggling Rhythms,* uses circus arts metaphors—like juggling, high\-wire walking, and sword swallowing—to describe the day\-to\-day survival strategies students rely on as they prepare for a volatile labour market in the aftermath of COVID\-19\. It argues that a paradigm shift is needed and offers recommendations to policymakers, educators, and employers\.    

Read Dr\. Taylor’s summary here in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/to-survive-todays-economy-university-students-are-using-circus-like-tactics-264839)

#### [***Critical Futures: Community\-Engaged Research in a Time of Crisis and Social Transformation***](https://utppublishing.com/doi/book/10.3138/9781487550202)

*Critical Futures* is a collection of essays by scholars, community researchers, and activists that explore decolonial approaches to community\-based research that transcend traditional academic hierarchies\. Combining theoretical and practical perspectives, the book offers new approaches to the relationship between research and community, with the goal of opening new pathways toward a more just and sustainable future\. It’s edited by Stuart R\. Poyntz, a professor and director of SFU’s Community\-Engaged Research Centre Initiative \(SFU CERi\), Am Johal,   CERi co\-director, and Kari Grain, a research associate at SFU CERi and lecturer in the Department of Educational Studies \(EDST\) at UBC who sometimes hosts her UBC graduate course at the UBC Learning Exchange\.

#### [***Chinatown Vancouver: An Illustrated History***](https://donnaseto.ca/)  

**By Donna Seto** 

[Image](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/03/Screenshot-2026-03-20-125715.png)

Every place in Vancouver has its own story, but for years the vibrant, multi\-layered story of Chinatown has been ignored\.

During COVID\-19, Donna Seto, who works at UBC and is a “[writer, self\-taught artist, and occasional academic](https://houseofanansi.com/products/chinatown-vancouver?srsltid=AfmBOoo9a-xBlB1N1dcPv8VeXUIHeweOw0P7ZTEnMrMDSI4S9-cnJQ3F),” decided to start a new creative path\. She started drawing and painting, discovering it is never too late to try something new—and to explore a place that was dear to her\. The result was her book: “*Chinatown Vancouver: An Illustrated History\.”* 

Through photographs and illustrations, Seto tells the story of one of the first neighborhoods in Vancouver and opens a gateway to the past to reflect on the history and stories of this place\.        

“This book brings together illustrations, stories and interviews with the community\. It is an attempt to fill part of that gap\. It is not the end of the conversation; it is just the beginning,” Seto said at an [event](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xw4V5UkQjoE&amp;embeds_referring_euri=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.bing.com%2F&amp;embeds_referring_origin=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.bing.com&amp;source_ve_path=MjM4NTE) at the Vancouver Public Library\. “I am hoping this book helps to spark a lot of different conversations about this neighborhood, but also our identities as people in Vancouver and our identities as Canadians\.”  

Like Larry Grant’s book, “*Chinatown Vancouver: An Illustrated History”* is an invitation to get to know the past as a way toward a better future together\.
