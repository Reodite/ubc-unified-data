### Organized by the CLEAR project, this was the second in a series of forums focused on climate mitigation in the Downtown Eastside\. Here are some themes that emerged\.

February 26, 2026

[Image](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/02/photo_4992457985191775053_y-11-940x705.jpg)

Provincial ministers, community leaders, researchers, students, and many more came together on Nov\. 21 at 312 Main Street to share ideas and discuss best next steps to prepare for the next heat event in the Downtown Eastside\.

Organized by the [CLEAR project](https://learningexchange.ubc.ca/community-based-research/climate-equity-action-resilience-clear/), the day included five rounds of lightning talks from researchers, community members, as well as policy makers like MLA Amshen – Joan Phillip and MLA Hon\. Christine Boyle, minister of housing and municipal affairs\.

Read some of the highlights below\.

#### **1\. ‘It’s too hot to think small’**

Staying cool is a fundamental human right\. Ensuring people can do so is complicated\. It requires an in\-depth, systemic understanding and multi\-pronged responses rather than disconnected, one\-off changes\.

This was the gist of Dr\. Liv Yoon’s presentation, the first of the day\.

Dr\. Yoon is an assistant professor in the UBC School of Kinesiology who led a research project called [Too Hot to Think Small: The Case for a Right to Cool in British Columbia](https://climatejustice.ubc.ca/projects-and-partnerships/too-hot-to-think-small-the-case-for-a-right-to-cool-in-british-columbia/)\. The report argues that some of the dominant assumptions in the wake of the deadly 2021 heat dome—the power of one\-off technological interventions like free air conditioning units, not seeing climate risks as part of the housing crisis, or focusing solely on physiological risks and not socio\-economic risks—may leave communities like the Downtown Eastside ill prepared\. To become truly resilient, we must think bigger\.

“As such,” the report says, “the Right to Cool must uphold an expansive and comprehensive vision: one that connects heat\-related policy to the pressing hu­man rights struggles of our time, namely: housing justice, labour rights, disability justice, poverty eradication, and social equity\. Rights\-based solu­tions must work on multiple fronts should we hope to promote systemic change\.”

As Dr\. Yoon said at the event, the best way to build climate resilience is to make sure people have appropriate housing, make sure landlords can’t block needed improvements or renovict people when making improvements, make sure workers are protected, make sure cooling measures are affordable, and make sure to collect disaggregated data to see clearly which groups are most at risk\.

#### 2\. **Heat mitigation is essential not extra**

In the next presentation, Amanda Burrows, executive director of First United, picked up on the theme that housing and climate risks are inextricably linked\. She explained how this informed the design of First United’s housing redevelopment at 320 East Hastings St\., highlighting how after the heat dome in 2021 they added plans for a state\-of\-the\-art HVAC system to make their first floor a cooling centre during times of extreme heat, and their air circulation and filtration in the community spaces in their first four floors can act as a refuge from wildfire smoke\.

[Image](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/02/Tanya_Fader_2.jpg)

Tanya Fader, interim director of programs at the PHS Community Services Society\.

Later in the day, Tanya Fader, interim director of programs at the PHS Community Services Society, which provides housing and other services, expanded on the theme by describing the extraordinary effort required to support residents during the heat dome\. Tanya described how PHS instituted regular wellness checks, put in extra air conditioners, scoured the city for extra water and sports drinks to keep people hydrated\.

Both Amanda and Tanya described how it can be a struggle to convince funders that climate mitigation strategies are now an essential budgetary item for non\-profits\.

“When the heat dome hit,” said Tanya, “I had to take the risk of spending money \[we didn’t have\] or people would die\. We didn’t have any core funding for extreme events,” noting that earthquakes are approved expense line items for non\-profit budgets but not other extreme weather events\.

[Image](https://learningexchangeaug2018.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/02/Joan_Phillip_3.jpg)

MLA Amshen – Joan Phillip addresses the event\.

In break\-out sessions later in the day, some people mentioned they thought funders needed to be part of these discussions\. As one person said, “There is a disconnect between those who are here and those who can put money on the table who can commit and be accountable\.”

Tanya’s comments were part of a presentation on a report called [**Climate Resilience Roadmap for Non\-Profits: From Crisis to Collective Power**](https://www.sfu.ca/ceri/publications1/handbooks---toolkits/climate-resilience-roadmap/climate-resilience-roadmap.html) released with Sean Condon from Vancity Community Foundation and Tara Mahoney from the SFU’s Community Engaged Research Initiative \(CERi\)\.

#### 3\. **The Downtown Eastside is unique; emergency responses need to understand this**

As Dr\. Yoon’s presentation highlighted, understanding unique contexts is essential\. A good example came out of a presentation by the Eastside Illicit Drinkers Group for Education \(EIDGE\)\.

EIDGE is a peer\-led advocacy group of Downtown Eastside residents who use illicit alcohol\. They are working with Amber Grant, a SSHRC\-funded postdoctoral research fellow in UBC’s Faculty of Forestry &amp; Environmental Stewardship, to look at how people who drink are treated as criminals in areas that could protect them from the heat, like bus shelters and under trees in parks\.

Members of the group described how police often pour out alcohol when they are drinking in public spaces, but without the alcohol, they could suffer dangerous—even fatal—withdrawal symptoms, and without the covered outdoor spaces, they could suffer from dangerous effects of extreme heat\. They advocated for accessible indoor cooling and warming spaces for drinkers, more trees in the Downtown Eastside, reforming bylaws that govern park usage, and affordable housing for all\.

Other people at the event advocated for practical emergency measures, like emergency kits in housing and community facilities\. The Too Hot to Think Small report recommended installing thermometers in apartments to monitor rising heat levels, and free transit so people could get to cooling \(or warming\) facilities more easily\.

A number of people also mentioned the need for community climate response teams, including Tara Mahoney, from the SFU’s CERi presenting on the [**Climate Resilience Roadmap for Non\-Profits: From Crisis to Collective Power**](https://www.sfu.ca/ceri/publications1/handbooks---toolkits/climate-resilience-roadmap/climate-resilience-roadmap.html) report\.

#### **4\. ‘Less talk, more do’**

In break\-out groups after the talks, a number of people talked about the urgency of the situation\.

 “We have some of the answers\. Let’s do them,” adding that it’s time for substantial action\.

Another participant noted that what’s missing now is not the ideas but the political will and financial capital to make the ideas happen\.

As for how to organize and marshal existing \(if scarce\) resources democratically, some participants suggested a collaboratively run and community\-led climate hub\.

A number of people also mentioned the need for community climate response teams, including Tara Mahoney, from the SFU’s CERi presenting on the [**Climate Resilience Roadmap for Non\-Profits: From Crisis to Collective Power**](https://www.sfu.ca/ceri/publications1/handbooks---toolkits/climate-resilience-roadmap/climate-resilience-roadmap.html) report\.
