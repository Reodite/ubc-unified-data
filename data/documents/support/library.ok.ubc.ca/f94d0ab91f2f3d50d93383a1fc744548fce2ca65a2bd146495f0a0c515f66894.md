[Orange Shirt Day](https://orangeshirtday.org/) is a day to remember and honour the Survivors and Victims of the residential school system in Canada\. Secwepemc author and activist Phyllis Webstad is the Founder and Ambassador of the Orange Shirt Society and speaks of her first day attending residential school, where her new orange shirt was taken by school officials\. 

While wearing an orange shirt in solidarity with Indigenous people can be a meaningful personal action, many Indigenous leaders and academics speak of the importance of truth before reconciliation\. September 30 provides an excellent opportunity to learn more about the impacts of discriminatory colonial policies like residential schools on Indigenous communities\. It is equally important to recognize the continuing emotional burden that teaching this history places on Indigenous people\. September 30 offers an opportunity to consider our own responsibilities in reconciliation\. 

Here are a few ways that you can take part at UBCO Library\. 

[**Legacy of Hope Foundation Exhibition: Escaping Residential Schools**](https://events.ok.ubc.ca/event/legacy-of-hope-exhibition-escaping-residential-schools/2026-10-02/)  
Explore the exhibit on the main floor of UBCO Library, which gives a voice to those who escaped Residential Schools and honours those who died while trying to escape by using personal accounts from family members\. This exhibition is on until October 2\.

[**Orange Shirt Day crafting event \| September 28 and 29**](https://events.ok.ubc.ca/series/orange-shirt-day/)  
Stop by the Sawchuk Family Theatre from 11 am to 1 pm to craft an orange paper shirt, learn more about Orange Shirt Day, and reflect on Truth and Reconciliation\. The paper shirts completed at this event will be displayed around campus to help raise awareness about Orange Shirt Day and the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, which both occur on September 30\.

Wearing or displaying Orange Shirts is a way to honour Survivors of the residential school system and remember those children who did not return home\. Although an orange shirt can be a significant show of solidarity, students, staff, and faculty are encouraged to walk their learning forward by attending local events, spreading awareness, and taking time for personal reflection and self\-care\. See the [UBC Okanagan Orange Shirt Day webpage](https://ok.ubc.ca/about/indigenous-engagement/orange-shirt-day/) for information about on\-campus and community events\.

[Karina at the farmers' market with her display](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/09/Karina-for-Sharron-Simpson-web.png)

Karina Jarzecki at the Kelowna Farmers’ Market

This year, UBC Okanagan PhD Biology student Karina Jarzecki received funding through the [Sharron Simpson Family Community Engagement Endowment Fund](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/about/opportunities/sharron-simpson-family-community-engagement-endowment-fund/) \(SSFCEEF\) for her research project on the agricultural history of the Okanagan Valley\. Her project, *Digging Up Our Roots,* moves beyond wine grapes and tree fruits to recover a fuller, more comprehensive picture of agriculture in the Okanagan\. 

The funding award provided Jarzecki with a chance to explore and unpack the agricultural heritage in the Okanagan Valley\. Jarzecki states that she wanted to collect all the information that should could find, “ranging from the Indigenous food systems that were present prior to colonization, through settler agriculture as the Okanagan Valley saw increased settlement, and touching on the issues our agriculture is facing today\.”  

Jarzecki admits that as a Biology PhD student, she did not have experience with archival research coming into this project\. “I am very grateful to the amazing team at the UBC Okanagan Library,” she states\. Their support allowed Jarzecki to develop archival research skills\. “Everyone’s support in the beginning was priceless\.” 

With a goal of making her research accessible, Jarzecki showcased her findings through [an enormous agricultural infographic banner](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/09/Diggingupoourroots-1.jpg) illustrated by Jarzecki herself\. This banner was displayed along with seed packets from the UBCO Seed Library at a booth at the Kelowna Farmers’ and Crafters’ Market\. connecting historical agriculture with present\-day growing\.  

Children often came up to the booth, intrigued about getting some free seeds to grow\. “I was really touched by their enthusiasm and excitement,” she says\.  

[Karina Jarzecki with Poster](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/09/Karina-for-Sharron-Simpson-web-1-1.png)

Jarzecki believes that “the Kelowna Farmers Market was an ideal venue, as customers of the market already care deeply about our local food system\.” Many folks ended up leaving the booth with sachets of seeds or book recommendations\. “It was amazing to speak with a wide range of folks who were kind enough to share Okanagan agricultural history with me,” says Jarzecki, “even Sharron Simpson herself\!”  

While the *Digging Up Our Roots* project has come to an end, Jarzecki will continue researching agricultural history, which she says is especially enriching since she is currently researching potential crops for the Okanagan in 2050\.  

The [Sharron Simpson Family Community Engagement Endowment Fund](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/about/opportunities/sharron-simpson-family-community-engagement-endowment-fund/) \(SSFCEEF\) supports experiential learning opportunities at UBC Okanagan Library that establish and enhance impactful university\-community relationships with an emphasis on raising awareness of public history and regional identity\. Successful applicants work with community partners and university mentors on their proposed project while gaining valuable skills in collaboration, secondary research, project management, and knowledge dissemination\.

[https://students\.ok\.ubc\.ca/academic\-success/learning\-hub/faculty\-resources/academic\-integrity/](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/)[Rina Garcia Chua standing in the Library stacks on the second floor](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/08/RGC2-1-768x1152.png)

[The Academic Integrity Matters \(AIM\) program](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/) provides UBCO students with educational support to learn and apply best practices for academic integrity\. The program offers [four distinct Canvas courses](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/#descriptions) to help address specific issues of academic integrity at UBC Okanagan\.  

With the rise of generative AI \(GenAI\) tools being used in universities across the nation, AIM developed a [Generative Artificial Intelligence Canvas modules](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/#descriptions) to address the relationship between GenAI and coursework at UBCO, including ethical considerations, best practices, and when the use of GenAI contradicts the values of academic integrity\. 

This new course has garnered media attention from news outlets like CBC and Global news\. This attention is not surprising to Rina Garcia Chua, Academic Communication and Integrity Manager\. 

[view CBC Article](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ai-cheating-postsecondary-9.7269674) 

“At UBC Okanagan, whether GenAI can be used or not is a program or instructor level decision\. So what is acceptable in one course may not be acceptable in another\.” This uncertainty can result in a lot of confusion for both instructors and students\.

She includes that many of the students she sees through the AIM program are “overwhelmed, confused, or stressed about whether they can or cannot use Generative AI for\.” Added to this, many students feel too embarrassed to ask their instructors questions about using GenAI tools\. 

Although AIM’s GenAI modules do not themselves set out rules about the use of GenAI, they provide best practices and encourage students to advocate for themselves and ask questions if the GenAI policies set by their instructors are unclear\. 

AIM’s [Generative Artificial Intelligence course](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/#descriptions) is available for faculty to [integrate into their course Canvas to be assigned for credit](https://students.ok.ubc.ca/academic-success/learning-hub/faculty-resources/academic-integrity/#assign-course) or can be completed by students independently\. Once students complete the course, they will receive proof of completion certificates and gain confidence in their understanding of best practices when it comes to GenAI\.  

Whether or not a student decides to use GenAI in their coursework will depend on the guidance provided by their instructor\. Either way, Rina Garcia Chua leaves us with an important consideration underlying these resources: “If the internet breaks down tomorrow, or AI suddenly disappears, how can we make sure people can do what they learned to do with their own skills and innate creativity?” 

This course will be updated to meet the evolving needs of students and instructors\. Questions or concerns about the course can be sent to [aim\.ok@ubc\.ca](mailto:aim.ok@ubc.ca)\.

### Trail Times Collection

[Trail Time newspaper](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/07/Trail-Daily-Times_1971-05-06-1.jpg-768x1107.png)

First called *Trail Creek News*, *The Trail Times* has provided local and international news coverage to the city of Trail, BC since 1895–just a year after the town was first settled\.

The Trail Museum &amp; Archives worked with MicroCom \(recently acquired by Consentia\) to digitize 15,676 newspaper issues spanning from 1896 to 2022\. All issues up until 2022 are now openly accessible through the BCRDH\.  

Covering Trail’s extensive development, labour issues, and world events, the digitization of this historic newspaper provides a snapshot into the life and times of the population of Trail, BC and the West Kootenay region\.  

[Trail Museum &amp; Archives](https://trail.ca/en/play/trail-museum-archives.aspx) is grateful to Black Press Media for granting permission to make these items available and for the generous support of the following funders, who ensured this project was completed in one phase:

[Jessica Lowry with client](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/06/Untitled-design-83.png)

Attending university as an international graduate student or post\-doctoral researcher can be challenging in many ways\. On top of the rigorous demands of academic programs and research, adjusting to cultural norms and linguistic expectations both inside and outside of the lab or classroom can prove difficult\.  

Through the English Language Development program, the [Centre for Scholarly Communication \(CSC\)](https://csc.ok.ubc.ca/) aims to address these challenges to help researchers transition into the UBC Okanagan research community with ease and confidence\.  

Jessica Lowry, Academic Communication Consultant in the CSC, is one member of the team who facilitates this program by offering free English language development support through [one\-on\-one consultations](https://csc.ok.ubc.ca/consultations/writing-and-academic-communication-consultation/) and group conversation circles\. These wrap\-around supports in the CSC help researchers develop strategies and skills to improve their listening, speaking, reading, and writing skills in English\.  

“There aren’t always opportunities to practice conversational English in the research laboratory or classroom,” states Jessica\. And while developing the skills and confidence to communicate with an academic audience is important, there are additional language skills an international researcher might want to develop to feel comfortable in their new environment\. During one\-on\-one English Language Development sessions, researchers can practice academic and conversational English and set individualized goals for their development\. These meetings are customized to suit each researcher’s needs: whether they want to have more conversations in English or learn terminology relevant to a specific field, topic, or situation\.

“Some want to work on presentations, while others want to go over ordering their coffee or asking someone if they’d like to meet up,” Jessica describes\. All of these elements are relevant and important for a wholistic and fulfilling university career\. 

“Our top goal is for students to develop confidence in speaking English,” Jessica says\. “And that comes through practice\.”

Along with the one\-on\-one sessions, researchers can also join the [English Language Conversation Circle](https://events.ok.ubc.ca/series/english-language-conversation-circle/), which Jessica describes as a “low\-risk space to just chat and practice English with other language learners\.”

It’s important that the Centre for Scholarly Communication be a connection point on campus\. “Researchers might start coming to us for English language development to help build confidence communicating in English and then return for support with writing publications, writing their thesis, and more\. It’s important to build relationships first\.”

### Meet some of the participants\! 

[Shadi in red coat with flowers in her hair standing infront of stand up paddle boards](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/06/20250818132923971_save-1-scaled-e1780426987800-768x566.jpg)

**Shadi’s Experience \| Master of Education** 

Shadi had lots of options for her graduate studies\. She chose UBC O because of its strong academic standing and the positive interactions that she had with her supervisor\.  

Back in Iran, Shadi was used to being a top student, but as an international student attending a graduate program in a new country, she was feeling less confident\.  

“When I started my master’s degree, I realized that I needed more practice speaking and expressing my ideas in English within academic contexts\.” 

Shadi first learned about the English Language Development program at a n  tabling event on campus\. 

“I needed a space for myself where I could think aloud and respond to questions my supervisor might ask me, or the questions in the classroom when we are asked to share our ideas\.” 

She decided to attend some one\-on\-one sessions to help her regain confidence\. 

“In every session we picked a topic in advance\. Sometimes it was relevant to student life or to teaching\. I really loved this freedom to pick a topic, read about it in advance, get prepared, and then come to the meeting\.” 

The one\-on\-one sessions provided Shadi with a safe space to practice her academic and everyday communication\.  

“My consultant helped me connect my ideas and explain my perspective more confidently\.  Whenever I had presentations or discussions, we would practice them first in our meetings\. Then I felt much more relaxed and comfortable in class because I had the experience to talk to someone beforehand\.” 

---

**Yuan’s Experience  \| Master of Health and Exercise Science**  

Yuan was attracted to UBC Okanagan because of the smaller campus size and the possibility for a closer connection with fellow graduate students and professors\. She hoped that being part of a smaller campus would allow her to better understand the life and culture of the local community\.  

In China, Yuan would read English articles but never spoke English in her daily life\. She admits that not having many opportunities to practice English made her a little scared to come to UBC Okanagan\.  

“I was nervous about coming across one of my classmates or a professor — what should I say, what should I talk about?”  

When her friend joined the English language development program, she decided to join as well\. 

“Now, I feel I’m improving,” she says\. “The program provides a comfortable environment\. I can speak English with more confidence\. I don’t mind my mistakes\. I can comfortably chat with other people in English\.” 

Yuan credits the English language program with helping her to feel more at ease interacting with others on campus\. “The best way you can improve your language is to practice and use it without stress\.”  

When asked if Yuan had any advice for other English language learners, she said that confidence and being open\-minded is helpful\. “And don’t be too hard on yourself\. Accept that improving language takes time\.”

After accessing English Language Development support and explore language learning strategies at home, like watching English television, Ian says that he has more confidence\.

Many will recognize Rose as the friendly face at the Library Service Desk most mornings\. After 48 years of service, Rose Cresswell is retiring from UBC Okanagan Library\. 

[Image](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/05/Rose_Sized.jpg)

Rose Cresswell, Library Services Assistant

It’s impossible to imagine UBCO Library without Rose\. This is because Rose has been the face and heart of this library since before the UBCO campus opened in 2005\.  

Before UBC Okanagan opened, Rose worked in this library, which was then a part of Okanagan University College \(OUC\) North Kelowna campus\. Before OUC, Rose worked at Okanagan College on KLO, which later branched out to the North Kelowna campus\. And so, the history of UBCO Library is very much aligned with Rose’s presence in it\. 

[Rose Cresswell and other Library employees in 2006](https://library.ok.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/116/2026/05/Rose2006.jpg)

Rose Cresswell \(in green on the right and in the second row\) along with other UBCO Library employees in 2006

UBCO Library has been fortunate to have Rose as its steadfast caretaker since the beginning\. To say that she will be missed is an understatement\. Her welcoming presence, exemplary work ethic, and her kindness will remain qualities that we can all continue to strive for\. 

Rose, thank you for your service to UBC Okanagan Library\. Enjoy your retirement\! 

Come by the library and say farewell to Rose\. Her last morning is Friday, May 29\.
