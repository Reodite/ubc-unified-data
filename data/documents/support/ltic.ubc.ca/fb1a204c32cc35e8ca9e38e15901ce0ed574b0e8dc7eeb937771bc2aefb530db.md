We want to help you build applications that integrate with UBC’s learning technology systems\. However, accessing student data — even your own — requires institutional oversight because of BC privacy law\.

## The short version

To move forward, you’ll need a faculty sponsor who can serve as the project owner for a Privacy Impact Assessment\. Design your application to keep data on the user’s device, avoid third\-party services, and request only the access you truly need\. These choices make the approval process significantly simpler\.

Once you have a sponsor and a clear understanding of your data flows, reach out to us\. We’ll help you navigate the process from there\.

If you’re unsure about anything, and in general \(to help prevent frustration\), we recommend you fill in our [idea intake form](https://ltic-2026.sites.olt.ubc.ca/i-have-an-idea/) and we can help ensure you’re spending your time and effort on the right things\.

The details matter, and the rest of this guide explains why and how to get it right\.

## **1\. **Fostering Student\-led Innovation****

If you’re a UBC student who has built, or wants to build, an application that works with Canvas or other tools in the [Learning Technology Ecosystem](https://lthub.ubc.ca/initiatives/learning-technology-decisions/), and wants to know how to get approved to do that, this post is for you\.

We regularly receive messages from students who have created something useful, fun, or both: study dashboards, assignment trackers, end\-of\-term summaries, and everything in between\. This kind of initiative is exactly what we want to encourage\. Students often understand their own learning experience in ways that lead to creative, practical solutions\.

At the same time, connecting your application to systems like Canvas means working with student data, and UBC has processes in place to ensure that data is handled responsibly\. These processes exist to protect you and your fellow students\.

This post walks you through what’s involved\. Our goal is to help you understand the landscape, design your project in a way that makes approval more straightforward, and connect you with the right people along the way\.

## ****2\. Why Student Data Access Requires Institutional Oversight****

When you request access to Canvas data, you’re not just accessing your own information\. Even a “read\-only” application that displays a student’s activity back to them is, from the university’s perspective, a system that handles personal information—and that comes with legal obligations\.

In British Columbia, public institutions like UBC are governed by the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act \(FIPPA\)\. This legislation sets out rules for how personal information can be collected, used, stored, and disclosed\. UBC doesn’t have discretion here; compliance is a legal requirement\.

To ensure we meet these obligations, UBC uses a process called a Privacy Impact Assessment, or PIA\. A PIA evaluates how personal information flows through a system: what data is collected, where it goes, who can access it, how long it’s retained, and what protections are in place\. The goal is to identify and address privacy risks before a system goes live, not after something goes wrong\.

This is why we can’t simply issue API credentials on request\. It’s not about doubting your intentions or your technical ability—it’s about ensuring that any system accessing student data has been reviewed and that someone with institutional accountability is responsible for it\.

The good news is that the PIA process is designed to be proportional\. A simple, well\-designed project with minimal data handling will face a lighter review than a complex system that stores data or sends it to third parties\. How you design your application matters, and we’ll cover that shortly\.

## ****3\. **The Role of a Faculty Sponsor******

UBC’s privacy processes require that any project handling student data has a “project owner” who is a UBC employee that takes responsibility for ensuring the project complies with FIPPA and university policy\. This person is accountable for the project through any Privacy Impact Assessment and for its ongoing operation\.

As a student, you can’t fill this role yourself\. This isn’t a reflection of your capabilities; it’s simply how institutional accountability works\. A faculty sponsor provides the oversight that allows your project to exist within UBC’s framework\.

A good faculty sponsor doesn’t need to be technical\. They need to understand what your project does, what data it accesses, and be willing to take on the responsibility of oversight\. This could be a professor in your department, a course instructor whose teaching relates to your project, an academic advisor, or a faculty member with an interest in learning technology\. The connection doesn’t need to be formal, what matters is that they’re willing to support your work\.

When approaching a potential sponsor, come prepared\. Have documentation ready that explains your project’s purpose, what data you need to access, how that data will be handled, and why you’ve designed it the way you have\. Be clear about what you’re asking of them: institutional oversight and accountability, not technical development\. Most faculty will appreciate that you’ve done your homework and aren’t asking them to build the thing themselves\.

The next section provides guidance you can share directly with a potential sponsor, so they understand what they’re agreeing to\.

## 4\. ****Guidance for Faculty Sponsors****

This section is written for faculty members who have been approached by a student seeking sponsorship\. Feel free to share it directly\.

### **What sponsorship means**

By agreeing to sponsor a student project, you become the “project owner” for the purposes of UBC’s Privacy Impact Assessment process\. This means you are responsible for ensuring the project complies with FIPPA and UBC Policies such as [UBC’s Information Security Standards](https://cio.ubc.ca/information-security/policy-standards-resources), and for accepting any risk treatment plan that results from the PIA review\.

This is an oversight role, not a development role\. You are not expected to build, host, or maintain the application\. Your responsibility is to understand what the project does, how it handles data, and to ensure it operates within the boundaries established during the privacy review\.

### **What you’ll need to do**

Your involvement will typically include reviewing the student’s documentation to ensure you understand the project’s purpose, architecture, and data flows; submitting a PIA Inquiry through the UBC Self\-Service Portal \(or reviewing one the student has prepared on your behalf\); responding to any follow\-up questions from UBC’s Risk Advisors; and approving the final PIA report if one is required\.

One important thing to note is that when the initial PIA Inquiry is submitted you will be asked to **complete a self\-assessment**\. Check your email once the PIA is submitted to find a link to the self\-assessment\.

If the project scope changes, for example, if the student wants to access additional data or introduce a third\-party service, you would need to notify the PIA team\.

### **What you’re not responsible for**

You are not expected to write code, provide technical support, or host infrastructure\. You are also not personally liable for the student’s work, provided the project operates as documented and within the scope of the approved PIA\. Your role is to provide institutional grounding and accountability, not to become the project’s developer \(unless you want to be\)\.

### **Questions to ask the student**

Before agreeing to sponsor a project, you may want to ask the student to explain what data the application accesses and why, where that data goes \(stored on a server, kept in the user’s browser, sent to a third party\), how long data is retained, what happens to the project and its data after the student graduates, and whether the student has reviewed UBC’s PIA process and designed the project with privacy in mind\.

A well\-prepared student will have clear answers to these questions\. If they don’t, it may be worth asking them to return once they’ve thought through their design more carefully\.

For more detail on the PIA process, see UBC’s Privacy Impact Assessment overview at privacymatters\.ubc\.ca\.

## 5\. Technical Recommendations\.

How you design your application has a direct impact on how complex the privacy review process will be\. The following recommendations will help you build something that is easier to approve and, in many cases, more respectful of your users’ privacy by design\.

### **Keep data on the user’s device**

The simplest way to reduce privacy risk is to never store student data on a server you control\. Instead, use the browser’s localStorage or sessionStorage to keep data on the user’s own device\. Your application can fetch data from the source system, process it client\-side, display the results, and never persist anything beyond the user’s machine\.

When data doesn’t leave the user’s device, the privacy calculus changes significantly\. There’s no server to secure, no database to protect, no question of what happens to the data if your project ends\. This pattern won’t suit every project, but if it suits yours, it will make the path forward much smoother\.

### **Avoid third\-party services**

If your application sends student data to an external service—a cloud database, an AI API, an analytics platform—that introduces new privacy considerations\. Each third party that receives student data would need to be assessed as part of the PIA process, and in many cases, that service would need to have undergone its own privacy review\.

This is often where straightforward projects become complicated\. If you’re considering using a third\-party service, ask yourself whether it’s truly necessary\. If you can achieve your goals without it, you’ll save yourself considerable time and complexity\.

If a third\-party service is essential to your project, be prepared to document exactly what data is sent, where that service stores its data \(and whether that’s within Canada\), and what the service’s own privacy and security commitments are\.

### **Request read\-only access with minimal scopes**

When you request API access to systems in the Learning Technology Ecosystem, you’ll typically specify which endpoints or scopes your application needs\. Request only what you actually require, and favour read\-only access wherever possible\.

For example, a Canvas application that can only read a student’s own assignment submissions is a different proposition from one that can access full course rosters or post on a user’s behalf\. The narrower your access, the smaller the risk surface, and the simpler the review\. The same principle applies to any system: if you only need to read data, don’t request write access\.

### **Don’t collect what you don’t need**

Even within the access you’re granted, be deliberate about what data you actually fetch and process\. If your application only needs certain fields to function, don’t pull down everything available “just in case\.”

**Less data means less to secure, less to explain in a PIA, and less that can go wrong\.**

### **Document your data flows**

Whatever design decisions you make, document them clearly\. A simple diagram showing what data enters your application, where it comes from, where it goes, and how long it stays there will be invaluable—both for your own clarity and for anyone reviewing your project\.

Good documentation signals that you’ve thought through your design\. It also makes the PIA process faster, because you won’t be reconstructing your architecture from memory when someone asks how your application works\.

## 6\. Getting Access to Data

Once you’ve designed your application with privacy in mind and identified a faculty sponsor, here’s what the process of getting access typically looks like\.

### **Prepare your documentation**

Before initiating any formal process, pull together a clear description of your project\. This should include the purpose of your application and the problem it solves, which systems you need to access and what data you’ll retrieve, how that data flows through your application \(a simple diagram is helpful\), where the data will be stored \(ideally: only on the user’s device\), whether any third\-party services are involved, how long data is retained, and what happens to data when a user stops using the application\.

This documentation serves multiple purposes\. It helps you clarify your own thinking, gives your faculty sponsor something concrete to review, and provides the foundation for any privacy assessment\.

### **Work with your sponsor to submit a PIA Inquiry**

Your faculty sponsor will submit a PIA Inquiry through UBC’s Self\-Service Portal\. This initial inquiry helps determine the level of review your project requires\. Not every project needs a full Privacy Impact Assessment—simpler projects with minimal data handling may proceed with a lighter\-touch review\.

You can prepare the inquiry on your sponsor’s behalf, but they will need to submit it and be listed as the project owner\. Be responsive during this stage; if the Risk Advisors have follow\-up questions, answering them promptly keeps things moving\.

### **Receive guidance on next steps**

Based on the inquiry, UBC’s privacy team will advise whether your project can proceed as described, requires modifications, or needs a more detailed assessment\. If changes are recommended, this is an opportunity to adjust your design before you’ve invested too much effort in a particular approach\.

For projects that require a full PIA, your sponsor will work with a Risk Advisor to document risks and establish a treatment plan\. Once complete, your sponsor will review and approve the final report\.

### **Request data access**

With privacy requirements addressed, you can proceed to request the technical access you need\. Depending on the system, this might involve requesting API credentials, a developer key, or another form of access\.

For systems in the Learning Technology Ecosystem, contact LTIC to discuss your specific needs\. We’ll work with you and your sponsor to provision access appropriate to your project’s scope\.

### **Consider starting with a pilot**

For projects intended to serve other students, it’s often wise to begin with a small pilot group, perhaps volunteers from a single course or program\. A limited rollout lets you test your application in practice, identify issues early, and demonstrate that it works as intended\. It can also simplify the privacy review, since a pilot with 20 users is a different scale of risk than a launch to the entire campus\.

## 8\. Setting Realistic Expectations

Not every project will have the same path forward\. Being realistic about what’s involved can help you decide how to proceed and where to invest your effort\.

### **Projects that are likely to move quickly**

If your application only accesses data about the authenticated user \(not other students\), stores data only in the user’s browser rather than on a server, does not send data to any third\-party services, requests read\-only access with a narrow scope, and you have a faculty sponsor willing to support the project, then you’re in a good position\. Projects like this involve less risk, which means less review\. The process may still take some time, but there are fewer obstacles to work through\.

### **Projects that will require more extensive review**

Some designs introduce complexity that requires closer examination\. If your application stores data on a server you control, sends data to third\-party services \(even for processing rather than storage\), aggregates data across multiple users, or requests write access or broad API scopes, expect the process to take longer\. None of these are necessarily dealbreakers, but each adds questions that need to be answered\. You’ll need thorough documentation, and your faculty sponsor will need to engage more actively with the PIA process\.

### **Projects that may not be feasible**

We want to be honest: some projects face significant barriers\. If you cannot find a faculty sponsor, the path forward is limited—there’s no mechanism for a student to be the project owner for a PIA\. If your design requires sending student data to a third\-party service that hasn’t been assessed and can’t be, that’s likely a stopping point\. And if the scope of what you’re proposing is essentially enterprise\-level including broad access across many systems, thousands of users, ongoing operational support, then it may not be realistic as a student project, even a well\-intentioned one\.

### **Timelines**

Privacy reviews take time, and that timeline isn’t entirely within our control\. A straightforward project with good documentation might move through the process in a few weeks\. A more complex project could take several months, particularly if it requires a full PIA with multiple rounds of review\.

If your project is tied to a specific deadline such as an end\-of\-term demo, a capstone presentation, a course requirement etc\. then factor this into your planning\. Starting early is always better than discovering late in the term that you’re waiting on a process that can’t be rushed\.

## 10\. FAQs

### **Can I test my application with just my own account before going through this process?**

In most cases, no\. Even accessing your own data through an API requires credentials that we can’t issue without some level of review\. That said, if you’re at a very early stage and want to explore whether your idea is technically feasible, reach out to us\. Depending on the system and what you’re trying to do, there may be options, such as working with documentation, sandbox environments, or sample data, that let you experiment before committing to a full project\.

### **What if I only want to access my own data, not anyone else’s?**

This does simplify things, but it doesn’t eliminate the need for oversight entirely\. API credentials that work for your account could, in principle, be misused or compromised to access other users’ data\. The review process is lighter for single\-user, self\-only applications, but some process is still required\.

### **What happens to my project after I graduate?**

This is an important question, and one your faculty sponsor will want to understand as well\. If you graduate and your application is still in use, someone needs to remain accountable for it\. Options include transitioning ownership to your faculty sponsor or another UBC employee, shutting the application down and ensuring any stored data is properly deleted, or handing the project to another student who can take over with their own sponsorship arrangement\. Plan for this from the start rather than leaving it unresolved\.

### **Can my application eventually be used by students at other institutions?**

Possibly, but that’s beyond the scope of what we can help with here\. Our process is designed to assess applications accessing UBC data for UBC users\. If you want to expand to other institutions, each would have its own privacy requirements and approval processes\. Design with portability in mind if this is a goal, but don’t assume approval at UBC guarantees approval elsewhere\.

### **What if I want to use an AI service like ChatGPT or Claude in my application?**

This falls under the guidance about third\-party services\. If you’re sending student data to an external AI service, that service becomes part of your data flow and needs to be assessed\. Many AI services store prompts, process data outside Canada, or have terms of service that create complications for institutional data\. This doesn’t mean it’s impossible, but it adds significant complexity\. If AI features are central to your project, be prepared to document exactly what data is sent and to demonstrate that the service meets UBC’s requirements, or consider whether the AI features can work with non\-sensitive data instead\.

### **How long does the whole process take?**

It varies\. A well\-documented project with minimal data handling and a responsive faculty sponsor might move through the process in a few weeks\. More complex projects, particularly those requiring a full PIA, can take several months\. The biggest factors are how clearly you’ve documented your project, how quickly you and your sponsor respond to follow\-up questions, and the current workload of UBC’s privacy team\. Starting early is the best way to avoid deadline pressure\.

### **I’ve seen other students with apps that connect to Canvas\. How did they do it?**

Some may have gone through this process\. Others may be using workarounds that don’t actually involve institutional API access\. For example, browser extensions that scrape data client\-side, or tools that ask users to manually export and upload their own data\. These approaches have their own limitations and risks\. If you want to build something that integrates properly with UBC systems and can be used with institutional support, this process is the path forward\.

### **What if my request is denied?**

If a PIA review identifies risks that can’t be adequately addressed, your project may not be approved as designed\. This isn’t necessarily the end\. Often there are modifications that would make the project viable\. We’d rather help you redesign than simply say no\. If your project genuinely can’t proceed, we’ll try to be clear about why so you can make informed decisions about whether to pursue a different approach\.
