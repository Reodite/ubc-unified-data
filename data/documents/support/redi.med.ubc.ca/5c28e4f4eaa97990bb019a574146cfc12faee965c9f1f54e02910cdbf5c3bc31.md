# Transforming Health for Everyone: Strengthening Medical Curriculum in an Era of Truth, Reconciliation &amp; Inclusion

Summary and reflection prompts for the [It Starts with Us](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/transforming-health-for-everyone-strengthening-medical-curriculum-in-an-era-of-truth-reconciliation-and-inclusion/) series webinar hosted on November 26th, 2025\. **Panelists and moderators:** Derek K\. Thompson, Dr\. Nadine Caron, Lauren Casey, Dr\. Rose Hatala, Shannon Field, Dr\. Maï Yasué and Madison Tardif

## Learning Objectives

1.  **Identify** and **analyze** structural, curricular, and institutional barriers to Indigenous health, cultural safety, and decolonization\.
2.  **Affirm** the responsibilities of settler educators and leaders to advance Indigenous health education\.
3.  **Apply** relational principles to teaching and learning environments to reduce the risk of harm, increase safety for learners and patients, and respond constructively to mistakes\.
4.  **Evaluate** the roles of leaders and faculty in curriculum change for reconciliation goals in our health and education systems\.

---

> **Question 1:** How can we lead and teach in a way that is truly inclusive, accountable, and rooted in justice and reconciliation?

### 1\. The curriculum needs to be reoriented to better teach empathy and compassion\.

-   Learners will encounter patients who are carrying the effects of trauma, who distrust the healthcare system and who are apprehensive about receiving care\.*“A lot of our people are choosing ill health because of the impediments that trauma has had on their lives\. If they’re First Nations, a history lives in our DNA, and if it lives in ours, it lives in yours\.”* \-D\. Thompson
-   Teachers, learners and staff face barriers when they advocate for curricular change to support better healthcare outcomes\.*“Some of the ways I do things \[in my teaching\] are in direct relationship to what I knew was fundamentally wrong \[when I was a student\]…I want \[learners\] to see my humanity… No one ever did that for me and I wish they had\.”* \-Dr\. N\. Caron

### 2\. More support for reconciliation in the curriculum is needed\.

-   Building buy\-in and momentum is worthwhile but often takes persistence\.Examples: *…Hallway lurking to \[follow\-up with\] people, repetition and reframing of requests, chunking and scaffolding of projects to avoid overwhelm…* \-M\. Tardif &amp; L\. Casey

### 3\. Settler faculty, staff and learners need to take on the bulk of the institutional labour for curricular change to be successful\.

-   Indigenous faculty, staff and learners cannot do this work by themselves\.*Writing grants, navigating bureaucracy, advocating for funding, and pushing curriculum change forward are not responsibilities that should default to Indigenous colleagues\. Indigenous expertise should guide the work, but the labour of implementation must be carried by settlers\.* \-Dr\. R\. Hatala

> **Question 2:** How can we overcome barriers to empathy and compassion in the curriculum and support lasting change?

| Barriers | Might show up as…\* | Try this… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curriculum not tied to assessment | “We don’t teach that because it’s not required\.” | Work to align Indigenous health learning &amp; assessment\. |
| Clinical time pressure | “I don’t have time to ‘reflect’ or ‘teach relationally\.'” | Question system design &amp; advocate for resources &amp; time to do the work\. |
| Departmental silos | “I don’t know who to ask\.” “Am I alone in this work?” “I can’t find examples of a process or a template that I could modify\.” | Seek collaborators and share information\. Access [centralized supports](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/transforming-health-for-everyone-strengthening-medical-curriculum-in-an-era-of-truth-reconciliation-and-inclusion/#Resources), [learning communities](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/transforming-health-for-everyone-strengthening-medical-curriculum-in-an-era-of-truth-reconciliation-and-inclusion/#Resources) &amp; retreats to reduce isolation and burnout\. |
| Faculty unprepared to teach content; a need for more cultural knowledge and/or pedagogical tools | “I was hired for my expertise, not my pedagogy\.” “I’m not qualified to teach that\.” “I’m afraid I’ll make mistakes &amp; get in trouble\.” | Seek training; avoid improvisation\. Implement harm\-reduction pedagogy, focused on repair &amp; accountability, rather than punitive measures\. Talk to [CTLT Indigenous Initiatives](https://indigenousinitiatives.ctlt.ubc.ca/research-resources/) for support and learning resources\. |
| Repeated negative responses to funding or curricular requests; leadership support without resources; institutional racism masked as process or lack of funds | “We can’t do that because… \[funding or process\-based reason\]\.” “Of course I support X, but…” | Persevere\. Innovate &amp; problem\-solve to find a way forward\. Start small, share visibly; invite colleagues to join in rather than asking if they agree\. Ask leaders what they have done, funded, &amp; protected–not what they think or have said\. |
| Historical denial or avoidance of colonial health outcomes | “Stories from the past are often exaggerated; don’t believe everything you hear…” “People are responsible for their own poor health…” | Teach from historical accuracy rather than neutrality\. Discuss the science behind intergenerational trauma and health outcomes\. |
| Faculty and learner resistance to change | “Why is Indigenous history more important than my history?” “It’s unfair when Indigenous people get special treatment\.” | Call out racism\. Ask for help if you need it\. Expect discomfort; do not remove challenging content to restore comfort\. Explain that discomfort is not harm\. |

*\*Examples provided here are non\-exhaustive\. They represent signals for changemakers to watch for–as intuitions, in conversations or through other manifestations–that can inform how they approach their work\.*

## Self\-Reflection: What is my role in curriculum change?

*Thinking about my resources:*

-   Where do I hold power–curricular, clinical, institutional, etc\.– or have the ability to influence positive change?
-   How am I using \(or not using\) my abilities to advance Indigenous health outcomes, cultural safety, and health equity for all?
-   What labour do I expect Indigenous colleagues to carry?
-   What systems or supports do I need to move from having “good intentions” to taking action–accountability and repair?

*Thinking about my next steps:*

-   What concrete steps can I take to accept more responsibility for Indigenous health and decolonization while still seeking guidance and accountability?
-   How can I apply the strategies from the video for responding to pushback, discomfort or a “no” when working in curricular or funding processes?
-   How do I respond when I make a mistake or cause harm in the educational context?
-   What is one small change I can make right now to incorporate relational principles into my professional practice?

## How can we help guide leaders’ support for enduring curricular change?

*Leading for reconciliation requires more than good intentions, representational content, or institutional statements\. It demands a fundamental shift in how leaders understand their responsibilities, relationships, and power within medical education\.*

**Pitfall 1: Treating reconciliation as an add\-on to the curriculum** We need to recognize that curriculum is not neutral\. Decisions about what is mandatory, what is optional, what is funded, and what is given protected time actively shape whose lives and knowledges matter\. Inclusion means embedding Indigenous health, cultural safety, and decolonizing approaches as core to medical education\.

**Pitfall 2: Avoiding accountability** We need to name racism and structural harm when they occur\. Institutional resistance, weaponizing processes, underfunding and avoidance of discomfort are not passive failures, but active forms of harm\. Leaders help when they start from, “How can we help make this happen?”

**Pitfall 3: Cramming lifelong learning to fit academic timelines** We need to see reconciliation as an ongoing transformation, not a compliance activity\. The goal is not to complete a course, meet a requirement, or avoid complaints, but to fundamentally shift how future health care providers understand their relationships to Indigenous peoples, to patients and to their own humanity\.

**Pitfall 4: Mandating Indigenous health content without preparation** We need to ensure faculty are prepared before new curriculum is mandated\. Educators must learn knowledge, humility and relational skills to teach it safely\. A lack of training places students at risk\. Institutions have a responsibility to train faculty, fund that training and recognize it as legitimate academic work\.

---

## How can educators build support with their teacher colleagues?

*Teaching for reconciliation is not an additional task layered onto medical education\. It is a reorientation of purpose, pedagogy, and practice toward care, relationality and justice\.*

-   **Reconciliation\-oriented teaching depends on relational and harm\-reduction pedagogy\.** Educators must assume that mistakes will happen but also recognize that harm has lasting consequences for learners and patients\. Accountability should not be punitive; it is about what happens after harm occurs\. We need teachers who are willing to listen, acknowledge mistakes, work to repair and then change\.
-   **Teaching rooted in justice attends to the hidden curriculum\.** What educators model in moments of stress teaches students as much as the formal content\. Educators that show their feelings and show care for the learners signal that being a good health care worker requires humanity and accountability\.
-   **UBC and Faculty of Medicine \(FoM\) have rich communities of support and resources\.** Groups such as the [Centre for Excellence in Indigenous Health](https://health.indigenous.ubc.ca/), [CTLT Indigenous Initiatives](https://indigenousinitiatives.ctlt.ubc.ca/), [CHES](https://ches.med.ubc.ca/programs/) and [FoM REDI](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/recordings/) create and facilitate learning spaces where respectful, meaningful dialogues can take place\. Educators from all levels of experience and knowledge can network, collaborate and find guidance in their curriculum journey\.
