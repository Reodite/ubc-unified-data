# Moving from Harm to Healing, Part II: A Practical and Relational Lens on Harm and Conflict

These reading notes summarize and provide reflective opportunities for viewers of the March 30, 2026 video of “[Moving from Harm to Healing, Part II: A Practical and Relational Lens on Harm and Conflict](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/moving-from-harm-to-healing-part-ii-a-practical-and-relational-lens-on-harm-and-conflict/),” a session from the *It Starts with Us* virtual panel series\.

**Link to Part I:** [Moving from Harm to Healing in EDI, Medical Education and Clinical Practice](https://redi.med.ubc.ca/moving-from-harm-to-healing-in-edi-medical-education-and-clinical-practice/)

## Featured Speaker

**Catherine Bergen** — Co\-founder of Just Outcomes Canada and a recognized leader in restorative conflict transformation, helps us move beyond the “why” of restorative practice to useful principles and frameworks that can guide everyday situations and address harm by promoting relationships, belonging, accountability, and repair\.

Panelists Dr\. Laura Bulk and Dr\. Joy Masuhara and moderators Madison Tardif and Dr\. Maï Yasué posed thoughtful questions about the healthcare setting that helped focus the restorative approach on applicable scenarios\.

## Learning Objectives

1.  **Compare** restorative to retributive approaches in the workplace\.
2.  **Analyze** workplace processes for responding to harm for whether they support restorative values of dignity and belonging\.
3.  **Relate** restorative practices to strategies for sharing power\.
4.  **Replace** retributive questions with restorative questions to improve relationships after conflict and harm\.

> Restorative approaches are compelling frameworks for organizations \(e\.g\., universities, programs, labs, clinics\) to rethink how their practices and cultures treat people \(e\.g\., in meetings, decision\-making, conflict, accountability\)\. By reorienting around community, relationships and repair, leaders and organizations can push back against adversarial, colonial values of traditional approaches\.

## Focus on Repair

Harm to people in and by organizations is inevitable and can occur across systems\. Restorative approaches focus on repair rather than punishment, addressing:

-   **\(A\)** the person harmed
-   **\(B\)** the person who caused harm
-   **\(C\)** the broader relational and systemic context

### Key Principles of Repair

-   Avoid framing accountability as blame
-   Identify responsibility as an invitation to restore trust and wholeness
-   Recognize that harm often originates from prior disconnection

> **Restorative approaches do not eliminate accountability or consequences\.**

Instead, they:

-   Set clear expectations
-   Provide opportunities for repair and growth
-   Acknowledge when someone may no longer be suited for a role but still uphold dignity
-   Believe that ignoring or dismissing harm \(“move on, just get over it”\) without repair leaves unresolved tensions that undermine organizational functioning

## New Guiding Questions for Restorative Approaches

When someone has been harmed by an organization — for example, by a decision, a change, or through an inquiry — a restorative approach has different priorities\.

### Adversarial Approach

-   What codes of conduct have been broken?
-   What evidence do we have to prove responsibility?
-   What disciplinary action needs to be taken?
-   How do we ensure no one shares any information about this?

### Restorative Approach

-   Who has been hurt?
-   What are their needs?
-   Who is obligated to meet these needs?
-   Who has been impacted?
-   What is the appropriate process to involve those impacted to set things right?
-   What can we learn moving forward?

## Restorative Approaches Can Shift How Organizations Treat People

While restorative approaches originated from a criminology context as an alternative response to harm, their principles can also apply to other organizational practices — for example, meetings, conflict engagement, decision\-making, and other workplace practices\.

### Shift From → Shift To

-   Blaming **individuals** for bad actions → Exploring the role **systems** play in creating the conditions
-   A process that **seizes power** from participants, a\.k\.a\. “power over” → A process that **empowers** participants, a\.k\.a\. “power with”
-   Parties experiencing **isolation** → Parties experiencing **connection**
-   Outcomes rooted in **punishment** → Outcomes rooted in **repair and growth**

## Emphasizing Dignity and Belonging in People\-Centered Practices

### With Dignity, People…

-   Are empowered, included &amp; respected
-   Feel autonomy and agency
-   Experience curiosity and positive regard

### With Belonging, People…

-   Feel interconnection
-   Exist in relationships of reciprocity, accountability &amp; support

**Dignity** \(individual wholeness\) is upheld through belonging \(being seen, respected, and included\)\.

**Belonging** \(relational wholeness\) depends on the assurance that one’s dignity is recognized\.

---

## Three Ways Leaders Can Use Their Power to Uphold Dignity and Belonging

Traditional organizational structures concentrate power at the top\. In contrast, restorative approaches improve effectiveness and foster equity by emphasizing “power with” rather than “power over\.” These practices also share decision\-making with those most impacted\.

### 1\. Consensus\-Based Decision Making

**What is it?** Ensuring everyone is heard and that decisions reflect what the group can live with, not majority rule\.

**How?**

-   Give voice to all participants
-   Build collective responsibility and ownership
-   Reinforce dignity through inclusion

**Ways to get started — Ask for a “Show of Thumbs”:**

-   👍 Thumbs up \= “I support the proposed idea\.”
-   ✋ Thumbs middle \= “I have concerns, but I can live with the proposed idea\.”
-   👎 Thumbs down \= “I do not support the proposed idea; I have an alternative proposal\.”

### 2\. Group Agreements to Uphold Dignity &amp; Belonging

**What is it?** Discussing up front how to have dignity and belonging in day\-to\-day operations\.

**How?**

-   Agree upon expectations for how people will show up in relationships
-   Define what happens when expectations are not met

**Ways to get started — Start &amp; normalize:**

-   Discussions about shared values across teams, units, programs, offices
-   Creating relational and practical pathways for accountability
-   Trying restorative approaches in day\-to\-day practices

### 3\. Decentralized Leadership

**What is it?** Reducing hierarchical barriers by sharing power and responsibility\.

**How?**

-   Help people broaden perspectives
-   Encourage shared understanding across the organization
-   Support continuous learning rather than top\-down certainty

**Ways to get started — Create Reference Teams:**

-   Composed of individuals directly impacted by decisions
-   They provide input into organizational changes
-   They ensure decisions reflect lived realities

## Q&amp;A: Principles of Restorative Approaches in Healthcare Settings

### Dignity &amp; Belonging

**Maï:** What do we do when \[participants in a restorative process\] are frustrated that we are all spending so much time and effort to uphold the dignity of the person who caused terrible harm?

**Catherine:** Leaders who believe the research in the efficacy of restorative principles can make agreements in advance that everyone deserves dignity\.

### Principles of Repair

**Madison:** How can participants of a restorative \[harm investigation\] process evaluate the success of an intervention — especially in healthcare settings where big emotions are often considered inappropriate?

**Catherine:** Participants may need to create new questions to evaluate a restorative intervention, such as: “Do you feel heard?”; “Did you feel like you had a chance to give input?”; “How was the process?”; “Would you do this again instead of an investigation?”; “How well did we adhere to our values?”

### Repairing Harm

**Maï:** How do you pitch restorative approaches to learners in less powerful roles who have a fear of retaliation?

**Catherine:** Consider one\-on\-one conversations to make advance agreements about what the results will be if it doesn’t work out\. Helping those with less power feel secure before committing to participation is important, as well as believing them and giving them an out when they say they’re at risk\.

### Power Sharing &amp; Transparency

**Joy:** How do leaders learn what’s happening on the front lines to help them make better decisions and help people feel heard? How do you connect grass roots with leaders at the top?

**Maï:** I love that point that leaders are seeing the whole and need to make space for staff to understand the big picture because staff are often in the dark and wondering why the leaders are making their jobs harder\.

**Laura:** I think it really highlights the importance of humanizing one another\. It is easy to dehumanize leaders especially when we have the sense that there is no transparency\.

## Questions for Discussion

1.  What makes restorative approaches different from adversarial/colonial approaches to justice, change, and day\-to\-day operations?
2.  Where in health sciences, professions, and education do we tend to frame situations as adversarial, or prefer objectivity and independence instead of connectedness, transparency, engagement and interdependence?
3.  What changes to the classroom environment, organizational processes or teaching might accompany a shift toward restorative approaches?
4.  Howard Zehr, considered one of the originators of restorative justice, writes: *“Whether we have victimized or have been victimized, the journey from brokenness and isolation to transcendence and belonging requires us to re\-narrate our stories so that they are no longer just about shame and humiliation but ultimately about dignity and triumph\.”* Discuss\. How does Zehr’s idea of “re\-narrat\[ing\] our stories” show up in teaching, providing care, research or clinical work?
5.  What would need to change in your context \(workplace, learning environment, clinic, lab, etc\.\) to foster restorative approaches? What steps can you take to support those changes?
