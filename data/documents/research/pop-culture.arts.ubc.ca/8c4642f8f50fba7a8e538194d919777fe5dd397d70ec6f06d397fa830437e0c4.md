### Created by Dr\. Ben Hoy \(U Saskatchewan\)

*Shared with permission\.*

Games have a curious relationship to human life\. On the one hand, games form the basis of some of the most compelling metaphors we use to describe human interactions\. We talk about historical circumstances as ‘zero\-sum games,’ taking chances as ‘rolling the dice’ and sure things as ‘slam dunks\.’ We also use games to model and reflect human life, be that in the moralizing fables built into Snakes and Ladders or the military structure of chess\. The fact that games model aspects of human life are part of what make them compelling\.

This complex relationship and the fascination that humans have with games make them powerful tools for teaching and learning history\. Like popular history books, blogs, or articles, museum exhibits, and films, games are very often a form of popular history\. Historians, however, rarely seize the opportunities they offer\.

In the same sense that a theoretical model may reveal aspects of the past otherwise invisible \(while obscuring others\), games can give players an enhanced and experiential perspective on aspects of the past\. Games also create opportunities for learning by allowing reflection on the parts of the games that accurately reflect the past and which parts do not\.

In this course students will develop historical expertise through seminar readings, discussions, and an independent research project\. They will then work together with the instructor to create a game that models some aspect of that historical circumstance\. Finally, they will then evaluate the game they have produced in light of their knowledge of the period\.

#### Course Objectives

-   -   Students will be able to create, evaluate, and defend historical argument created in a game\-based environment
    -   Students will learn to think with games by creating an interactive experience from pre\-existing research
    -   Students will develop skills in verbal communication through team\-based interactions with their peers
    -   Students will explore the way history can and should be represented in non\-academic settings
    -   Students will analyze the value and limitation of models for understanding history
    -   Students will understand the major theories that surround game\-based learning and apply them as they create a game of their own
