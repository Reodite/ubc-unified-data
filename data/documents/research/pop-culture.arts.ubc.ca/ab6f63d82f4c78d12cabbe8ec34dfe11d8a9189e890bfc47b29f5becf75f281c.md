A scene from the Hand Games Competition, Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre, 2026 Arctic Winter Games\.

From March 8th to 13th, the leadership team for UBC’s SSHRC\-funded [Visual Storytelling in the Indigenous North](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/comics-studies/projects/visual-storytelling-indigenous-north/) project accompanied [6 Indigenous cartoonists and 3 Indigenous game designers](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/meet-the-awg-artists/) to the [**2026 Arctic Winter Games**](http://awg2026.org/) in **Whitehorse, Yukon** to document the event, create AWG\-inspired media, and build meaningful relationships\.

The team included Project Co\-Directors Drs\. [Shannon Leddy \(Métis\)](https://edcp.educ.ubc.ca/shannon-leddy/) and [Biz Nijdam](https://cenes.ubc.ca/profile/biz-nijdam/) and Project Co\-Leads Drs\. [Asta Mønsted \(Kalaallit\)](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/persons/asta-m%C3%B8nsted/), [Tim Frandy \(Sámi\)](https://cenes.ubc.ca/profile/tim-frandy/), and [Frederik Byrn Køhlert](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/person/3464631/frederik-byrn-kohlert), as well as 2 members of the project’s Advisory Council, 2 graduate students, and the project manager, Jennifer Nagtegaal\)\.

#### The Arctic Winter Games is a high\-profile circumpolar sporting competition for northern and Arctic athletes and a multicultural exposition for the circumpolar world\. The Games take place every 2 years and celebrate sport, social exchange, and culture while providing an opportunity for developing athletes to participate in friendly competition and share the cultural values of the global Indigenous North\.

The team’s trip to the Arctic Winter Games in Whitehorse more than exceeded expectations\. Survey feedback from participants was overwhelmingly positive: all respondents reported that the experience met or exceeded expectations, highlighting the trip’s strong sense of inclusion, community, and cultural resonance\.

After the team arrived over the weekend, we came together the next morning for breakfast at **[Burnt Toast Cafe](https://burnttoastcafe.ca/)** before holding our first project meeting to set the tone for the week\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2121-2048x1244.jpg)

From left to right: [Asta Mønsted \(Kalaallit\)](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/persons/asta-m%C3%B8nsted/) and [Tim Frandy \(Sámi\)](https://cenes.ubc.ca/profile/tim-frandy/) Burnt Toast Cafe\.

We shared the goals of our comics co\-creation and situated it within the broader work of the [**Visual Storytelling in the Indigenous North**](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/comics-studies/projects/visual-storytelling-indigenous-north/) project, including our upcoming visits to **Finland** and **Kalaallit Nunaat** \(Greenland\)\. Here, **Tim Frandy** \(Sámi\) spoke about emerging collaborations in **Sápmi**, while **Asta Mønsted** \(Kalaallit\) shared information about our partnerships in Kalaallit Nunaat \(Greenland\), including our upcoming work with **Meeqqat Angerlarsimaffiat**, the children’s home in Uummannaq\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2269-2048x1365.jpg)

In the foreground, Project Co\-director Shannon Leddy \(Métis\) listens to Project Co\-leads Asta Mønsted \(Kalaallit, left\) and Frederik Byrn Køhlert \(right\) in a morning meeting\.

Many of our mornings unfolded in the conference room attached to the hotel, which quickly became one of the most important spaces of the trip\. It was there that we shared stories, jokes, and baked goods, while creating art, playing games, and making new friends\.

#### Creating a sense of shared space has emerged as a core element of our shared work and research goals, and project participants specifically identified our project’s informal conversations and common gathering spaces as central to the trip’s strong feeling of community, care, and belonging\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2349-2048x1365.jpg)

The weather began relatively mild but quickly turned cold, though fortunately, the team was well equipped with rental cold\-weather gear\. Even so, the northern environment left a strong impression\. Members of the team later described the setting itself as grounding and emotionally impactful, even as the cold and transportation logistics sometimes made movement between venues more challenging\.

#### The most meaningful moments of the trip, however, emerged at the Games themselves\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2465-2048x1083.jpg)

A scene from the Snow Snake Competition, Mount Sima, 2026 Arctic Winter Games\.

While the Arctic Winter Games features familiar transnational winter sports such as skiing and speed skating, the **[Dene Games](https://www.awg2026.org/dene-games)** and **[Arctic Sports](https://www.awg2026.org/arctic-sports)** were what most clearly set these transnational sporting competitions apart\. The Games’ own materials emphasize that these events are rooted in northern and Indigenous lifeways, with sports such as stick pull tied to fishing, snow snake to hunting practice, hand games to long\-standing forms of gathering and exchange, and events like the one\-foot high kick, kneel jump, and knuckle hop grounded in strength, communication, endurance, and survival on the land\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2379-2048x1050.jpg)

A scene from the Stick Pull Compeition, Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre, 2026 Arctic Winter Games

One of the week’s clear highlights was the Hand Games at the **[Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre](https://kwanlindunculturalcentre.com/)**\. The atmosphere was extraordinary, with drumming, singing, and cheering making the event feel as much a communal gathering as a competition\. [Hand games are one of the most widespread games among Dene nations\.](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/dene-games) Historically, they were played for fun, skill, and trade or exchange, but their [popularity had fallen due to the effects of colonialism and residential schools\.](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/north/handgames-beginners-in-whitehorse-find-community-and-confidence-9.7030225) That all changed in 1998, when [Elder Yvonne Ann Smarch organized the first Annual Yukon Rendezvous Handgames Tournament](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/north/yukon-handgames-tournament-gets-new-name-1.7125424)\. Today, with hand games being taught in schools across the Northwest Territories and Yukon, there’s no shortage of adults and kids who are eager to learn and help preserve their cultural knowledge, and their important presence in the Arctic Winter Games is a testament to that\.

Another major highlight was the **[Cultural Gala](https://www.awg2026.org/cultural-gala)**, held March 12 and 13 at the Yukon Arts Centre, where nine contingents shared performances celebrating “culture, creativity, and the spirit of the North\.” The program brought together an extraordinary range of forms, including Greenlandic performance exploring identity, spirituality, and transformation; Sámi joik, film, and reindeer\-herding traditions; Alaskan dance rooted in language revitalization; Nunavik performance inspired by constellations and stories from Elders; Nunavut drum dancing and throat singing; Métis and Indigenous dance from Alberta North and the Northwest Territories; and contemporary dance from Yukon youth\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/IMG_7682-2048x1377.jpg)

A scene from Team Nunavik’s performance at the cultural gala with throat singing and aerial acrobatics\.

The AWG Cultural Gala beautifully interwove traditional cultural expression with contemporary music, movement, and performance\.

The team also took three memorable field trips\. One evening, we visited the [**local hot springs**](https://eclipsenordichotsprings.ca/), where we moved between Scandinavian\-style rock pools and Japanese onsen, laughing, sharing stories, and occasionally braving a roll in the snow between soaks\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/IMG_0845-1536x954.jpeg)

Entering into Carcross\.

Later in the week, we took a road trip to **[Carcross](https://destinationcarcross.ca/)** to visit the **[Carving Centre of Master Carver Keith Wolfe Smarch](https://destinationcarcross.ca/project/carcrosstagish-first-nation-carving-centre/)** and experience the surrounding area through the eyes \(and the stories\) of our personal AWG\-liason and former [**Minister of Community Services John Streicker**](https://yukonassembly.ca/member/john-streicker)\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/IMG_0891-1024x546.jpeg)

“Jean\-Claude Damn Van,” [Haa Shagóon Hídi \(Carcross/Tagish Learning Centre\)](https://www.ctfn.ca/haa-shagoon-hidi/visit), Carcross\.

Finally, on the Thursday after the Cultural Gala, we returned to [**Mount Sima**](https://mountsima.com/) in our rental van—affectionately nicknamed **“Jean Claude Damn Van”**— on the only truly clear night in hopes of seeing the northern lights\. We did not catch the aurora borealis in full glory this time, but we left comforted knowing we would be returning to the Yukon to see them properly, at the conclusion of the project that brought us here\. And even before we left Whitehorse, that prospect was something the team was already looking forward to\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2151-2048x1516.jpg)

\(L\) David Plamondon \(Nehiyaw\) and \(R\) John Streicker at the Whitehorse airport\.

#### Looking back, what stands out most about our trip to the Arctic Winter Games is not only how much we learned, but also how much the trip affirmed the value of this work\. Grounded in relational research practices and co\-created arts\-based inquiry, the Arctic Winter Games provided a powerful setting for building relationships, developing our methodologies, exchanging ideas, and imagining future work together\. We returned energized, more connected, and excited about what comes next\.

*Our time at the Arctic Winter Games was generously supported by our partnerships with the Arctic Winter Games International Committee \(AWGIC\), Education without Borders, and Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre \(Whitehorse, Yukon\), and The Truth Commission for the Sami People, Sweden\. It was financed by a SSHRC Partnership Development Grant and funding from UBC’s Indigenous Strategic Initiatives\.*
