## Welcome

The **Accessible Arts and Popular Culture Workshop** was a [Pop Pedagogies Award Program](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies/award/) funded project that explored the relationship between touch and the arts through student\-focused programming on non\-visual engagements with Ainu traditional crafts and Japanese popular culture\. The workshop was lead by V Rahbar with collaboration from the UBC Library’s Tomoko Kitayama Yen and Chelsea Shriver as well as guest presenters Dr\. Akiko Takesue of the Royal Ontario Museum \(ROM\) and Kay Slater of grunt gallery\.

[Workshop graphic recordings with textual descriptions](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/arts-based-research/aber-research-projects/accessible-arts-and-pop-culture-workshop/#graphics) were produced by University of British Columbia iSchool graduate students Care Barker and Miriam Rojas\.

Instructors and others who wish to to replicate the workshop are encouraged to check out [Workshop Details for Instructors](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/arts-based-research/aber-research-projects/accessible-arts-and-pop-culture-workshop/#details)\.

The workshop was a part of [the Pop Pedagogies Lightning Talk event](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies-lightning-talks-highlights/) with [V Rahbar’s lightning talk about the workshop available](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UMKcLc1Lavc) with auto\-generated subtitles only\.

## About the Workshop

The workshop was held from 1:30pm to 3:30pm on 13 November 2025 during [iSchool Community Learning Day 2025](https://ischool.ubc.ca/events/event/ischool-community-learning-day/), an inaugural occasion where the iSchool cancelled classes in favour of equity and inclusion focused events, workshops, and other engagements for students, staff, and faculty\. The workshop was a redesign of a special teaching session for ASIA 590\-36: Thinking with the Body: Embodied, Sensory and Non\-Representational Methodologies \(Dr\. Ayaka Yoshimizu\), previously taught Winter Term 2023 \(March 2024\) and Winter Term 2024 \(February 2025\)\.

The workshop included a panel of information professionals from the GLAM sector \(Dr\. Akiko Takesue and Kay Slater\) sharing their knowledge before an activity portion involving a touch tour \(Tomoko Kitayama Yen and Chelsea Shriver\) and tactile art room \(V Rahbar\) distributed at multiple learning stations\. The workshop spoke to how the arts and popular culture can be taught as a form of multi\-sensory art appreciation, non\-visual and tactile experiences made possible by libraries, art galleries, and museums supporting accessible engagement opportunities, with a focus on media from Japan\. At the end of the workshop, attendees left with a stronger understanding of how the arts and popular culture are not purely visual mediums but rather multi\-sensory, eroding against cultural institutions that continue to argue for a world where art is forbidden to be touched\.

### *Tactile Translation* Textual Description

#### **Text version of graphic work titled *Tactile Translation* \(2026\)\.**

Written by Miriam Rojas\.

Page Description:

Comic with 8 panels ordered in two columns\. At the very top, a text with the Title “Tactile Translation” accompanied by a smaller text detailing the credits “By: Miriam Rojas”\. Colours used vary between a dark blue, a muted purple, a warm ivory, and a soft pink\.  The colour palette tries to communicate softness but seriousness\.

##### Frame/Panel 1:

A reproduction of part of the Dodson Room at UBC where the panel occurred\. A screen appears bright with some drawings of the accessible arts examples that were presented that day\. The word “Touch?” appears as a question in the screen that is being projected on, giving meaning to the very distinct objects that are also on screen\. Bringing everything together with the theme of tactility or touch\. There is a text box accompanying the panel\. In quotation marks, as a referenced dialogue, the text reads: “Arts might not be accessible”\.

##### Frame/Panel 2:

Two people in the washroom sharing undisclosed dialogue\. They might be washing their hands or finishing washing their hands\.  As every panel is accompanied by a text box, this one is too\. On the text box, as a reference to the discussion that occurred during the panel and then must have continued in the washroom, a text reads: “That sentence must have stuck with us”,

##### Frame/Panel 3:

Four tables with four mantles each\. Something seems to be hidden behind the mantles\. The folds, relief, and contours that stand out reveal the existence of different objects under the mantles\. Accompanying the panel, there is another text box that reads: “The room we enter is filled with things we can’t see”\.

##### Frame/Panel 4:

The corner of one of the mantles accompanied by a label that describes the hidden artwork\. The seams of the two mantles, the one that serves as a support and the one that covers, are visible\. Even the mantles are part of the detailed work of the event, one that can be felt through the seams and weight of a mantle\. The drawn label next to the covered artwork reads in handwritten letter “Clamp 2021, XXXHolic Scroll”\. At the bottom of the panel, there is a box of typed text that reads: “I catch a scroll I’m interested in”\.

##### Frame/Panel 5:

A diagram of a scroll, without any picture\. There are arrows pointing out the decorative ribbons of the scroll, the ribbon that keeps the scroll closed, the paper and the cloth that was used in the making of the scroll, and the main cylindrical part of the scroll\. In the middle of the scroll, where usually the painting/picture/or text would be the word “PLAIN” is written\. Different patterns were used for different textures\. One of the arrows points out to a text that just says “fun\!”, in reference to a texture that is nice to the touch\. As with every panel, there is a text box at the bottom that reads: “But when I touch it this is what I see:”, inviting the reader to think of the diagram as the mental image that is formed when relying on touch to discover a scroll\. For this scroll the only sense of orientation one can have without relying on sight is the position of the main cylinder and the biggest ribbon that is supposed to keep the scroll closed or open, which are both on the same side\.

##### Frame/Panel 6:

A replication of the artwork inside the scroll, where we can see Kimihiro Watanuki with a nine\-tailed fox from popular manga xxxHOLiC\. Once again, accompanying the panel, the text box reads: “It gets me a bit sad that I can’t see the character inside”\.

##### Frame/Panel 7:

There are two images of Buddha that stand out due to the contrast of their colours\. With arrows, an image is tagged with written text as “Tactile Art” and the other as “Photo”\. The only image that seems to have any texture at all is the image corresponding to the Tactile Art\. The photo also seems to appear lighter with a similar color as the background in the panel, making it almost disappear\. The text box that goes with this panel reads: “In the other room there are tactile reproductions of art”\.

##### Frame/Panel 8:

This is a busier panel\. There are two hands interacting with what seems to be a tactile reproduction of art\. The hands must pertain to one person\. The left hand is holding it, and the right hand is sliding its index finger over it\. Right in front of this imagined person, there is a sculpture, which is identical to what appears on the sheet of paper that is being held\. We can assume that what is being held is a tactile art reproduction\. Shadows on the tactile art reproduction are given by dotted lines\. Next to the hands holding the reproduction, another hand holding a device for an audio tour guide\. There are people all around the sculpture, some are discussing it, pointing out at it, or simply looking at it\. Accompanying the panel, the text box reads “Now I can see what the artist wanted me to see”, wrapping the end of the comic on how tactile art can make art more understandable and reach more people\.

### *Accessible Arts Panel Comic* Textual Description

#### ***Accessible Arts Panel Comic* Comic**

By Care Barker, 2026

MLIS candidate at UBC iSchool

##### Production Notes:

The images in this comic were born digital on an iPad; these comics were drawn with an Apple pencil in Clip Studio Paint\. All three pages have backgrounds that use wet wash and wet blotting ink brushes to invoke a feeling of textured paper\. The backgrounds are coloured to invoke the spaces they represent\. The first two pages, which cover the academic talk given by Rahbar and the *Shokkan* Museum exhibit discussed by Dr\. Takesue, both have creamy parchment like backgrounds\. The third page, which focuses on the grunt gallery, uses splotchy grey wash to invoke grunge\. The content is organized in ways that follow the feeling of the speakers’ talks\. The pages are in full colour\. The speakers’ faces are depicted somewhat realistically, while other, unnamed characters are depicted simply, with little detail\. Care was taken to ensure that all text is a minimum of 18 point, which would make it large print when printed on 8\.5 x 11 paper\.

##### Page 1:

This page has no clearly defined panels, and the ideas are meant to flow from one word bubble to the next, left to right, top to bottom\. A sky blue rectangle occupies the top quarter of the page, and contains text that introduces the event\. The text is centered on the page\.

*Line one: Funded by the Pop Pedagogies Award Program*

*Line two: UBC iSchool Community Learning Day 2025 PRESENTS*

The third line is in bold, all caps, title text\.

*Line three: ACCESSIBLE ARTS &amp; POPULAR CULTURE WORKSHOP*

Below this, still on the blue rectangle, are the three portraits of the speakers, each enclosed in their own grey circles\. Their names are above their portraits, framed by a white paint\-like brushstroke that follows the grey circle\.

*From left to right, the portraits read: V\. RAHBAR, DR\. AKIKO TAKESUE, and KAY SLATER*

Rahbar is a femme presenting Latinx person\. She has dark, thick hair with a reddish tinge\. Her hair is tucked behind one ear, but seems to spill onto her face and over her shoulders, which are barely mostly cut off by the circular portrait\. Rahbar wears glasses that have a cat\-eye shape\. Beneath her portrait are the words, *UBC iSchool*, where Rahbar is a PhD candidate\.

The middle portrait is of Dr\. Akiko Takesue, a femme presenting Japanese person\. Dr\. Takesue also wears glasses, but with thin metal frames that are almost invisible\. She has shoulder length black hair, which is meticulously styled\. Her hair is parted on the left to sweep over the right side of her forehead, covering her right ear\. On the left side, her hair is tucked behind her ear\. Dr\. Takesue is smiling; her mouth is closed and there are smile lines\. She wears a dark pink blazer and charcoal grey blouse\. Beneath her portrait are the words, *Royal Ontario Museum*, where Dr\. Takesue works as the Bishop White Committee Associate Curator of Japanese Art &amp; Culture\.

The portrait on the right is of Kay Slater\. Kay is a non\-binary presenting white person\. They have a slim face with brown eyes and piercings in their nose and ears\. Their nose piercing is a silver ring\. They have a prominent undercut, with their long hair pulled back over the top of their head into a black and grey braid\. Kay is wearing a black t\-shirt\. Under their portrait are the words, *grunt gallery*, where Kay works as the Accessibility and Exhibitions Manager\.

Below the blue rectangle, on the left side of the page, Rahbar stands behind a wooden podium that says UBC\. A white cane equipped with a rolling tip leans against the podium\.

*RAHBAR: Hello everyone, I’m Rahbar\. I do a lot of work transforming arts from a purely VISUAL experience to one that uses sound, smell, touch, and more\.*

Beside the words for sound, smell, touch, and more, there are representations of each of those senses\. A speaker with sound waves moving towards an ear, a nose with smell lines, and an open hand, palm up, with a gold ring on the finger\.  The senses are separated but abstractly laid out as a face with a hand raised beside it\. The face has brown eyes\.

*RAHBAR: Tactile experiences with art teach us that not all objects exist only to be viewed through a locked glass case\.*

Below Rahbar is a pale green box on a blue wash background\. Inside the box is a quote\.

“Touch tours… give a person aspects of an artwork that may not be apparent to the eyes alone” \(Kleege, 2017, p\. 60\)\.

The quote is taken from Kleege, G\. \(2017\)\. *More than meets the eye: What blindness brings to art*\. Oxford University Press\.

On the right side of the page, a simplified cartoon man with dark skin and short, tightly curled hair is wearing a soft blue sweater, and brown dress pants\. He reaches towards a tall glass display case\. He is frowning\.

The display case is roped off, and has a sign in front of it which reads DO NOT TOUCH\.

The display case is highlighted as a focal point with manga iconography that shows shock, as if the case is surprised that the man is reaching towards it\.

Inside the case is a colourful, and abstract sculpture, that rises up in organic shapes out of a ridged golden pedestal\. There are many dimensions to this object, but it is difficult to know what the object is, or truly understand it, because the case obscures it and holds it at a distance from the patron who wants to engage with it\.

*RAHBAR: Accessible arts aim to invite more patrons into cultural institutions, not fewer\.*

##### Page 2:

Line 1: *SHOKKAN*: MATERIAL ENCOUNTERS IN JAPANESE ART  
Line 2: AN EXHIBIT AT THE ROYAL ONTARIO MUSEUM  
Line 3: with Dr\. Akiko Takesue, Associate Curator of Japanese Art and Culture

The page has a creamy yellow background with edges like curled or wet parchment\. Dr\. Takesue’s talk was delivered over Zoom, and accompanied by slides\.

The slides were translated into smaller comic panels to represent concepts and objects\. These small panels surround Dr\. Takesue, who stands in the bottom center of the page, in front of the Van Horne collection of Japanese ceramics\.

Beneath the title at the top left is Dr\. Takesue’s portrait\. She is there to guide us through the planned *Shokkan* exhibit\.

*TAKESUE: Our Shokkan exhibit is not about accessibility\. Pre\-18th Century, touch was the core sense in Japan\. Japanese has many words for “touch”\.*

Below Dr\. Takesue is a full page width panel\. Its background is like a silky fabric washed with splotchy shades of Japanese indigo dye that lighten in the middle and become darker towards the edges of the panel\. On this background is a complicated web of relationships, connected by pastel brushstroke arrows in *aijiro*, the lightest, nearly white shade of indigo dye\. The words are handwritten in a way that invokes Japanese calligraphic strokes\. The words are in *noukon*, the darkest blue shade of indigo dye\.

We get the sense that the Japanese understanding of touch is complex\. In the center of the web are the words, SENSE OF TOUCH\. Vision, language, memory, hearing, haptic sensation are all shown through arrows to interrelate to each other and to SENSE OF TOUCH, or *shokkan*\. The act of touch appears to be an entry point into the diagram, and relates back and forth to haptic sensation\.

*TAKESUE: Shokkan is the psychological sense of touch\. Memory, language, and other senses play a role\.*

*TAKESUE: Touch is critical to creation\. Texture and tactility help us appreciate Japanese art\.*

Below this large diagram, there is a simplified version of Dr\. Takesue\. She is wearing a dark gray blazer and matching skirt, and a reddish pink shirt with a ruffled scarf\. Her arms are spread wide to encompass the exhibition and more than 50 objects from the Van Horne collection of Japanese ceramics are shown behind her on a wall of thin shelves\. She is grinning broadly and seems excited\.

*TAKESUE: Small, handheld objects were prized by collectors and travelled around the world\.*

To the right of Dr\. Takesue is a panel where a potter’s hands are molding a ceramic bowl on a pottery wheel\. It is large compared to Dr\. Takesue\. It references the videos she said would be contained within the exhibit\. These videos are designed to show visitors how important touch is to the process of creation\.

*TAKESUE: Through their hands, artists infuse themselves in their work\.*

On the left of Dr\. Takesue, and wrapping around underneath her are panels which highlight objects and notable features of the exhibit, as Dr\. Takesue’s slides did\.

*TAKESUE: At tactile stations, our visitors will be able to touch objects\!*

Each of the following items is contained in its own panel, on an indigo wash background\.

UNFURL HANDSCROLLS: Panel one depicts a scroll of rolled parchment\. There are lemon shaped wooden knobs on each end\. The scroll is wrapped and tied with a traditional braided cord in red, grey, and white\.

GRIP SWORD HANDLES: A *katana* sword handle is shown, with an intricately wrapped blue handle\.

WRAP FUROSHIKI: *Furoshiki* is a style of cloth wrapping\. An object is wrapped in a blue and white patterned cloth that is drawn into a bow at the top\.

TOUCH NETSUKE: A *netsuke* is a miniature sculpture\. A carved wooden sculpture of a mouse hangs from a chain\. The mouse’s tail wraps around its legs\. It is shown from the side, its large eye looking up at the reader\. The mouse is smooth and round\. It is carved out of boxwood\.

FEEL KIMONO FABRIC: A section of red *kimono* fabric has embroidered green leaves and raised white peony designs, giving texture to the fabric\.

*TAKESUE: Japanese art is meant to be touched\.*  

##### Page 3:

This page includes four panels and each spans the length of the page\. Each panel touches on aspects of the grunt gallery’s work on accessibility\.

The grunt logo is recreated with thick, oil paint brush strokes in black and the word grunt dry brushed on top\. Beside this, thick brushwork creates a grey background, on which the words Exhibition \+ Accessibility Manager – KAY SLATER, have been dry brushed in white\.

Kay’s portrait is on the right side of the page\.

*KAY: The grunt gallery is small, but we work to serve our community so people can be their entire selves\.*

The first panel is labelled with white text on a black, rough edged background\. It reads: AUDIO TOURS\.

On a washed yellow background, there is a Yoto Player, a listening device for children\. It is a simple rectangular design with a slot to insert a card with audio data\. It has two large, orange dials, a built\-in speaker, and a tiny square screen in the bottom corner, which is displaying a happy face\. Next to the Yoto Player, there is a checkmark\.

Beside the Yoto Player, there is a specialty museum audio device\. It is complex, with at least 20 buttons, a thin green screen for displaying text, and a headphone jack, with a dangling ear piece\. The complicated device is crossed out with a large red X\.

*KAY: Yoto Players let us give audio tours with invisible context about the art and installation\.*

*KAY: They’re made for kids, so they’re durable and cheap to fix or replace\.*

The next panel is also labelled with white text on a black, rough edged background\. It reads: TACTILE OBJECTS &amp; TOUCH POLICY\. The panel has a light mauve watercolour background\.

*KAY: The artist chooses which objects are touchable\.*

A bunch of purple grapes sit in the middle of the panel, surrounded by a thick red circle with a slash through it\. A discount style sticker covers part of the circle, and reads, NO TOKENISM\.

*KAY: We don’t hand visitors plastic grapes to stand in for paintings of fruit\. They know what grapes are\. That’s just insulting\.*

The next panel is also labelled with white text on a black, rough edged background\. It reads: TACTILE NAVIGATION TOOLS\. This panel has a pale blue washed background\.

Kay details the painstaking process the grunt has taken to create navigational tools that do not rely on sight\.

*KAY: We tried paper maps with raised, tactile borders\. But architectural symbols rely on sight…*

There is a raised, red outline of a map on a piece of paper\. The dark\-skinned hand of a visitor taps one of the objects\.

*VISITOR: Is this a gate?*

*VISITOR: Is it art?*

Below, a *chibi* cartoon\-style portrait of Kay has giant sweat beads above their head\. Their eyes are shut and they seem embarrassed\. Their word bubble is melting\.

*KAY: No, it’s stairs…*

To the right there is an updated version of the tactile gallery, a 3d model made out of cardstock and mounted on a cardstock base with a legend in bright red\. It has two wide open doors\. There are many windows\. Inside there is a film playing on the back wall and a black sculpture in front of it\. There are also stairs\.

*KAY: Now we use 3D paper models for rapid prototyping\.*

The final panel is also labelled with white text on a black, rough edged background\. It reads: NON\-VISUAL ARTIST RESIDENCIES &amp; BLIND LED TOURS\. This panel has a brownish\-yellow watercolour background, which has darker streaks behind the figures\.

Two people are walking together\. One is a femme presenting person who is wearing a deep red bandana in their purple hair\. They are wearing sunglasses, a leopard print tunic, and orange pants\. They are holding the arm of a person with shoulder length dark hair\. This person wears a green long sleeved shirt, stonewashed jeans, and is using a roller ball white cane\. Their eyes are shown pure white, suggesting they are blind\.

*KAY: Make mistakes\. Practice shared authorship\. We mess up more when we don’t involve blind individuals\.*
