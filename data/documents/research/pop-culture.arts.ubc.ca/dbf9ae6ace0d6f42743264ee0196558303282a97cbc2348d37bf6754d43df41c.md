Earlier this year, the UBC Comics Studies Cluster introduced the three local comics artists selected to bring youth perspectives on trauma\-informed care to life: [Kristina Luu, James Lloyd, and Ivana Filipovich](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/youth-perspectives-on-trauma-informed-care-artists/)\. Now, the project has moved from research and consultation into the next stage of visual storytelling\.

Each artist has selected two of the six key messages that emerged from national youth consultations and is developing stories around them\. The messages come from [*Youth Perspectives on Trauma\-Informed Health Care: Report on a National Consultation*](https://cps.ca/uploads/tools/2026_CPS_Trauma-Informed_Health_Care_FINAL.pdf), produced by the Canadian Paediatric Society and B\.C\. Children’s Hospital Research Institute\. Co\-designed with a Youth Advisory Council, the consultation engaged more than 50 youth from across Canada\.

The six messages address what young people need from trauma\-informed care, including privacy and youth\-friendly spaces, having their own health concerns centred during appointments, and feeling able to speak openly about sensitive issues\. They also emphasize choice around who is present during appointments and the importance of having a clear plan for follow\-up and support after a visit\.

Following the submission of initial drafts and feedback from the project team – including health care providers and members of the Youth Advisory Committee – the trio of artists are now revising their stories\. The comics will translate the messages from the consultation into narratives that show what trauma\-informed care can look and feel like during a health\-care encounter\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/09/lloyd5.jpg)

James Lloyd, page 1 of Message 1 story: Safe care is provided in spaces that look and feel youth\-friendly, accessible, and protective of privacy\. Draft and in\-progress sketch; artwork is currently being revised\.

For Vancouver cartoonist James Lloyd, two messages have become stories about self\-advocacy and communication\. His developing work features Advo\-Cat, a feline companion who encourages young people to speak up when something about their care does not feel right\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/09/jlloyd2.jpg)

“Advo\-Cat arose as a wish fulfillment: The little pal I wish had been there in my childhood years to encourage me to speak up when circumstances simply weren’t right\.”

For Lloyd, the project also has a personal resonance\. “Self\-advocacy continues to be a challenge throughout my own life – as it is for many people who work creatively,” he says\. He has drawn on some of his own feelings while keeping the stories grounded in the research and the messages developed through the youth consultations\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/09/jlloyd3.jpg)

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/09/jlloyd4.jpg)

James Lloyd, pages 2 and 5, Message 3: Building a trusting relationship between patient and provider creates a safer care environment for youth\. Draft and in\-progress sketch; artwork is currently being revised\.

“I’ve always believed comics are at their best when they communicate challenging or complex subject matter in compelling terms,” Lloyd says\. He concludes: “I hope youth readers respond to these comics and feel a little less alone at their next appointment after reading them\.”
