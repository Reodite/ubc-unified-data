**A conversation with Karen Smith and Kabir Bhalla about using comics to support learning in MICB 211\.**

As part of its commitment to highlighting the many ways comics are being used in research and teaching, the UBC Comics Studies Cluster is pleased to share this project from the Department of Microbiology and Immunology\. The project offers a particularly compelling example of comics being used not simply as illustrations, but as a form of teaching and knowledge\-making\.

A team of researchers from the UBC Department of Microbiology and Immunology recently published “The Power of Narrative Storytelling: Using Comics to Bring Microbiology Concepts to Life” in the \*Journal of Microbiology &amp; Biology Education\*\. The project explores how comics can support student engagement, curiosity, and understanding by bringing complex microbiology concepts into narrative form\.

The project was led by Karen Smith, Lecturer emeritus and Honorary Lecturer in the Department of Microbiology and Immunology\. Its team brought together faculty and staff, graduate and undergraduate students, and a science education specialist\. Here, Smith and Kabir Bhalla, former project graduate academic assistant and storyline developer, discuss what inspired the project, what they learned through the creative process, and what comics can offer science education\.

## 1\. For readers who may be new to your work, could you briefly introduce the project and what inspired your team to explore comics as a way of teaching microbiology?

**KB \(KS approved\):** Our project took place in MICB 211, a foundational microbiology course at UBC, where we noticed that students were completing their assigned readings but sometimes struggled to identify key take\-home messages and apply what they had learned to case studies\. We wanted to offer students a different entry point into some of these challenging concepts, and comics immediately seemed like an exciting possibility – to make course concepts more approachable by combining humour and storytelling in ways that traditional textbooks and lectures could not\. We first asked students which concepts they found challenging and used their feedback to guide the development of three comics\.

## 2\. How did storytelling become central to your approach to teaching microbiology, rather than simply illustrating scientific concepts?

**KS:** We found that comics not only increased student engagement but also provided “aha” moments, overcoming barriers to learning some key concepts\. Comics provided a way in for students to share and discuss with others, increasing the relatability of the course concepts\. This became a natural part of seeing themselves as microbiologists, increasing classroom discussions and classroom community spirit\.

**KB:** As we created these comic narratives, we found ourselves becoming increasingly invested in and drawn into the worlds of the microbial characters, thinking about their motivations, interactions, and problems rather than just illustrating a scientific concept\. For me, this was especially exciting as I had taken MICB 211 \(formerly MICB 201\) as an undergraduate student and had always had these whimsical ideas about what some of these concepts might look like as cartoons and how different microbial processes could interact\. Storytelling gave us a way to bring the textbook to life\. Rather than showing students what a process looks like, we could offer them a situation to follow, a character to remember, or a problem to think through, with humour and narrative becoming part of the learning process\.

## 3\. What surprised you most about collaborating with comics creators and thinking through science in a visual, narrative medium?

**KS:** Once we identified the key concepts to target our comic narratives, the team handed over the reins to the story developer \(Kabir Bhalla\) and illustrator \(Craig Kornak\)\. This created an environment to unleash their full potential\. Although the initial ideas \(storyboards\) were presented back to the team for consultation, the ideas and resulting comic narratives far surpassed any expectations\. Removing any restrictions or preconceived notions initially ignited their imaginations\!

**KB:** I was surprised by how collaborative this creative process became\. Craig Kornak’s experience with cartooning brought questions and possibilities that I would not necessarily have considered\. In my role as story creator, the process was not a matter of providing drawing ideas but also having them faithfully illustrated\. We developed multiple prototypes, discussed different ways of visualizing concepts, and went back and forth\. Our illustrator, Craig, also took the initiative to learn about the scientific concepts we were representing, which made those conversations even richer\.

## 4\. Can you share a moment during the project when a comic solved a teaching problem that traditional instructional materials couldn’t?

**KS:** Our first comic, “Vancomycin Hot Tub,” targeted the differences between types of bacterial cells, which are central to understanding the nature and behaviour of different bacteria\. In lieu of repeating rote facts, the comic deftly illustrated the differences and their importance in a humorous manner\. This was far more effective and memorable than reading text or repeating facts on a slide in class\!

**KB:** The comic titled “The Dreaded Oxygen Aqua Spire” is a particularly meaningful example because it was inspired by an exam question I had encountered as an undergraduate student in MICB 211\. The question showed test tubes with microbial growth at different locations and asked students to reason what that growth revealed about microbes’ ability to survive under different oxygen concentrations\. I remember finding it quite a DREADED question myself \(pun intended\!\)\. We transformed that underlying problem into a game show featuring microbes with different oxygen tolerances, giving students a more accessible and memorable way to encounter the concept\. The humour also helped make a concept that could feel intimidating a little more approachable\. In a way, we took a question I once dreaded and turned it into something playful\.

## 5\. What do you hope comics researchers, educators, and artists take away from this project – and where do you hope these conversations lead next?

**KS:** In higher education, comics can be viewed as oversimplified, lacking depth and rigour\. Here, we have shown that this platform can be a way to deliver conceptually challenging ideas that engage learners in deeper levels of learning, by asking students to engage in the material, making deeper connections\. I hope that we can see this as an alternative strategy and an additional tool in the toolkit for any educator\.

**KB:** I hope other educators feel more comfortable considering comic\-based learning in their own educational contexts\. Comics do not need to simplify science by removing its complexity\. They can provide an accessible entry point into complex ideas while supporting meaningful conceptual engagement\. I also hope people see comics as more than illustrations, since narrative, humour, characters and visual sequencing can become part of learning design\. Most importantly, I hope that educators start with their learners’ needs and use comics intentionally to support those needs\. Students can also be collaborators and creators in this process, and I would love to see more educators, artists, students, and researchers experiment together with what comics can do across different disciplines and learning contexts\.

For the UBC Comics Studies Cluster, projects like this one demonstrate the value of bringing comics into conversations beyond the traditional boundaries of comics studies\. By sharing this work, the Cluster highlights how comics can contribute to teaching and research in fields such as microbiology, while opening further conversations about what the medium can make possible in the university\.
