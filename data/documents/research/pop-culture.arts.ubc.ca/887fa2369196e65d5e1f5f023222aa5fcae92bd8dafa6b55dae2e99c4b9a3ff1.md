Patrick Moran, Associate Professor, FHIS brings French gaming cultures into the classroom\.

Members of the UBC community gathered on March 2nd for the **Pop Pedagogies Lightning Talk Series**, an event showcasing innovative approaches to teaching with and through popular media and culture\.

Hosted by the **UBC Pop Culture Cluster as part of the** [**Pop Pedagogies Initiative**](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies/), the event celebrated the inaugural year of the [**Pop Pedagogies Award Program**](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies/award/), which supports instructors, researchers, and community partners developing creative ways to integrate popular media into teaching and learning\.

The lightning talks featured a diverse group of presenters from across the university, many of whom were awardees of the [Pop Pedagogies Award Program](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies/award/), including **[Patrick Moran](https://fhis.ubc.ca/profile/patrick-moran/), [Sara Ann Knutson](https://history.ubc.ca/profile/sara-ann-knutson/), Abbie Collett, [Alifa Bandali](https://grsj.arts.ubc.ca/profile/alifa-bandali/), [Victoria Rahbar](https://ischool.ubc.ca/profile/victoria-rahbar/), [Raymond Pai](https://asia.ubc.ca/profile/raymond-pai/), Alicia Matthews, [Cal Smith](https://english.ubc.ca/profile/cal-smith/), Andrew McIlvaney, [Stephanie Broder](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/stephanie-broder/), [Dakota Brown](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/dakota-brown/), Conan Lee, [Zoe Lam](https://asia.ubc.ca/profile/zoe-lam/), and Rosalie Gunawan\.** 

Together, the speakers demonstrated how media such as **comics, film, games, comedy and popular music** can serve as powerful pedagogical tools\. Their projects explored themes ranging from accessibility and inclusive literacy practices to diaspora, language learning, storytelling, and creative media production\. A number of presentations also demonstrated innovative models for **co\-creation**, **collaboration**, and **community engagement**\.

**Patrick Moran** explored key questions about French gaming cultures in his course on French popular fiction, “The two core questions that we’re asking in this course are, first of all, are games a form of fiction? Do games tell stories? It’s a very disputed question in the literature\. And question two, since it’s a French course on French games, are French games different from games produced in the Anglosphere? Can they tell us something different about how to tell stories and how to create fiction through games?”

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A1998.png)

Abbie Collett \(Left\), UBC History and Medieval Studies undergraduate student and Sara Ann Knutson \(Centre\), Assistant Professor of Teaching in History/Medieval Studies wait their turn to present on the representation of Vikings in popular culture\.

**Sara Ann Knutson** and **Abbie Collett** demonstrated a co\-creative pedagogical model called [“Students as Partners”](https://sap.ubc.ca/student-as-partners-partnership-toolkit/) for teaching popularly misappropriated historical figures like the Vikings: “So the partnership formed between me and Abbie, who’s a former student of the course, and together we worked to create a focused lesson plan to provide more specialized scaffolded support for student learning of popular media analysis and historicizing skills\. The result was a lesson plan for an 80\-minute discussion class, \[and\] while this lesson emerged from the course, we really wrote it with an eye to a wider audience\. So, we intend the activities to be adaptable to a variety of historical\-oriented courses that teach students how to analyze representations of the past and popular media\.” Their full lesson plan will be made available on the [**UBC Pop Pedagogies Archive**](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies-archive/)\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A1967.png)

Alifa Bandali, Assistant Professor of Teaching, GRSJ discusses Disney’s classic film disclaimers and their watered down response to racism\.

**Alifa Bandali** reminded us that it was bell hooks who said “pop culture is where the pedagogy is” before taking us through her course focused on Disney’s disclaimers on their classic films, which were released in response to racial and social justice reckonings in the 2020s\. “We wanted to ask students, and we do, to delve further and consider what this disclaimer or even this revised disclaimer is actually doing\. This is where the project, developing this disclaimer, seeks to explore the politics of media representations\.”

Accessibility and inclusion were also key themes\. **Victoria Rahbar’s work with accessible comics** demonstrated how tactile storytelling can expand literacy and open new possibilities for readers who are often excluded from traditional print cultures\. “Essentially the workshop was speaking to how the arts and popular culture can be taught as a form of multi\-entry engagement and art appreciation with a really big focus on the tactile, the touching aspect of art\. And so by the end of it our attendees left with a stronger understanding of how the arts and popular culture are not purely visual mediums but rather multi\-entry ones\.”

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A1946.png)

Raymond Pai, Lecturer in Cantonese Language and Culture, Asian Studies talks punchlines and pedagogy in Cantonese language learning\.

Several presenters highlighted how **student media creation**—including standup comedy, visual storytelling, and interactive media—can transform the classroom into a space for experimentation and collaboration\. **Raymond Pai** explained, “In our project, students are not passive recipients of jokes\. They analyze, reinterpret, and ultimately create their own comedic narratives\. \[…\] Stand\-up comedy is explicitly performative\. Language is embodied, timed, voiced and staged\. When heritage learners step on stage to tell their own stories, they are not simply speaking Cantonese\. They are performing a diasporic Cantonese identity into existence\.” **Alicia Matthews**, a student herself running a student\-led seminar on media and propaganda, emphasized the importance of empowering students to engage with the popular media they already know and inhabit: “I encourage the students to actively disagree or critique the syllabus materials, and it’s been a way to empower students by treating their lived experiences and the pop cultural phenomena that they’re personally familiar with as worthy sites of learning\.”

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2037.png)

Cal Smith, PhD Student, English Language and Literatures advocates for zine\-making as a tactile learning experience\.

A few presenters described **creative and playful storytelling** exercises that intersect with **zine forms** to provide new personalized pedagogical experiences\. **Cal Smith’s** zine\-making workshop, where students created a small autobiographical zine, demonstrated student investment when they can bring their own experience to the learning\. “I was really excited in this class because typically when I teach classes with worksheets or some other kind of material that you hand out, you’ll have this funny moment at the end of a class where students will hand back the material to you as though I want their kind of worksheet\. But this was the first class where students would keep the material\. They were making these zines and they didn’t hand them back\. They took them home\.” **Stephanie Broder’s** game\-based character creation activity allows students to explore multi\-faceted identity expressions\. “My character design exercise, usually delivered in a four\-page zine called ‘Let’s Make Characters,’ starts with a simple task\. Roll a die and draw your result as best as you can\. All these character features are designed by me to be easily copyable, even for beginner artists, as well as to introduce basic shapes as building blocks to creating unique facial features\. Participants can play in this realm for as long as they want\.”

**Andrew McIlvaney** teaches a secondary English class on **comedic writing** \(including sketchwriting, screenplays, and headlines\) and uses popular media to reveal comedic storytelling structures that do multiple kinds of work,”When we’re talking about pilots and television scripts I really like to use *The Simpsons* to teach story structure\. It’s deceptively simple, but you know, there’s so much going on\. And there’s a nice little opportunity with that as well to sort of break down the dialogue in these scripts\. They’re not just moving the plot forward, they’re also revealing something about the character or some sort of central irony or a commentary about society as well\.” **Conan Lee** also turns to **cinematic worlds** in his science\-based pedagogy workshops for secondary education: “These fictional settings are often found in sci\-fi or fantasy and immerse learners in environments that are removed from everyday experiences and provide them a safe, engaging, and most importantly culturally neutral space to explore complex issues that blend scientific, social, and ethical dimensions\.”

Other talks emphasized the importance of connecting classroom learning with public culture\. **Zoe Lam and Rosalie Gunawan** partnered in a collaboration between Lam’s Cantonese language class and the **Chinese Canadian Museum’s** exhibition “[Dream Factory: Cantopop Mandopop 1980s\-2000,](https://www.chinesecanadianmuseum.ca/exhibitions/dream-factory-cantopop-mandopop-1980s-2000)” which served as a starting point for discussions about migration, diaspora, and cultural identity\. “The goal of this collaboration is to move popular culture from the margins to the centre of language education\. We don’t just play some songs in the beginning or at the end of the class\. We want to make popular culture the centre, and we want to examine how Cantopop functions as a pedagogical tool for teaching language and identity\. We would like to foster student engagement and cultural literacy, and we also want to bridge university teaching and community knowledge through the collaboration with a local museum\.”

Taken together, the presentations illustrated how popular culture can act as a powerful bridge between academic research, student creativity, and public knowledge\.

[Image](https://phh-comicstudies-2024.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/04/182A2081.png)

Dakota Brown, MA Children’s Literature Candidate presents the Pop Pedagogies Archive\.

Graduate student **Dakota Brown**, who supports the Pop Pedagogies Archive, encouraged attendees to make use of the Archive as a resource: “The purpose is to collect and share pedagogical content that uses pop culture to think about how media connects to current issues and how it can even contribute to social and political change\. So whether you’re experimenting with creating a new unit, redesigning a course, or looking for an activity that might click for you and your class, this is meant to reduce friction and give you a good starting point to pull from\.”

The **Pop Pedagogies Initiative** continues to support educators across UBC who are experimenting with new ways of teaching through the media that shape contemporary cultural life\. Applications for the **Pop Pedagogies Award** will [re\-open in August 2026](https://pop-culture.arts.ubc.ca/pop-pedagogies/award/)\.
