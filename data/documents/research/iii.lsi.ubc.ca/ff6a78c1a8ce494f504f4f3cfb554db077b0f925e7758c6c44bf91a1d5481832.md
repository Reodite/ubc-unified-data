[https://iii2\.sites\.olt\.ubc\.ca/files/2013/05/IMG\_4483\.jpg](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/IMG_4483.jpg)[IMG\_4483](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/IMG_4483-300x225.jpg)**April 29th** was the **Day of Immunology \(DOI\)**, a day designated by immunologists around the world to bring immunology to the public\. The second year of this event was a huge success at the Life Sciences Institute again\. DOI was led by Dr\. Ninan Abraham \(I\-cubed Research Group Leader, Department of M&amp;I/ZOOL\)\. This outreach event involved graduate student volunteers to deliver the program which took place on April 26th and April 30, 2013\. The event targeted grades 5\-6 students and incurred no costs to the schools\. I\-cubed graduate student presented at the LSI to students from a Langley home\-school, and included parents who took part in the activities, then visited a second school in Kitsilano, delivering activities at their classroom\.  
The day’s activities and take home messages focused on the following key points:

1.  The microbial world is all around us
2.  The body is used to dealing with microbes all the time
3.  We can help protect ourselves with proper basic hygiene practices and by vaccinations\.

The [Prezi presentation](http://prezi.com/n-55eef9t8ws/introduction-to-microbes/?auth_key=11e1e155b8947e696eef445bf866d450f4af4ee2&amp;kw=view-n-55eef9t8ws&amp;rc=ref-14310959) about microbes, infection and the immune response was adapted by Morgan Roberts, a grad student in the Ken Harder lab \(I cubed research group, Dept of M&amp;I\)\.

**April 26\[th\] event with Lochiel U\-connect from Langley**: This is a home\-schooled group of 5\-6\[th\] graders that came to the LSI[https://iii2\.sites\.olt\.ubc\.ca/files/2013/05/IMG\_4442\.jpg](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/IMG_4442.jpg)[IMG\_4442](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/IMG_4442-225x300.jpg) with teachers and parents\. The exercises were performed in the LSI and included: microscope stations with slides to view, swabbing of plates, viewing viral plaque assays on fixed plates, and demonstrating the spread of microbes using the GloGerm kit\. After lunch, the students and parents got tours of the Flow Cytometry facility and the confocal microscope with research experiments on display\.

The DOI group noted major advantages of bringing this younger cohort of students to the UBC campus, and to the LSI\. The volunteers were able to show the students high\-end equipment and show them the open floor plan labs\. The Graduate student volunteers worked with Dr\. Abraham to develop hands\-on exercises and demonstrations\.

The off\-site Day of Immunology was on April 30\[th\] at **General Gordon Elementary**, where the LSI team of Researchers and Students provided hands\-on demonstrations to a total of 53 students in two classes \(Grade 5 and 6\)

**Summary of the Activities:  
**

1.  **Microscopes Station** – dissecting microscopes were set up with:

[https://iii2\.sites\.olt\.ubc\.ca/files/2013/05/2\.jpg](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/2.jpg)[2](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/2-300x225.jpg)

-   Pre\-grown plates with colonies\. Students observed the colonies with and without microscopes\. The week before, bacterial plates were swabbed with samples from the lab and offices, labeled and grown at 37\[o\]C\. After bacterial growth was noted, the bacteria were kept in a 4\[o\] C fridge\. There were a variety of bacteria growing since the swabbed samples included a computer keyboard, soccer ball, water from a plant, hair, and a door handle\.
-   Plaque Assays – tissue culture plates containing monolayers of animal cells were either infected with Influenza virus or uninfected\. Cells were killed by fixatives and stained to show differences in the monolayer of cells\. For example virus\-infected cells show plaques, or holes in the monolayer, where the virus has killed the cells\. Uninfected cell monolayers do not have plaques\.

**2\. Swabbing Station** – make your own plates

[https://iii2\.sites\.olt\.ubc\.ca/files/2013/05/4\.jpg](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/4.jpg)[4](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/4-300x225.jpg)

-   In partners, students were given sterile Q tips that they dipped into a conical tube of sterile water, and then used the swab to wipe a surface in the lab, collecting bacteria\.
-   The Q tips were then used to streak bacterial growth plates\. Plates were collected and returned to an LSI 37\[o\]C incubator for growth overnight\.
-   The teacher from Lochiel U\-connect was then emailed a picture of all the plates and the teachers at General Gordon School were given the plates so the students could observe them in their classrooms after the bacteria grew\.

**3\. GloGerm –** showing how infection can spread

[https://iii2\.sites\.olt\.ubc\.ca/files/2013/05/5\.jpg](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/5.jpg)[5](https://iii2.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2013/05/5-300x225.jpg)Students were exposed to GloGerm, a harmless agent, at the swabbing station\. GloGerm gel was put on the water tubes \(students were least likely to wonder why the water tubes were wet\) and when students removed the lid from the water, they were exposed\.

Later, after everyone was done at the swabbing stations, Dr\. Abraham announced that they had been exposed to a harmless glowing “germ” and determined who had been exposed by locating glowing hands using a black light\. Everyone’s hands were checked and then the class tried to figure out where the “germ” came from\. After explaining that it is a training tool, the students were directed to wash their hands\.
