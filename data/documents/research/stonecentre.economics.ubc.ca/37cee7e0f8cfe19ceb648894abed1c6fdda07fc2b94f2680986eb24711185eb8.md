**ABOUT**

---

This project examines the potential for technological adoption to produce political backlash\. Focusing on AI specifically, we argue that workers are most likely to see the technology as a threat when exposure to disruption is not matched by credible local opportunities to adapt, move into better tasks, or benefit from local economic change\. Combining local job\-posting data, occupational skill profiles, and a survey experiment, we test whether personalized information about opportunity changes how workers assess AI risk, and what they want from government in response\. 

**EXPECTED OUTCOMES**

---

This paper proposes that support for populist and anti\-globalist politics among technologically vulnerable workers and regions does not necessarily derive primarily from technological vulnerability itself, but rather from broader feelings of labour market insecurity\. Additionally, demand for political responses to technological change depends less on a worker’s vulnerability to displacement by technology, and more on whether the worker possesses skills that are transferable to other jobs available within their local labour market\. In advancing existing research, this paper will draw on observations from the literature on trade, which shows that, although the aggregate employment effects of trade liberalization have been modest, local labour markets exposed to import competition have experienced pronounced job and wage losses\. Contrary to standard assumptions, workers often remain in these declining regions, making the effects persistent, and these local shocks have been linked to increased support for protectionist and populist parties in hardest‑hit communities\.  

**HOW IT RELATES TO THE STONE CENTRE’S MISSION**

---

This project is central to understanding the causes and consequences of income and wealth inequality because it examines how two key drivers of contemporary inequality— technological change and geographic sorting—interact to shape redistributive and political attitudes\. Technological disruption can generate concentrated job losses and wage stagnation for workers whose skills are poorly matched to emerging opportunities, exacerbating local economic inequalities\. However, not all workers at risk perceive themselves at risk, raising questions about how economic shocks translate into political behaviour and demand for redistribution\. Building on evidence from trade shocks, which shows that geographically concentrated economic disruption fosters political backlash and redistributive pressure, this research clarifies the mechanisms linking technological exposure, skill alignment, and political responses\.
