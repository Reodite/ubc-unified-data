[Secret Societies and Gender\-Based Violence Against Women in Sub\-Saharan Africa](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/danny_lu_final_thesis.pdf)

**Danny Xiufeng Lu** **\| August 2026**

**Abstract**

Secret societies – associations whose members possess knowledge prohibited to non\-members – are widespread institutions across pre\-colonial and contemporary sub\-Saharan Africa\. Motivated by ethnographic and anthropological accounts describing their role in the subjugation of women and the facilitation of violence against them, I test two hypotheses: that women belonging to ethnic groups with secret societies experience greater levels of violence overall, and that they experience greater male backlash when seeking greater autonomy\. Through the construction of a new ethnicity\-level dataset reporting the presence of secret societies across sub\-Saharan Africa, and using a number of strategies – including ordinary least squares regressions, a spatial regression discontinuity design, and the analysis of a randomized controlled trial – I find evidence consistent with both hypotheses\. These results provide new empirical evidence that traditional and cultural institutions are an important yet understudied factor with respect to understanding the causes of violence against women\.

[Impact of Transitioning to a Non\-Incentivized Emergency Physician Pay Model on Emergency Department Performance, Physician Productivity, and Physician Satisfaction](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/Brian-Wall-Paper.pdf)

**Brian Wall** **\| August 2026**

**Abstract**

Emergency physicians face competing incentives to move patients quickly, complete safe and thorough care, and sustain a viable workforce\. I study a January 2024 payment reform in Victoria, British Columbia, that replaced a blended, partially volume\-linked emergency physician pay model with fixed shift\-rate pay\. Using Nanaimo as a comparison site, I estimate site\-month difference\-in\-differences models and physician\-month decompositions\. The reform slowed flow: median wait to physician increased by 46\.5 minutes, non\-admitted ED length of stay increased by 36\.6 minutes, and a standardized throughput index combining patient wait times and length\-of\-stay outcomes worsened by 1\.09 standard deviations\. Patient volume per 8\-hour\-equivalent shift fell by 1\.15 patients\. At the same time, lab orders rose by 62\.4 per 100 ED visits and hospital admission fell by 1\.41 percentage points\. I do not detect clear short\-run safety deterioration\. The reform reduced the rewarded margin, patient volume, and speed, while shifting care toward more ED\-based workup and potentially improving workforce sustainability\.

[Parental Incarceration, Its Timing, and the Effects on Child Homelessness in America](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/09/Jerry_Eiswerth_Paper.pdf)

**Jerry Eiswerth** **\| August 2025**

**Abstract**

Parental incarceration and child homelessness rates both exploded in the United States from the 1980’s to the early 2000’s\. Given that roughly half of all state and federal inmates in 2016 were parents of minor children, it may be that increases in parental incarceration are a primary cause of the rise in child homelessness\. Therefore, understanding the link between parental incarceration and child homelessness is crucial for improving child well\-being\. This study uses panel data and a series of econometric techniques to estimate the relationship between parental incarceration and child homelessness, and finds that paternal incarceration is associated with higher rates of child homelessness in general, but finds no evidence that maternal incarceration has any effect\. Further, it provides suggestive evidence that paternal incarceration may only have adverse effects if the father is incarcerated during early childhood, but provides a protective effect in middle adolescence\.

[Who Gets Pushed Out of School by Natural Disasters? Evidence from Costa Rica](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/09/Vincenzo_Trunfio_Paper.pdf)

**Vincenzo Trunfio** **\| August 2025**

**Abstract**

In this paper, I identify and solve a methodological concern present in previous related literature\. Then, using data from Costa Rica, I demonstrate that extreme weather events have negative consequences for human capital accumulation across several dimensions\. I also show the effects are heterogeneous across genders, school grades, and zones; this allows me to identify vulnerable populations\. Although my data does not allow me to rule out selective migration driving the effects on dropout rates, the patterns in the heterogeneity seem consistent with students dropping out rather than just transferring schools\.

[For Whom the Benefit Tolls: Measuring the Labor Market and Household Formation Impact of the Alberta Child Benefit](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/09/Doga_Kirilmis_Paper.pdf)

**Doga Kirilmis** **\| August 2025**

**Abstract**

In this paper, I investigate the impact of non\-labor income on parents’ activities in the labor market and in household formation\. I exploit a provincial reform to the child benefit program in Alberta in 2016\. My analysis utilizes a Differences\-in\-Differences research design with data from the Canadian Income Survey\. My results suggest a reduction in working hours for single mothers, and an increase for single fathers\. Savings fall on the individual and household level, and fertility has a small increase\. The findings provide further evidence that gender norms continue to play a role in the persistence of the gender wage gap\.
