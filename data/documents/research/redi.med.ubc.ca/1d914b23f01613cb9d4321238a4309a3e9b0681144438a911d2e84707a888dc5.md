*Research Voices from the Field* is a new feature that showcases cutting\-edge research that breaks barriers and promotes inclusion in medicine\. Each edition spotlights a research publication and includes insights directly from the authors—revealing their motivations, the significance of their findings, and why the research matters for healthcare professionals everywhere\.

In this edition, **Bonita Sawatzky**, Member of the Centre for Health Education Scholarship \(CHES\), Director of Respect, Equity, Accessibility, Diversity and Inclusion \(READI\), and Associate Professor in the Faculty of Medicine Department of Orthopaedics, reflects on why she co\-authored **“*[Lasting Impact of Patient\-Led Medical Education](https://asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tct.70147),”* highlighting the impact of patient educators in helping future physicians better understand life with chronic illness and disability\.

#### *Bonita Sawatzky, Member of the Centre for Health Education Scholarship, Director of Respect, Equity, Accessibility, Diversity and Inclusion \(READI\), and Associate Professor in the Faculty of Medicine Department of Orthopaedics, reflects on why she co\-authored *“*[Lasting Impact of Patient\-Led Medical Education](https://asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tct.70147),” highlighting the impact of patient educators in helping future physicians better understand life with chronic illness and disability\.*

As someone who has been a medical educator for over 25 years, I have seen many changes in our undergraduate medical program to ensure that physicians graduate with an understanding of their role in a complex society and among an immensely diverse population\. There’s an emphasis on being good communicators and showing empathy toward patients, yet little time is often devoted to truly listening to patients’ stories during their education\. I believe it’s important to include patient educators when they are not in crisis, so they can help future physicians understand what life with chronic health conditions or disabilities looks like outside the clinic or hospital\.

Cathy Kline and I co\-authored a paper that shows that including patients with chronic illness and disability as educators can foster greater patient\-centredness\. This aligns with DEI principles by recognizing patients not as passive recipients, but as active partners in their care\. Training that normalizes listening to these voices prepares physicians to provide more equitable care and helps counteract biases that often disadvantage patients with complex needs\.

We found that graduating medical students who participated in the optional first\-year Health Mentors Program \(HMP\), compared to those who didn’t, were more likely to integrate patients’ and caregivers’ perspectives into their care planning\. Their care plans relied less on unnecessary diagnostic testing—which could help reduce healthcare costs without compromising care or well\-being—and included more referrals to allied health professionals, reflecting a more inclusive, collaborative approach across disciplines\. In my role as READI Director for Orthopaedics, I see this as essential to DEI work, as siloed approaches to healthcare too often exclude allied health voices\.

#### [Lasting Impact of Patient\-Led Medical Education](https://asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tct.70147)

#### **Authors:** [Bonita Sawatzky](https://asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/authored-by/Sawatzky/Bonita), [Cathy Kline](https://asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/authored-by/Kline/Cathy)  

#### **Abstract**

**Background:** Our university offers an interprofessional program to medical students in Year 1 of a 4\-year undergraduate medical program: Health professional students learn from a health mentor—someone living with a chronic condition\. This helps foster patient\-centredness, empathy and communication skills\. Long\-term assessment of patient involvement in medical education is rare; thus, this study explores the lasting effects of 3\-year post\-program at entry\-to\-practice\.

**Methods**: We conducted a case\-based study of fourth\-year medical students to evaluate the impact of learning from patients in the Health Mentors Program \(HMP\)\. Students analysed a video case of a person with cerebral palsy who fell at home and created a care plan\. We compared students who participated in the HMP with those who did not, assessing how often they considered the patient’s and caregiver’s perspectives, the number of diagnostic tests ordered and referrals to other professionals and community services\.

**Findings**: *T*\-tests showed that HMP students significantly prioritised the patient’s and caregiver’s voices \(*p* \= 0\.014, Cohen’s *d* \= 0\.6\) and ordered fewer diagnostic tests than non–HMP students \(*p* \= 0\.001, Cohen’s *d* \= 3\.3\)\. However, there were no significant differences in medical consults, referrals to allied health professionals or community services\.

**Conclusions**: This was the first, limited attempt to use case\-based assessments to measure the long\-term impact of patient\-centred learning\. Integrating patient perspectives into preclinical education may enhance students’ ability to work collaboratively with patients in care planning\. Designing structured assessments around patient\-centred care can help ensure that students retain and apply these skills in their clinical careers\.
