**Investigating the relationships between the microbiota and gut osmolality**

Gut osmolality, defined as the concentration of non\-absorbed particles within the intestinal lumen, is a fundamental physical parameter regulating water flux in both mammalian and bacterial cells\. Although malabsorption\-driven increases in luminal osmolality are known to alter microbiota composition, the mechanistic responses of gut commensals to this physical stress remain poorly understood\. In this thesis, I investigate how gut bacteria respond to changes in gut osmolality at transcriptional, physiological, and phenotypic levels, using genetic engineering as both a discovery and application platform\. By expanding the genetic toolkit for *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron*, we developed a modular fluorescent transcriptional reporter circuit\. Then, by identifying promoters specifically activated under high\-osmolality conditions and integrating them into this reporter chassis, we engineered non\-invasive bacterial biosensors capable of continuously monitoring malabsorption\-associated osmotic shifts in the gut\. Beyond transcriptional responses, we identified genetic systems that confer osmotic tolerance in gut commensals and explored their transfer into osmotic\-sensitive taxa to enhance their stress resilience—an approach aimed at restoring beneficial microbes often depleted in industrialized gut environments\. Finally, we demonstrate that elevated gut osmolality triggers lysogenic phage induction, revealing a previously unrecognized environmental driver of microbiome dynamics\. Collectively, this work establishes gut osmolality as a key ecological force shaping microbial function and positions genetic engineering as a framework to dissect and reprogram microbiome\-environment interactions\.
