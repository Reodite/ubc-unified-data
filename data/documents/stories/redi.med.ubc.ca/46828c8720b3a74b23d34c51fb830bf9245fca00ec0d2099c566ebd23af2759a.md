[A Sacred Commitment to Truth and Reconciliation: From Generation to Generation](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/A-Sacred-Commitment-to-Truth-and-Reconciliation-From-Generation-to-Generation-9-940x392.png)

*Written by Derek K Thompson – Čaabať Bookwilla \| Suhiltun , Director, Indigenous Engagement in Kitsilano to celebrate the birth of his grandson, Miles Thompson, on the morning when the Thunderbirds brought him into the world\.*

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

On June 25, 2021, the University of British Columbia Faculty of Medicine offered a sincere and formal apology for its role in the continuing legacy of oppression, assimilation, and cultural genocide experienced by Indigenous peoples – First Nations, Inuit, and Métis\. That apology was not an ending\. It was a beginning\.

### “That apology was not an ending\. It was a beginning\.”

It was a moment in which the Faculty chose to turn toward the truth – to listen when the truth is difficult, to learn when the truth unsettles us, and to acknowledge the truths that Canada has too often avoided\.

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

As a First Nations person, I carry these truths not only as history, but as inheritance\. I carry them through the stories of those who came before me\. I carry them in the experiences of my family and community\. I carry the memories of what was taken, what was denied, what was endured, and what survived\.

But I also carry something else\. I carry the extraordinary strength of those who came before me\. I carry their resilience, their perseverance, their teachings, their love for their families, and their determination to protect culture, language, land, community, and identity\. And I carry their hope that the generations who follow us might inherit something better\.

Today, that responsibility has become profoundly personal in a new way\. **I have just become a grandfather for the first time\.**

On the morning of August 14 the Thunderbirds arrived commanding the world to welcome my grandson, Miles Thompson\. Life is an amazing and mysterious thing – my Mom, Maude, passed away on Friday, March 14, 2025, and my grandson was born on Friday, August 14\. I cried tears of pure happiness and tender sadness\. I cried in honor of my Mom, now a great\-grandmother\. I cried for my son, Chazz, now a father for the first time\. And I cried at this most honorable of transformations, me, now a grandfather\.

[Miles Thompson](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/Miles-Thompson_14-August-2026_DKT-720x960.jpg)

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

With that new title comes a new understanding of what reconciliation means\. When I look into the eyes of my grandson, I do not simply see the future\. I see generations\. I see the Ancestors who came before us\. I see the generations who carried us through hardship\. I see my own generation and the responsibilities we have inherited\. And I see a child who deserves to grow into a world where being Indigenous is not accompanied by the burden of racism, discrimination, exclusion, or the denial of dignity\.

### “I do not simply see the future\. I see generations\. I see the Ancestors who came before us, and I see a child who deserves to grow into a world where being Indigenous is not accompanied by the burden of racism, discrimination, exclusion, or the denial of dignity\.”

A child who deserves to know their identity with pride\. A child who deserves to know their culture, their history, their language, their community, and their people\. A child who deserves to enter a health\-care system where they are seen, heard, respected, and safe\. A child who deserves to inherit not only the truth of our history, but the possibility of a new future\.

That is why Truth and Reconciliation has become even more deeply personal for me\. It is no longer only about what happened to previous generations\. It is about what we choose to leave for the generations yet to come\.

For me, reconciliation is an act of love\. It is love expressed through responsibility\. It is love expressed through courage\. It is love expressed through the willingness to confront difficult truths so that our children and grandchildren do not have to carry the same burdens\.

### “For me, reconciliation is an act of love\. It is love expressed through responsibility\. It is love expressed through courage\.”

The apology offered by the Faculty of Medicine on June 25, 2021, matters because words can open a door\. But reconciliation requires us to walk through that door\. It requires us to transform relationships\. It requires us to challenge racism wherever it exists\. It requires us to examine the systems, structures, assumptions, and practices that continue to create inequity for Indigenous peoples\.

It requires us to listen to Indigenous voices – not simply when they are invited into the room, but when decisions are being made\. It requires Indigenous knowledge, leadership, cultures, rights, and ways of knowing to be respected not as additions to the work of medicine, but as essential to creating a healthier future\.

And it requires institutions to understand that reconciliation is not something that can be completed\. It is a relationship\. It is a responsibility\. It is a lifelong journey\.

### “Reconciliation is not something that can be completed\. It is a relationship\. It is a responsibility\. It is a lifelong journey\.”

As a Nuuchahnulth grandfather – as a *dad* *īqs*, I find myself thinking differently about the word *legacy*\. Legacy is not simply what we inherit\. **Legacy is what we leave behind\.**

The generation before us gave us teachings, stories, resilience, and survival\. Our responsibility is to carry those gifts forward while changing what must be changed\. And now, as a grandfather, I have another responsibility – to help create a future that my grandson can inherit with pride\.

I want my grandson, and my grandchildren, to grow up knowing that the truth was not hidden from them\. That their Ancestors were not forgotten\. That their suffering was not dismissed\. That their resilience was honored\. That their culture was valued\. And that there were people and institutions willing to change because they understood that reconciliation is ultimately about the dignity and wellbeing of generations yet unborn\.

There are many Indigenous peoples, families, and communities who continue to carry profound hurt and grief\. We honor those who have been lost\. We honor those who continue to grieve\. We honor survivors and their families\. We honor the knowledge keepers, Elders, leaders, parents, grandparents, aunties, uncles, and young people who have carried Indigenous identity and culture forward despite generations of attempts to suppress it\.

And we honor the strength that made it possible for our children and grandchildren to still be here\.

We also recognize those within our institutions and settler communities who have chosen to listen, learn, stand beside Indigenous peoples, and accept the responsibility to become part of a different future\.

But standing together does not mean that we all carry the same history\. It means that we recognize the history honestly\. It means that we make room for one another’s truths\. It means that those who have experienced harm should not also be asked to carry the responsibility for fixing the systems that caused it\. And it means that those of us who have the ability to change those systems must have the courage to do so\.

For the Faculty of Medicine, this commitment must live at the very heart of our purpose\. It must shape how we educate, how we research, how we practice, how we listen, how we lead, and how we understand health itself\.

Because health is more than the absence of disease\. Health is belonging\. Health is safety\. Health is dignity\. Health is identity\. Health is connection to family, community, culture, land, spirit, and one another\.

### “Health is more than the absence of disease\. Health is belonging\. Health is safety\. Health is dignity\. Health is identity\.”

And for Indigenous peoples, these connections have too often been disrupted by systems that were never designed with us, for us, or alongside us\.

Reconciliation asks us to build something different—something grounded in relationship, respect, reciprocity, accountability, and love\.

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

Perhaps this is why the image of a tree speaks so powerfully to me\.

A tree may have many branches, but it is sustained by its roots\. Our institutions may have many faculties, disciplines, professions, programs, and people\. But beneath them are relationships, histories, responsibilities, and connections that sustain us all\.

The roots remind us where we have come from\. The trunk represents the strength we build together\. The branches reach toward the future\. And the youngest leaves remind us why we must continue to grow\.

**My grandson is one of those leaves\.**

## “**My grandson is one of those leaves\.**”

And when I think about the future of medicine, I think about the world that child will inherit\.

I want that world to be different\.

Not because we have forgotten the past, but because we have finally learned from it\. Not because the truth has become easier, but because we have become courageous enough to face it\. Not because reconciliation has been completed, but because generation after generation has chosen to continue the work\.

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

The Faculty’s commitment to Truth and Reconciliation is therefore more than an institutional commitment\. It is a commitment to relationship\. It is a commitment to justice\. It is a commitment to healing\. And, for me, it is a commitment to the generations\.

### “The Faculty’s commitment to Truth and Reconciliation is more than an institutional commitment\. It is a commitment to relationship\. It is a commitment to justice\. It is a commitment to healing\.”

To those who came before us\. To those who walk beside us\. And to those who are waiting for us in the future\.

I now have a grandchild who will one day ask me about the world we lived in and the choices we made\.

I hope I can tell them that we did not turn away\.

That when we learned the truth, we listened\. That when we saw injustice, we acted\. That when relationships were broken, we worked to repair them\. That when racism appeared, we confronted it\. That when Indigenous peoples spoke, we made room to hear them\. And that when we were given the opportunity to leave the world better than we found it, **we chose to do so\.**

The work ahead remains immense\. The journey remains lifelong\. But there is hope\.

There is hope in every relationship strengthened\. There is hope in every truth spoken\. There is hope in every barrier removed\. There is hope in every Indigenous student who sees themselves belonging in medicine\. There is hope in every health professional who learns to understand Indigenous peoples with greater humility and respect\. There is hope in every family that experiences a health system that is safer than the one that came before\. And there is hope in every child and grandchild who can grow up knowing that their identity is a source of strength, not something they must defend\.

**I want my grandchild to inherit more than our memories of what was lost\. I want them to inherit what we had the courage to build\.**

[Leaf divider](https://med-fom-cpp-2022.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/08/light-yellow-divider-leaf-2-360x91.png)

So we remember\.

We listen\.

We learn\.

We act\.

We heal\.

We walk together\.

And we carry this responsibility forward—from generation to generation\.

For the Ancestors who carried us\.

For the generations who stand beside us\.

And for the grandchildren who will one day ask what we did with the truth we were given\.

**May they look back upon this generation and know that we chose courage over silence, relationship over distance, action over intention, and hope over resignation\.**

**May they know that we did not simply speak of reconciliation\. We lived it\.**

And may they inherit a future in which the roots of our shared humanity are strong enough to sustain every branch, every community, and every generation yet to come\.
