This past summer, [Dr\. Ben Britton](https://mtrl.ubc.ca/ben-britton/)‘s group welcomed Channing Chen, a second\-year Mechanical Engineering student at UBC, as an undergraduate research assistant, funded through NSERC USRA\. Working alongside Graeme Francolini, Channing developed a profilometry system that can map surface topography and measure material removal on a sample surface, with the end goal of integrating it into a robot\-enabled microscopy suite that automates characterization\.

> **“I never expected this USRA experience to go beyond doing basic lab assistant tasks\. Having my own independent project, with the creative freedom to design and fabricate a system for use in a professional laboratory, was truly a pleasant surprise\.”**

---

********What motivated you to apply for an NSERC USRA with Dr\. Ben Britton’s group, and how did you first make aware of the opportunity?********

When I first joined Dr\. Ben Britton’s group as a research assistant back in January, he mentioned the possibility of continuing my work through an NSERC Undergraduate Student Research Award \(USRA\), which funds students to gain research experience\. The idea of spending my summer months fully dedicated to what had become my passion project instantly motivated me\. I like to think of myself as a builder, spending most of my time outside my academics designing CAD models or writing code\.

> ***“I have always had a desire to enter research but never knew where to begin, especially as an undergraduate\. A national grant that would support me in pursuing my work full\-time was an opportunity I knew I had to take my shot at\.”***

********Has this experience matched up with what you had in mind before you started?********

My USRA experience in Dr\. Ben Britton’s laboratory has definitely exceeded my expectations\. Within the first month of my USRA, I gained training in material polishing for SEM sample preparation and became comfortable operating within a materials research environment – this alone would have met my expectations\.

**********Can you tell me what your research is about?**********

Working closely with my mentor who is a PhD candidate in the group, Graeme Francolini, I developed a profilometry system that can map surface topography and measure material removed from a sample surface, with the end goal of implementing it within the lab’s robot\-enabled microscopy suite that automates characterization\. This involved designing components in SolidWorks and programming Python\-based control to integrate an interferometric sensor and a precision stage into an optical microscope\. Alongside this, I’ve also had the opportunity to meet the other researchers within the research group, whether it be shadowing imaging sessions on a scanning electron microscope or conversing during weekly group meetings\.

**********How did this hands\-on research complement what you’ve been learning in your undergraduate coursework?**********

Developing my project contextualized my Mechanical Engineering coursework by transforming concepts I had only seen in a lecture hall into real\-world, physical applications\. Understanding three\-dimensional planes was essential to designing surface removal measurement tools\. Even the matrices from my first\-year linear algebra course were the foundation for calculating the pixel\-millimeter conversion for a click\-to\-move function\.

> ****“Through working on my NSERC USRA, I found the intersection between my interests in mechanical design, software engineering, and materials research that I had been searching for\.”****

************Advanced characterization is a research field that can sometimes feel daunting to undergraduates – do you agree?  How has working in this field changed your view?************

I completely agree that it feels intimidating at first\. At the start of my undergraduate degree, I didn’t have a single clue what “Electron Backscatter Diffraction \(EBSD\)” or “Scanning Electron Microscopy \(SEM\)” meant\. They seemed merely theoretical and completely distant from my life\. However, after joining this group, speaking with the team’s researchers at weekly meetings, and receiving training for the laboratory’s microscopy tools, I soon discovered how applicable it can be to everyday life\.  
  
I remember during one meeting, our research group collectively read and discussed a materials paper, and vehicle crumple zones came up – a topic I was familiar with and had personally explored\. I quickly realized that advanced characterization is what helps us better understand the materials required for our world’s energy, manufacturing, transport, and electronics\.

**************What is the most fascinating or unexpected thing you’ve discovered while working with advanced characterization tools?**************

To be completely blunt, the most unexpected thing I’ve discovered while using advanced characterization tools was how intricate and expensive they are\. It’s still astounding to me that the machines in the laboratory I’m in every day cost the same as a house \(although maybe not in Vancouver\)\. Even the precision stage and confocal interferometer I was personally integrating, components small enough to fit into your palm, are incredibly complex works of engineering capable of micrometer precision\.

****************Looking back, what were your biggest takeaways from the USRA experience?****************

> *****“My biggest takeaway from my USRA is understanding that failure is feedback\. Although it’s a rather cliché phrase to say in day\-to\-day life, it was a key notion I overlooked early on\.”*****

Feedback exists in the context of technology systems, where it’s a process that feeds output back into the system to correct issues\. Ensuring that this was always implemented led me to solve many of the problems while developing my work, such as when I needed to link a precision stage that wasn’t designed for Linux to the microscopy suite’s computer\. Beyond programming, at the end of every month, I would also have a check\-in meeting with my supervisors, where they would provide me with input to improve my work and set me on the right track\. This feedback loop is what helped my project grow to where it is now\.

******************What advice would you give to other undergrads who are considering applying for a USRA?******************

One crucial piece of advice I have for other undergraduates is not to wait before applying for a USRA or a research position in general\. I know that a lot of students often hold off until they’ve developed further experience or learned about the specific industry tools\. However, the truth is that not only do you acquire many of the necessary skills on the job, but oftentimes the knowledge that you already possess can prove to be quite significant in what you do\.

> ******“A USRA is designed to develop your interest and potential for research, not to test it\.”******
