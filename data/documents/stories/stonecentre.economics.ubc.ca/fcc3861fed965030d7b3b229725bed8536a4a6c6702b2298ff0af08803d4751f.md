*Thomas Lemieux is a professor at the Vancouver School of Economics and Director of the Stone Centre for Wealth and Income Inequality\. **He spoke with research assistants Sophia Bertuzzi Samilski, and Callysta Limeus\.***

**How would you describe the state of inequality in Canada right now?**

It’s high\. In most countries, it’s pretty high\. Not as high as some other countries, like our southern neighbors, but the trends I would say over the last couple of decades have been going up\. 

**Why should people care about inequality?**

In economics, we tend to focus a lot on average annual GDP per capita — a simple measure of the wealth of countries, of the standards of living, etc\. — but, I think intuitively to almost anyone, it’s very clear that if you have a thousand people and we all earn comparable incomes versus a case where one person is a billionaire and all the others are barely surviving, most of us would pick the first society\. We’re social animals\. We care about each other\. It’s not just our own well\-being that counts, but also the society’s\.

**Who’s involved with the Stone Centre?**

From the very beginning, we had a leadership team of three of us — David Green, Nicole Fortin, and I — all colleagues at the Vancouver School of Economics\. The three of us have been working closely since the very beginning\. 

We have a large number of faculty affiliates — many of them here in the Vancouver School of Economics, but we’re also taking a very multidisciplinary approach\. 

We have several affiliates from other departments in the Faculty of Arts — including sociology, psychology, political science — and also with a few affiliates from the Allard School of Law and Sauder School of Business\.

We tend to have one or two postdocs a year, and about ten PhD fellows who receive research funding to support their dissertation work\. We also have five to ten fellows in the inequality stream of our MA program\.

[Image](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/07/DSCF0821-1024x731.jpg)

**What does economics bring to the study of inequality?**

We take all the statistical econometric methods to study wages — and the determination of income and inequality more generally — very seriously\. Economic thinking gives you a way of thinking very clearly about the underlying forces behind inequality; we really want to see the underlying root causes, or the causal factors\. Economics is well\-equipped for using statistics and measurement, and also thinking about models to better understand the root cause of inequality\. But there are many other issues where we’re not as well\-equipped\.

**Where do other disciplines come in?**

Firstly, at the Stone Centre, we’re looking at both the causes and consequences of inequality and also potential remedies\. Political science is very important, especially when you start thinking about the consequences of inequality and how it potentially leads to political polarization\. When you have an extreme concentration of income and wealth — which means that some people have extreme economic and political power — it starts undermining a democratic society\. 

Second, we are social animals\. Sociologists think about this quite deeply, and psychologists help us understand why, as humans, we care about others, worry about others, and feel that a very unequal society is not what we want\. 

Finally, you can think of inequality as a moral issue\. I think most people would agree that it’s an important moral issue\. As an economist, how do I think about morality? I’ll go talk to the philosopher first, because I don’t know where to start from\.

[Image](https://stonecentre.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/07/DSCF4591-1024x683.jpg)

**Is there anything on the horizon that could reshape inequality research?**

These days, there have been different waves of technological change\. Right now, it’s AI, which is the big name in town that could actually have a major impact on the kind of work we’re doing\. We don’t know yet whether it’s going to expand or reduce inequality, or maybe not have such a huge impact\.
