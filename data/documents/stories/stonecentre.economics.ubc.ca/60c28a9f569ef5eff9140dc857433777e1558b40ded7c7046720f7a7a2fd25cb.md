*Danny Lu was a 2025\-2026 MA Thesis Fellow\. **He spoke with research assistant Bhavya Dubey\.***

**When you started your thesis on the historical roots of inequality, what was the biggest gap in your understanding that you feel you’ve successfully bridged this year?** 

This is a difficult question to answer because, when I started my thesis, I would say that everything about the historical roots of inequality was one big gap in my understanding\. Given the nature of my thesis, I think I’ve learned the most about gender inequality\. 

**How has your work with Nathan Nunn affected your own research, and which pieces of his research were most impactful to your training?** 

I’ve been very fortunate to have taken classes with, worked for, and been advised by Nathan, all of which has been very influential in different ways\. As a student, I found his course and research on comparative development entirely eye\-opening, both in terms of learning how ignorant I was beforehand, and in terms of the range of questions that economists could answer\. These played a big part in my decision to pursue a PhD in economics, and they definitely sparked my interest in economic history\. Otherwise, as his advisee and research assistant, I’ve learnt a great deal about the nature of research\. He always has the answers to my questions, and through working on my thesis and our joint project, I’ve developed a much better sense of the type of researcher I want to be, as well as the questions I am interested in\. 

**How has the interdisciplinary nature of the Stone Centre’s seminars expanded how you think about your own research?** 

I’ve enjoyed this aspect of the seminars\. It’s always interesting to learn how other fields have different ways of answering the same questions economists may be interested in\. In this regard, my ideas often form after reading an interesting qualitative piece of research\. I hope the seminars continue to incorporate fields beyond the social sciences in the future\!

**Your research examines how pre\-colonial secret societies correlate with contemporary gender\-based violence in Sub\-Saharan Africa\. Beyond the data, what was the most striking insight you found about the effects of these institutions?** 

That, despite what their name might suggest, pre\-colonial secret societies were widely known, well documented, and empirically unexplored\. Although I focus on one potential consequence of their existence, I think there are certainly other questions one could answer in relation to this institution\. 

**What other interesting ideas would you like us to share about your research completed as a Stone Fellow?** 

The main questions my thesis answers are related to and motivated by a literature documenting how secret societies facilitate violence against women\. I find evidence suggesting that this relationship exists, and that there are potential policy implications\. However, given that these institutions were often a dominant form of social structure where present, it would be interesting to test whether there are also any benefits\. For instance, there may be greater prosocial norms within genders\. This would be consistent with the idea that, for an institution to persist, it should have some beneficial function\.
