*Brian Wall was a 2025\-2026 MA Thesis Fellow\. **He spoke with research assistant Bhavya Dubey\.***

**With experience in both frontline medical and economic research, which ideas do you believe these fields could offer to each other?**  
Frontline medicine exposes problems that matter deeply for patients and health systems but are not easily answered by traditional clinical research methods such as randomized trials\. Economics offers tools to study natural experiments and still make credible causal claims about policy choices\. Medical professionals often hold themselves to high standards when applying evidence to individual patient care, but health policy decisions can have even larger system\-wide effects and are not always held to the same evidentiary standard; economics research helps fill that gap\.

  
**If other regions want to move away from paying doctors solely based on patient volume, what are the biggest challenges they will face in making that transition work?**  
The biggest challenge is preventing slower throughput from becoming an unacceptable access problem\. Regions need to pair payment reform with explicit safeguards: monitoring wait times, length of stay, patients leaving without being seen, admissions, safety outcomes, and physician staffing\. They also need internal fairness and transparency, because changing pay changes both incentives and how physicians perceive the distribution of effort and reward\.  

**Looking ahead, how can hospitals ensure that removing volume\-based incentives doesn’t end up slowing down care as local communities continue to grow?**  
Hospitals should not remove volume incentives and assume operations will take care of themselves\. They need demand\-responsive staffing, real\-time flow monitoring, clear escalation protocols, and ongoing review of whether patients are waiting longer, leaving before assessment, or returning sicker\. A balanced model may be needed: one that supports thorough care and physician sustainability while still protecting timely access for patients\.

**How has your collaboration with Sam Norris affected the work you did as a Stone Fellow, and the questions you targeted in your research?**  
Working with Professor Norris helped me turn frontline clinical experience into sharper, more defensible economic questions\. He pushed me away from asking whether the reform was simply good or bad and toward understanding the trade\-offs it created across throughput, workup, safety, and physician workforce sustainability\. His economics expertise helped transform observations from practice into a rigorous empirical framework\.  

**If you had the chance to present your thesis findings to a room full of policymakers today, what is the one core message you would want them to walk away with?  
**Physician payment models change what the health system produces\. Removing volume\-based incentives may slow patient flow, but it can also shift care toward more complete emergency\-department workup and may support a more sustainable physician workforce\. Policymakers should treat compensation reform as a multidimensional design choice, not a simple productivity lever\.
