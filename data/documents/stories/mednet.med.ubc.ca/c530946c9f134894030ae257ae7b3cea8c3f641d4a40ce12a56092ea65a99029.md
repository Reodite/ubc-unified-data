Meet Dr\. Bonita Sawatzky, Associate Professor in the Department of Orthopaedics &amp; the Canfield Distinguished Scholar in Patient Partnerships\.

She/her

Associate Professor

### How long have you worked at the UBC Faculty of Medicine?

Since 1985\!

My first role in the Faculty of Medicine was as a summer research student in the Department of Medical Genetics\. After two summers, I got my first full time job with the Department of Surgery at the BC Children’s Hospital Spinal Cord Clinic, running the clinic database, before moving on to work as a research assistant in the Department of Orthopaedics\. While working there I completed my Masters and PhD, and became a faculty member in the Department in 1998\.

### **Tell us what you do at the Faculty in one or two sentences:**

I run a research program at ICORD looking at the biomechanics of mobility for people with spinal cord injuries, as well as leading a patient\-partner international registry for adults with arthrogryposis\. In 2022, I closed my mobility research lab to focus more on my role as Respect, Equity, Accessibility, Diversity &amp; Inclusion \(READI\) Director in Orthopaedics, as well as other initiatives in the MD undergraduate program — including my appointment as the Canfield Distinguished Scholar for Patient Partners in 2025\.

### **As the Canfield Distinguished Scholar in Patient Partnerships, what are your goals or aspirations?**

The Canfield Distinguished Scholar in Patient Partnerships role supports research, teaching and outreach initiatives in patient partnerships in health professional education\. The honorific was established thanks to a generous donation from long\-time educator, Carolyn Canfield, and is supported by the [Centre for Health Education Scholarship](https://ches.med.ubc.ca/about-ches/) \(CHES\) and the [Patient and Community Partnership for Education](https://health.ubc.ca/pcpe) at UBC Health\.

Patient partners are a well\-established part of our undergraduate medical education program, and they serve an important role in helping learners consider our health care system from the patient’s perspective and what can be done to move towards a better and healthier patient experience\.

However, there is evidence that as learners progress in their postgraduate training, there is less emphasis on the patient perspective\. So, in this role, my goal is to include more learning from patients into postgraduate medical training\. I am currently working on an academic training session featuring surgeons who have themselves been surgical patients\. The session, which incorporates creative elements like imagery and art, is designed to showcase the patient’s voice from a peer perspective\.

### What’s one thing we might be surprised to learn about you?

I love being outside and spent 20 years sailing each summer with the Jericho Disabled Sailing club — many of those years as part of the racing club\.

### Are there any research collaborators you’d like to acknowledge and why?

I’d like to acknowledge my newest colleague from Kings College London, Dr\. Kat Low, who is a researcher and educator in Arts and Humanities in Art in Medicine\. She is teaching me — a quantitative researcher — how to teach in a more creative fashion\. I’m learning so much\!

### What is your favourite quote and why?

“I’m ok, I’ve never been better\!” This was part of a story a colleague shared when he took a group of scouts on a hike when he realised a girl was missing\. He called out to her and she said, “I’m down here\!” \(She slid off the trail\)\. Then she said, “I’m ok, I’ve never been better\!” Apparently, she is quite a little trooper\.

I’ve had a lot of challenges in my life and when it gets rough and my husband asks how I am, I say, “I’m ok, never been better\!”
