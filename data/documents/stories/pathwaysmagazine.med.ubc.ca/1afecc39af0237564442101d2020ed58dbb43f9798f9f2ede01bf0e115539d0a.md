##   
**Every doctor once had a mentor\.**  
  
**Someone who taught them how to heal, save lives and lead with compassion\.  
  
Every day across British Columbia, nearly 13,000 clinical faculty are delivering care on the front lines while training the next generation of doctors, researchers and health professionals\.  
  
Here are a few of their stories\.**

[Image](https://med-fom-pathways-2025.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/01/Shreya-Moodley_Carine-VanderMerwe_Pediatrics_DayLife-2-FT.png)

**Dr\. Shreya Moodley**

Vancouver, Lower Mainland

8:00 a\.m\.

UBC clinical assistant professor Dr\. Shreya Moodley \(center\) and postdoctoral trainee Dr\. Carine van der Merwe examine their first patient of the day, seven\-year\-old Kita Kobayashi\.

It’s early, and already the sounds of shuffling feet and childish laughter fill the lobby of the BC Children’s Hospital Heart Centre\. Inside, UBC clinical assistant professor Dr\. Shreya Moodley and postdoctoral trainee Dr\. Carine van der Merwe examine their first patient of the day, seven\-year\-old Kita Kobayashi\.

Kita was born with transposition of the great arteries — a rare heart defect where the two main arteries are connected to the wrong chambers, preventing blood from getting oxygen\. She had corrective surgery at birth and has been under the care of Dr\. Moodley ever since\.

While Dr\. van der Merwe checks Kita’s pulse, Dr\. Moodley chats with Kita about her favourite colour, food, subject in school and what she likes to do for fun\. Kita’s answers come easy — green, chocolate, gym and gymnastics\.

[Image](https://med-fom-pathways-2025.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/01/Shreya-Moodley_Carine-VanderMerwe_Pediatrics_DayLife-3.png)

*Because pediatric heart defects like TGA are rare and can be fatal, there are few opportunities to practice life\-saving procedures\. To enhance education and patient care, Dr\. Moodley and her team at BC Children’s Hospital have created a 3D printed, patient\-specific heart for safely practicing complex pediatric cardiac procedures\.*

Heart appointments can be long and technical — especially for kids — often involving ultrasounds, electrocardiograms and physical exams\. For parents, they can be filled with anxiety as they wait to find out whether their child is ok\. That’s why Dr\. Moodley always makes a point of connecting with patients and their families as part of the examination — and training\.

“Trainees watch how we speak, how we listen and how we respond in moments of difficulty,” she says\. “The technical skills are important, but it’s also important to understand that the practice of medicine extends beyond diagnosis and treatment to the heart of the human experience\.”

During the final heart check, Dr\. Moodley says that Kita’s tests look good and that she’ll see her next year\. Dr\. van der Merwe congratulates Kita with a high\-five\. Before the doctors leave, Kita smiles and reaches for Dr\. Moodley’s stethoscope\. She hands it to Kita and shows her how to use it\.

“Can you hear that?” says Dr\. Moodley, watching Kita’s expression as the little girl puts the stethoscope to her own chest\.

“Yeah,” says Kita, smiling\. It’s the first time she is hearing her own heartbeat\.

**Dr\. Aryn Khan**

Vanderhoof, Northern B\.C\.

10:55 a\.m\.

UBC medical students Jason van Zyl \(left\) and Justin Grenier observe as UBC clinical faculty member Dr\. Aryn Khan performs a minor procedure at her family medicine clinic in Vanderhoof\.

It’s a bright, cold morning in Vanderhoof and Dr\. Aryn Khan is busy seeing patients at her family medicine clinic\. Joined by UBC medical students Justin Grenier and Jason van Zyl, she moves from exam room to exam room — supporting a patient with an atypical pregnancy, performing a minor procedure, and treating a sports injury\.

At every stop, she encourages the students to get involved, honing their skills by taking patient histories, assisting with procedures, and making small talk\. Along the way, Dr\. Khan gives and receives plenty of hugs\.

“The thing about rural medicine is that you get to do everything and you get to know everyone,” she says\. “I honestly love what I do and couldn’t see myself doing anything else\.”

For the next six months, Justin and Jason will join Dr\. Khan in all aspects of her practice as part of the Northern Regional Integrated Clerkship pilot program — which also includes rotations in obstetrics, emergency medicine, palliative care and more\.

The program, which officially launches this summer, offers third\-year UBC medical students in the Northern Medical Program the opportunity to split their annual training between a northern community like Vanderhoof and the University Hospital of Northern British Columbia in Prince George\.

“I hope that by teaching students through my eyes and passion, they fall in love with rural medicine like I have,” says Dr\. Khan\.

**Bridget Mensah**

Surrey, Lower Mainland

1:30 p\.m\.

UBC clinical instructor Bridget Mensah \(center\) and her team guide UBC midwifery student Mpho Holele \(right\) during a childbirth training simulation\.

As Surrey Memorial Hospital bustles with patients and activity, Bridget Mensah and her team are guiding fourth\-year UBC midwifery student Mpho Holele through a childbirth training simulation that will prepare her for real\-world emergencies\.

During the simulation, Mpho encounters shoulder dystocia — a potentially life\-threatening situation where a baby’s shoulder becomes lodged in the pelvic bone after the head is delivered\. She quickly jumps into action by moving the mother, role\-played by a fellow colleague, into a different position\.

“Very good,” Mensah says, offering encouragement and advice as Mpho uses the rotational techniques she’s learned to successfully deliver the simulation baby\.

A registered nurse and midwife from Ghana, Mensah was part of UBC’s bridging program, which prepares internationally educated midwives to register and practice in Canada, strengthening the workforce with their global expertise and experience\. She now teaches UBC midwifery students at her clinic in Surrey alongside seven colleagues — each one a former UBC student she mentored\.

“Everyone deserves a teacher who can see their potential and watch them excel,” says Mensah\. “It’s such a rewarding experience\.”

**Dr\. Johann Nel**

Comox Valley, Vancouver Island

3:30 p\.m\.

At the Comox Valley Hospital Emergency Department, UBC medical student Avery McNair performs an abdominal ultrasound on a patient under the guidance of UBC clinical faculty member Dr\. Johann Nel\.

It’s mid\-afternoon and the ER at North Island Hospital Comox Valley is hectic\. Amid the buzz, Dr\. Johann Nel and third\-year UBC medical student Avery McNair are assessing a patient with abdominal pain\.

From there, they quickly pivot to treating a young patient with an injured ankle while awaiting the results of another patient’s chest X\-ray\.

“I enjoy doing my job while teaching,” says Dr\. Nel\. “These are really bright students who also have the latest information, so I learn from them as well\.”

Like many rural doctors, Dr\. Nel practices both family and emergency medicine, enhancing both the scope of patient care and educational training opportunities\.

During his decade of teaching in Comox, Dr\. Nel and his colleagues have trained dozens of UBC family medicine resident doctors, many whom have stayed to practice\.

Last year, the Faculty of Medicine expanded its [Integrated Community Clerkship \(ICC\)](https://mdprogram.med.ubc.ca/about/integrated-community-clerkships/) program to Comox, giving third\-year undergraduate medical students like Avery 12 months of immersive training in a rural community\. Dr\. Nel says the new program is crucial to inspiring even more graduates to stay and practice\.

“Rural places really need healthcare providers,” explains Dr\. Nel\. “When students have these opportunities to come here and experience all the avenues of rural practice and the town, they really love it\.”

**Jayme Peel**

Prince Rupert, Northern B\.C\.

5:15 p\.m\.

UBC clinical instructor Jayme Peel trains up to 14 health professional students in rural practice each year at the Prince Rupert Interprofessional Student\-led Model Clinic\. The clinic is delivered in partnership with the University of Northern British Columbia and the Northern Health Authority\.

In the vibrant port city of Prince Rupert, occupational therapist \(OT\) Jayme Peel and UBC OT student Kendall Ferguson are practicing taking specialized measurements for a mock wheelchair assessment at the Prince Rupert Interprofessional Student\-led Model Clinic \(PRISM\)\. Getting precise measurements is essential in preventing patients from falling or developing bed sores, circulatory problems or spinal misalignment that can lead to chronic pain and deformities\.

“Our role is to help people get back to living their lives as independently as possible,” says Peel\. “That can mean teaching an older adult how to dress and cook by themselves, recommending adaptive equipment after an injury or supporting someone experiencing mental health challenges\.”

The UBC clinic — which is one of several innovative student\-led models across B\.C\. — also encourages team\-based care with UBC OT and physical therapy \(PT\) students training and working together to provide optimal care for patients\. For example, while a PT may help a patient gain strength and mobility after a fall, the OT can ensure home safety through routine changes and home modifications\.

“Getting students into that interdisciplinary mindset early on is important,” explains Peel\. “Providing excellent care for someone can take a whole healthcare team working together towards a single goal — which is to improve the health and quality of life for the patient\.”

Student wellness is also a top priority for Peel, who trains approximately 14 students each year\.

“Incorporating work\-life balance is essential to avoiding burnout,” she explains\. “I always emphasize to students the importance of taking care of yourself so that you can provide the best care for others\.”

**Dr\. Jared Baylis**

Kelowna, Interior B\.C\.

7:45 p\.m\.

UBC clinical faculty member Dr\. Jared Baylis guides UBC medical students during an emergency medicine simulation session\. *Each year, UBC students and trainees* prepare for real\-world practice with high\-fidelity mannequins, virtual reality and standardized patient programs\.

Nearly 1,400 kilometers away, in B\.C\.’s Interior, Dr\. Jared Baylis is running an emergency medicine simulation session for UBC medical students Benjamin Koenig, Julia Speirs and Nathan Loo at the Kelowna General Hospital \(KGH\)\.

As the simulation mannequin goes into cardiac arrest, Dr\. Baylis guides the students through each critical step, including monitoring vitals, chest compressions and intubation\.

“There can be a lot of high\-stakes situations in the ER where you have to work together as a team and make decisions quickly,” says Dr\. Baylis\. “I want medical students and residents to feel ready when these situations arise — and simulation offers them a safe place to practice, perfect their skills and ask questions\.”

Dr\. Baylis was one of the first residents to complete his training in UBC’s Emergency Medicine Residency Program in Kelowna when it launched in 2014\. The experience inspired his passion for teaching and innovation — and his decision to stay and join the Faculty after his studies\. “I had amazing mentors at UBC who inspired me to teach the next generation of emergency doctors\.”

The training lasts more than an hour and feels almost as intense as the real thing — which is the point\. By the end, the students are feeling the weight of the long hours but are energized by the new knowledge and skills they’ve gained\. Dr\. Baylis and the students chat and share a few final reflections before saying their goodbyes and heading home for the night\.

## **There are many more stories just like these ones\. Stories of UBC clinical faculty who devote their lives to caring for others while training the next generation of doctors, researchers and health professionals\.  
  
Through their teaching and mentorship, they inspire the next wave of UBC graduates to become educators and healthcare providers — growing and strengthening the healthcare workforce while improving the health of millions of British Columbians\.  
  
That’s the incredible power and legacy of clinical teaching\.**
