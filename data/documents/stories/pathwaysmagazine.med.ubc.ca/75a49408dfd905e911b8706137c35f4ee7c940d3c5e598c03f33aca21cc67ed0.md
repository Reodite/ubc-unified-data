DR\. SHREYA MOODLEY   

VANCOUVER  

8:15 AM

It’s early, and already the sounds of shuffling feet and childish laughter fill the lobby of the BC Children’s Hospital Heart Centre\. Inside, UBC clinical professor of pediatric cardiology Dr\. Shreya Moodley and postdoctoral trainee Dr\. Carine van der Merwe examine their first patient of the day, seven\-year\-old Kita Kobayashi\.

Kita was born with transposition of the great arteries — a rare heart defect where the two main arteries are connected to the wrong chambers, preventing blood from getting oxygen\. She had corrective surgery at birth and has been under the care of Dr\. Moodley ever since\.

While Dr\. van der Merwe checks Kita’s pulse, Dr\. Moodley chats with Kita about her favourite colour, food, subject in school and what she likes to do for fun\. Kita’s answers come easy — green, chocolate, gym and gymnastics\.

[Image](https://med-fom-pathways-2025.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2026/01/Shreya-Moodley_Carine-VanderMerwe_Pediatrics_DayLife-3.png)

Heart appointments can be long and technical – especially for kids – often involving ultrasounds, electrocardiograms and physical exams\. For parents, they can be filled with anxiety as they wait to find out whether their child is ok\. That’s why Dr\. Moodley always makes a point of connecting with patients and their families as part of the examination – and training\.

“Trainees watch how we speak, how we listen and how we respond in moments of difficulty,” she says\. “The technical skills are important, but it’s also important to understand that the practice of medicine extends beyond diagnosis and treatment to the heart of the human experience\.”

During the final heart check, Dr\. Moodley says that Kita’s tests look good and that she’ll see her next year\. Dr\. van der Merwe congratulates Kita with a high\-five\. Before the doctors leave, Kita smiles and reaches for Dr\. Moodley’s stethoscope, who hands it to her and shows her how to use it\.

“Can you hear that?” says Dr\. Moodley, watching Kita’s expression as the little girl puts the stethoscope to her own chest\.

“Yeah,” says Kita, smiling\. It’s the first time she is hearing her own heartbeat\.

DR\. ARYN KHAN   

VANDERHOOF  

8:15 AM

It’s a bright, cold morning in Vanderhoof and Dr\. Aryn Khan is busy seeing patients at her family medicine clinic\. Joined by UBC medical students Justin Grenier and Jason Van Zyl, she moves from exam room to exam room — supporting a patient with an atypical pregnancy, removing a piece of metal from the injured arm of another, and treating a sports injury\.

At every stop, she encourages the students to get involved, honing their skills by taking patient histories, assisting with procedures, and making small talk\. Along the way, Dr\. Khan gives and receives plenty of hugs\.

“The thing about rural medicine is that you get to do everything and you get to know everyone,” she says\. “I honestly love what I do and couldn’t see myself doing anything else\.”

For the next six months, Justin and Jason will join Dr\. Khan in all aspects of her practice as part of the Northern Regional Integrated Clerkship \(NRIC\) pilot program — which also includes rotations in obstetrics, emergency medicine, palliative care, and more\.

The program, which officially launches this summer, offers third\-year UBC medical students in the Northern Medical Program the opportunity to split their annual training between a northern community like Vanderhoof and the University Hospital of Northern British Columbia in Prince George\.

“I hope that by teaching students through my eyes and passion, they fall in love with rural medicine like I have,” says Dr\. Khan\.

Jayme Peel  

Prince Rupert, Northern B\.C\.  

5:15 p\.m\.

In the vibrant port city of Prince Rupert, Occupational Therapist \(OT\) Jayme Peel and UBC OT student Kendall Ferguson are practicing taking specialized measurements for a mock wheelchair assessment at the Prince Rupert Interprofessional Student\-led Model Clinic \(PRISM\)\. Getting precise measurements is essential in preventing patients from falling or developing bed sores, circulatory problems or spinal misalignment that can lead to chronic pain and deformities\.

“Our role is to help people get back to living their lives as independently as possible,” says Peel\. “That can mean teaching an older adult how to dress and cook by themselves, recommending adaptive equipment after an injury or supporting someone experiencing mental health challenges\.”

The UBC clinic — which is one of several innovative student\-led models across B\.C\. — also encourages team\-based care with UBC OT and physical therapy \(PT\) students training and working together to provide optimal care for patients\. For example, while a PT may help a patient gain strength and mobility after a fall, the OT can ensure home safety through routine changes and home modifications\.

“Getting students into that interdisciplinary mindset early on is important,” explains Peel\. “Providing excellent care for someone can take a whole healthcare team working together towards a single goal — which is to improve the health and quality of life for the patient\.”

Student wellness is also a top priority for Peel, who trains approximately 14 students each year\.

“Incorporating work\-life balance is essential to avoiding burnout,” she explains\. “I always emphasize to students the importance of taking care of yourself so that you can provide the best care for others\.”

Dr\. Jared Baylis  

Kelowna, Interior B\.C\.  

7:45 p\.m\.

Nearly 1,400 kilometers away, in B\.C\.’s Interior, Dr\. Jared Baylis runs an emergency medicine simulation session for UBC medical students Benjamin Koenig, Julia Speirs and Nathan Loo at the Kelowna General Hospital \(KGH\)\.  
As the simulation mannequin goes into cardiac arrest, Dr\. Baylis guides the students through each critical step including monitoring vitals, chest compressions and intubation\.

“There can be a lot of high\-stakes situations in the ER where you have to work together as a team and make decisions quickly,” says Dr\. Baylis\. “I want medical students and residents to feel comfortable and ready when these situations arise — and simulation offers them a safe place to practice, perfect their skills and ask questions\.”  

Dr\. Baylis was one of the first residents to complete his training in UBC’s Emergency Medicine Residency Program in Kelowna when it launched in 2014\. The experience inspired his passion for teaching and innovation — and his decision to stay and join the Faculty after his studies\. “I had amazing mentors at UBC who inspired me to teach the next generation of emergency doctors\.”

The training lasts more than an hour and feels almost as intense as the real thing — which is the point\. By the end, the students are feeling the weight of the long hours but are energized by the new knowledge and skills they’ve gained\. Dr\. Baylis and the students chat and share and a few final reflections before saying their goodbyes and heading home for the night
