OPINION

The healthcare challenges we face today are the result of remarkable progress and point to an even healthier future, say **Dr\. Constance LeBlanc** and **Dr\. Anna Karwowska** of the Association of Faculties of Medicine of Canada

Canada’s healthcare system is at an inflection point\. Headlines often focus on strain: emergency department backlogs, staffing shortages, and the rising cost of care\. These pressures are very real\.

But underneath them lies a more hopeful and profoundly important truth\. The challenges we face today are in part the result of remarkable progress — of more people living longer, of rapid population growth, and of the extraordinary sophistication of modern diagnostics and treatments\. In other words, we are grappling with the growing pains of success\.

Consider longevity\. Canadians are living years — sometimes decades — longer than in previous generations\. Longevity should be celebrated: it is proof of medicine rooted in strong science and discovery, of good public health, and of effective vaccinations\. However, with this gift come complex, chronic conditions that demand sustained, team\-based care\. At the same time, Canada’s population is expanding and diversifying, pulling care closer to communities and highlighting the urgency of equitable access\.

> ## Our healthcare system is being asked to do more because it *can* do more\. The capability to detect disease earlier, individualize treatments and support people throughout longer lifespans is one of the great achievements of modern science\.

Layer onto this the proliferation of advanced imaging, genetic screening, precision therapeutics and AI\-assisted clinical tools, and the picture becomes clear: our system is being asked to do more because it can do more\. The capability to detect disease earlier, individualize treatments and support people throughout longer lifespans is one of the great achievements of modern science\.

The task before us now is to align our workforce, funding models and training programs to match that capability\. Healthcare, then, is not merely an industry: it is an ecosystem grounded in patient\-centred care, reconciliation, social accountability education, research and interprofessional collaboration\. By keeping the patient at the centre of all that we do, we ensure care is aligned with the needs of our evolving communities\.

Health care begins with learning: the rigorous scientific training that produces clinicians, scientists, and health leaders; the interprofessional practice that empowers teams; and the community partnerships that amplify local wisdom\.

It continues with meaningful work: careers that blend service and discovery, that invite curiosity and compassion in equal measure, and that offer a path for people to contribute to something larger than themselves\.

And it culminates in the most human of outcomes: a longer, better life\. When people are healthy, they can study, innovate, care for loved ones, build businesses and give back\. This is economic prosperity in its most resilient form — rooted in human potential\.

It is also joy: the simple joy of time well lived\.

Canada’s faculties of medicine are the engines of this ecosystem, alongside our training partners in hospitals, community clinics, Indigenous health organizations and public health agencies\. They are where we transform scientific breakthroughs into clinical practice, and where we cultivate the next generation of clinicians, educators, researchers and innovators\.

> ## When people are healthy, they can study, innovate, care for loved ones, build businesses and give back\. This is economic prosperity in its most resilient form – rooted in human potential\.

Together, these institutions demonstrate not only what health care is capable of today, but where it is going tomorrow — toward team\-based, data\-informed, community\-embedded care that meets people where they are and anticipates where they’re going\.

At the University of British Columbia \(UBC\) Faculty of Medicine, this vision is deeply integrated into its approach to training and community engagement\. For decades, UBC has been a leader in medical education, setting a high standard for academic excellence and innovation\. Its distributed medical education model remains one of its defining strengths, ensuring learners train in diverse communities across B\.C\. and gain a deep understanding of regional needs, cultural contexts, and the importance of local care delivery\.

UBC’s work also strengthens Canada’s broader health system by contributing innovations in education and research, by forging partnerships, and by supporting interprofessional education that reflects modern team\-based care\. By preparing graduates to practice in digitally connected, culturally safe and community\-oriented environments, UBC helps ensure that the province — and Canada as a whole — has a healthcare workforce ready for the future\. These efforts demonstrate what is possible when education, research and community service are aligned with purpose\.

The path forward will require sustained partnership\. Governments have a role in enabling workforce planning and funding models that reward team\-based, preventative and community\-oriented care\. Health authorities and community partners are essential in shaping local solutions\. Faculties of medicine must continue to lead with evidence, humility and a relentless focus on patient and learner experience\.

> ## Are we preparing more people to deliver excellent care? Are we building careers that sustain the people who sustain us? And are we helping more Canadians live longer, healthier, more joyful lives? The answer, strengthened by institutions like UBC, is yes\!

All of us — educators, clinicians, researchers, policymakers, patients and their families — must hold fast to the idea that our current challenges are solvable not despite our progress, but because of it\.

If health care is about promoting health and wellbeing through education, research and social accountability, then the measure of our success is straightforward: Are we preparing more people to deliver excellent care? Are we building careers that sustain the people who sustain us? And are we helping more Canadians live longer, healthier, more joyful lives?

The answer, strengthened by institutions like UBC, is yes — and we are just getting started\!

*Dr\. Constance LeBlanc is an emergency physician and president and CEO of the [Association of Faculties of Medicine of Canada \(AFMC\)](https://www.afmc.ca/)\. Dr\. Anna Karwowska is a pediatrician and AFMC Vice\-President, Education\.*
