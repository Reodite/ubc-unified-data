**The role of a plant host on evolutionary  and competitive dynamics of microbe\-microbe interactions** 

Plant hosts share close functional relationships with surface\-colonizing microbiota and influence the interactions among these resident microbes\. Understanding this tripartite host\-microbe\-microbe system can aid in engineering tailored plant\-microbial communities for agriculture and bioremediation\. We investigated how the plant host affects competitive interactions among root\-colonizing *Pseudomonas fluorescens* across evolutionary time and through the host immune system\. Specifically, we asked: 1\) how repeated passaging of an opportunistic root pathogen on plants, with or without a competitor, affects its virulence and competitiveness, and 2\) how the plant immune system differentially impacts competitive outcomes in wild\-type versus immunocompromised plants\.

In our passaging experiment, virulence in the opportunistic pathogen *Pseudomonas* sp\. N2C3 evolved similarly over time regardless of competitor presence\. However, populations evolving without a competitor exhibited a trade\-off between competitiveness and virulence, whereas these traits became unlinked under competition\. Trajectory analysis showed that while evolutionary paths did not differ in direction or shape between treatments, they significantly differed in overall trajectory orientation over time\.

We also examined how the host immune system alters microbial competitive outcomes by evaluating immunogenic capability, colonization ability across genotypes, and tolerance to reactive oxygen species on wild\-type and immunocompromised hosts\. We found that immunogenicity, rather than colonization ability or immune tolerance explains shifts in competitive outcomes\. When relative abundance differed between host types, the more immunogenic strain competed better in immunocompromised hosts\. This suggests that wild\-type immunity actively suppresses the growth or competitive ability of highly immunogenic strains, an effect that is relieved in immunocompromised hosts\.

Overall, our work demonstrates how the plant host shapes rhizosphere microbe\-microbe competitive interactions from both evolutionary and ecological perspectives\.
