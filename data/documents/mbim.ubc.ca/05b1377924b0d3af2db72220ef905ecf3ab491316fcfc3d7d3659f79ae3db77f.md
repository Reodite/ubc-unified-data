**By Sarah Anderson, PhD**

For Abigail Cubertson, the summer of 2026 was her third spent in a research lab at UBC\. 

“I enjoy learning about STEM,” said Culbertson, a member of the Métis Nation and a recent high school graduate\. “It’s great to experience different fields and try to find out what I’m passionate about\.” 

Through the [seed2STEM Summer Research Program for Indigenous Youth](https://icord.org/seed2stem/), Culbertson has been able to do just that\. The program invites Indigenous students in grades 9 through 12 to participate in a six\-week paid internship at labs at UBC’s Vancouver and Kelowna campuses as well as other nearby academic, clinical, and public health research institutions\. “Seed2STEM provides Indigenous students with an opportunity to experience STEM firsthand and develop a more tangible understanding of what a future career in research may look like while building the skills, confidence, and relationships that can support their pursuit of educational and career pathways,” said Dr\. Katlyn Richardson, the manager of the seed2STEM program\. 

By offering paid positions and additional supports including transit passes and loaner laptops, the seed2STEM team aims to reduce financial and logistical barriers to participation\. They even provide travel arrangements and homestays in order to welcome students who live too far to commute — Culbertson, for example, came all the way from Lethbridge, Alberta to join in\. 

Members of the host research team complete pre\-placement training and receive practical tools and tips for effective supervising, covering areas spanning inclusive onboarding, trauma\-informed approaches, and clear communication\. “One of our goals is to foster greater awareness within research teams of barriers faced by Indigenous peoples in pursuit of careers in STEM and build teams’ capacity to provide welcoming and inclusive research environments,” said Dr\. Richardson\. 

This summer, seed2STEM welcomed 112 students to its Metro Vancouver and Kelowna sites, representing Indigenous communities across Canada\. Three labs in the Department of Microbiology and Immunology hosted seed2STEM students, providing hands\-on research experience, mentorship, and a glimpse into life in a university lab\. 

[A map of Canada with home communities of Seed2STEM students labelled](https://mbim.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/2026%20seed2STEM%20communities.png)

A map shows the home communities of students who participated in the seed2STEM program from 2023 to 2026\.  \(Image courtesy of seed2STEM\)

Culbertson was placed in the lab of Dr\. [Maria Tokuyama](https://mbim.ubc.ca/people/maria-tokuyama), where she learned about endogenous retroviruses \(ERVs\) — remnants of ancient viral infections that became integrated into humans’ genetic code\. By growing cells, exposing them to an ERV, and extracting and sequencing their genetic material, she worked to identify which cellular receptors interact with specific ERVs\.  

This project contributes to the thesis work of Austin Guild, a graduate student in the Tokuyama Lab who studies how ERVs may be protective against herpes simplex virus\-2 infection\. “We tried to find a research area that we are working on right now where a new student could come in and make a meaningful contribution toward our ongoing success,” said Dr\. Tokuyama\. “We also thought about some hands\-on experimental techniques such as cell culture and RNA extraction that are foundational skills in the lab, but that someone who’s never touched a pipette before can master in a reasonable amount of time\.” 

[A scientist uses a pipette at a lab bench](https://mbim.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/IMG_9896%202.jpeg)

Abigail Culbertson uses a Nanodrop instrument to measure RNA concentration and purity\.

It’s valuable not only for the seed2STEM students to learn these skills, but for the trainees to teach them\. “Abigail is the fourth high school student that I’ve mentored, and it’s something that I really enjoy,” said Guild\. “I’ve had the opportunity to do a lot of intensive, one\-on\-one instruction, which is rare in other teaching positions in graduate school\.” 

It’s also quite rare for university labs to open their doors to high school students\. “This is one of the first opportunities I've seen to work with students earlier on in their educational time frame,” said Dr\. [Phillip Domeier](https://mbim.ubc.ca/people/phillip-domeier), whose lab also hosted a seed2STEM student this summer\. In this way, the program allows students to gauge their level of interest in science before embarking on their undergraduate studies, where they must quickly commit to and invest in a specific discipline\.

Dr\. Domeier hopes that his participation in the program will help paint a more realistic portrait of academic research\. “There this kind of ‘science fiction’ image that we project where everything in a laboratory is super high\-tech and everything works perfectly the first time and we never have to troubleshoot,” he said\. 

For those working with finicky biological samples, this image is often rapidly dismantled\. In Dr\. Domeier’s lab, incoming 11\[th\] grader Samantha Xu worked with graduate student Josepha Klas, using mice models to study how exposure to microplastics influences susceptibility to and symptoms of autoimmune diseases\. Conceiving a project for a high school student gave the group the chance to “zoom out,” focusing on a broader research question that has a very concrete, real\-world impact\. 

That’s one benefit of enhanced representation in the lab, Domeier said: “It expands our capacity to ask questions that are outside of our purview because we’re stuck in our own thought boxes\.” Furthermore, working with people from diverse scientific backgrounds challenges the members of the lab to convey complex concepts in an accessible way, preparing them to communicate their research findings more meaningfully to funding agencies, patients and patient advocates, and the general public\. 

Honing this skill, Jake Felker, a graduate student in the lab of Dr\. [Jim Sun](https://mbim.ubc.ca/people/jim-sun), prepared a slideshow presentation to provide a lay language explanation of the seed2STEM research project he oversaw\. Felker worked with incoming 12\[th\] grader Lillie to study the role of a key protein called MX2 in the activation of macrophage immune cells and how this process affects the outcome of infections such as tuberculosis\. 

[A scientist works in a cell culture hood\.](https://mbim.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/IMG_9986.jpeg)

Lillie works in a biosafety cabinet\. “I did cell culture by myself today,” she said\.

This project involves several experimental techniques that are highly visual in nature, including microscopy and flow cytometry, a method that uses fluorescent dyes to sort cells based on physical or chemical characteristics\. “While we hope to impart some understanding of the research, it’s not so much about the hard science, but rather what would be fun for the student to do and see,” said Dr\. Sun\. 

[A man lectures at the front of a classroom](https://mbim.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/IMG_0019.jpeg)

*Dr\. Andrew Santos, who leads Indigenous student engagement and support in the Department of Microbiology and Immunology, describes how bacteria release chemical signals to communicate\.*

Facts, fun, and everything in between: By the end of the summer, the seed2STEM students had experienced the full spectrum of academic research\. They attended lab meetings, analyzed data, and even presented their preliminary findings at a poster session similar to those held at scientific conferences\. Through the poster session, a lunch mixer, and a microbiology crash course led by lecturer Dr\. [Andrew Santos](https://mbim.ubc.ca/people/andrew-santos), the students were also able to connect with other members of the department and share their experiences in the program with one another\. 

[A student stands next to a scientific poster](https://mbim.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/inline-images/IMG_0429_3.jpeg)

*Samantha Xu presents her research findings at the poster session\. “Instead of speaking in front of a large group, people approached me individually to ask questions, which made me much less nervous,” she said\.*

  
  
  
  
  
As the students’ time in the lab comes to an end, Dr\. Sun hopes that the seeds planted throughout the program will blossom in the future\. “My hope is that, through this early exposure, we have nurtured these students’ interest in and excitement about scientific research and that that they consider STEM as a possible career,” he said\. Surveys of past participants reveal that from a cohort of 81 high school graduates, 80 percent entered post\-secondary education; of that group, 88 percent studied STEM fields\. 

Culbertson, for one, hopes to pursue post\-secondary science education in the future\. “Due to my absolutely wonderful experiences in the seed2STEM program, I will be doing it at UBC,” she said\. “I'll make sure that I make it here and nowhere else\.”
