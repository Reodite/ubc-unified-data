**Team:** Tahia Devisscher, PhD; Mercy Babatunji, MSc

**Timeline:** January 2025 – December 2026

**Partners:** Association of Forest and Nature Therapy, Nature and Forest Therapy of Canada

[Image](https://riselab.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2025/03/IMG_1225-683x1024.jpg)

Forest bathing, known as ‘Shinrin\-Yoku’ in Japan, is the practice of immersing oneself in a forest and mindfully engaging all senses\. This practice was introduced in the 1980s in Japan to promote public health and alleviate increasing levels of stress associated to a period of rapid modernization and lifestyle change\. Since then, a growing number of studies have been assessing the health and well\-being effects associated to forest bathing worldwide\. In western countries such as Canada, forest bathing and guided ‘forest therapy’ is increasingly recognized as a preventive, low\-cost intervention to reduce stress, and support mental health\.

While forest bathing is gaining momentum in western countries as a new nature\-based care practice that is intentionally crafted to improve well\-being, the capacity of forest bathing to strengthen human\-nature relationships and support sense of personal fulfillment and resilience has not been carefully examined yet\. Understanding how this practice can serve to strengthen human\-nature relationships is important because the increasing disconnect between humans and nature, particularly in the context of rapid urbanization, has been identified as a root cause of the environmental crises we are facing this century\. In fact, increasing connection to nature has been proposed as a *leverage point* for sustainability where strengthening human\-nature relationships in a complex system can have wide\-ranging influences\. The goal of this project is to evaluate the potential for guided forest therapy as an effective experience that can support and enrich the connection between humans and nature in the urban context\.
